## VII. Regulatory Impact Analysis

### A. Statement of Need

ONC continually evaluates the voluntary Certification Program (42 U.S.C. 300jj– 11(c)(5)) to improve the interoperability of health IT, while also assessing ways to reduce administrative costs and burden. This final rule is a result of our ongoing evaluation of the Certification Program and aligns with EO 14192 on Unleashing Prosperity Through Deregulation. EO 14192 was issued January 31, 2025, and sets the Administration’s policy to “significantly reduce the private expenditures required to comply with Federal regulations to secure America's economic prosperity and national security and the highest possible quality of life for each citizen.”58 This final rule is an EO 14192 deregulatory action and removes and revises several regulations and policies to unleash prosperity. We believe the changes will reduce

burden on developers of certified health IT, returning time and effort to them, and in turn, enhancing competition, entrepreneurship, and innovation. These deregulatory actions will reduce barriers to entry for novel technologies and digital health solutions, give time back to developers of certified health IT to focus on the needs and requests of their customers, and, overall, advance progress toward nationwide interoperability and universal patient access to their electronic health information.

### B. Overall Impact

We have examined the impacts of this rule as required by EO 12866, “Regulatory Planning and Review”; EO 13132, “Federalism”; EO 13563, “Improving Regulation and Regulatory Review”; EO 14192, “Unleashing Prosperity Through Deregulation”; section 202 of the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995 (2 U.S.C. 1532); and the Regulatory Flexibility Act (RFA) (Pub. #### 1. Regulatory Planning and Review Analysis

This rule is consistent with the policies outlined in EOs 12866, 13563, and 14192. EO 12866 and 13563 direct agencies to assess all costs and benefits of available regulatory alternatives and, if regulation is necessary, to select regulatory approaches that maximize net benefits (including potential economic, environmental, public health and safety effects, and distributive impacts). EO 14192, as noted above, focuses on deregulatory actions and cost savings associated with deregulatory actions. This rulemaking is significant under Section 3(f)(1) of EO 12866, and we have prepared an RIA.

##### a. Costs and Benefits

These finalized deregulatory actions provide substantial relief for developers of certified health IT and significant cost savings realized from the revision and removal of certain program requirements. For the purpose of estimating the cost savings of this rulemaking, we have determined that these policies would continue absent this regulatory action. The cost savings have been estimated using the best available data and modeled on the costs estimated and

finalized in prior ONC rulemakings. As articulated in the preamble, these deregulatory actions reduce the effort of developers of certified health IT to meet ongoing program requirements and reduce barriers to entry for new program participants. The actions, importantly, return effort back to developers of certified health IT to innovate their technology and focus on the needs of their clients and end users.

Comments. Multiple commenters argue that the RIA underestimates or overlooks significant downstream costs that may shift to providers, health information exchanges, networks, and patients when certification criteria are removed. They warn that eliminating requirements—particularly in privacy, security, and interoperability—could increase variability, reduce auditability, and force providers to independently validate or maintain capabilities previously ensured through certification. Safety net and resource—constrained organizations expressed concern that weakened Federal baselines may expose them to security risks, added vendor fees, contractual burdens, and additional operational responsibilities they are not equipped to absorb. Several commenters emphasized that these unintended consequences may widen inequities, especially for small clinics and rural providers, unless ONC fully evaluates downstream impacts and implements post-final monitoring to mitigate burden shifting. Collectively, commenters urged ONC to account for patient harms, civil rights risks, and the ongoing need for reliable, standards-based functionality within its cost-benefit analysis.

Response. This rulemaking and impact analysis examines the continued value of maintaining program requirements weighed against the ongoing cost of that compliance. Our original analysis and public comments helped shape final decisions and economic analyses presented in this finalized impact analysis. The finalized revisions to the Certification Program carefully considered public comments, and, in particular, we have decided not to finalize proposals to remove criteria that enable secure and confidential access and use of patient health information and certain information exchange capabilities. Finalized revisions to the Certification Program largely encompass reductions in the frequency and scope of data reporting

and documentation that developers are required to provide to comply with certification, specifically – narrowing requirements for the Real World Testing and Insights Conditions of Certification and removing annual reporting of source attributes for the “decision support interventions” criterion. We proposed to remove 34 criteria and have, instead, finalized the removal of 14 criteria, with some criteria, previously proposed to be removed, revised instead with a narrower scope, retaining essential functionality and standard references to ensure a necessary floor of technical capability are available to all certified health IT users. The decreased regulatory burdens and estimated cost savings presented in this impact analysis relate to the compliance costs associated with testing and certification and do not fully encompass the total costs of implementing the technology by end-users.

We considered the effects of revisions to the Certification Program on end-users as part of our calculus; however, we have limited data on how revisions to program requirements may affect the availability or functionality of certified health IT to these end-users. It is difficult to estimate the wide variation in how certified health IT is ultimately deployed and implemented by end-users, as well as evaluate how those choices are tied to Certification Program requirements or the requirements and specifications of other programs that adopt certified health IT. For example, we finalized removal of the “direct project” criterion in § 170.315(h)(1) but have retained reference to the Direct Messaging standard in § 170.202(a)(2). Survey data from 2025 show that a majority of hospitals often use the Direct Messaging standard to send and receive information with outside organizations.59 However, other electronic exchange methods complement or substitute for this messaging protocol, like those used for network-based exchange via State, national, and qualified health information networks, and use of those methods by hospitals now exceed use of Direct Messaging. This change occurred despite the broad certification of Health IT Modules to the criterion and ubiquitous adoption of certified

Comments: Commenters argued that the projected cost savings in the proposed rulemaking are significantly overstated because most certifications involve routine maintenance rather than new development. They note that many features are already implemented, so large claimed savings—such as millions of hours or over a billion dollars—lack evidentiary support. Overall, they believe the RIA misrepresents the true scale of savings by not accounting for the limited effort required to maintain existing capabilities.

Response: We thank commenters for their input. We agree with the commenters’ assessment that most certifications are recertifications that require annual maintenance costs rather than new development or integration costs. This impact analysis took a variety of approaches to estimate cost savings, based on the type of deregulatory action. In all cases, our approach to estimating cost savings calculated maintenance costs as a fraction of new development costs finalized in prior ONC rulemakings to estimate cost savings for the revision or removal of a program requirement. For example, in Table R-1: Panel A “Estimates of Annual Labor Hours Saved for the Removal of Certification Criteria,” average maintenance costs across

all removals was 15% with a maximum maintenance cost of 27% of new development costs. As described in our methodology for removed criteria, we leveraged data and information collected from the developer community, as part of the Department’s request for public comment on deregulatory actions. The public feedback and discussions with developers and other interested parties provided additional context on the ongoing costs of meeting certain Certification Program requirements, in addition to the overall value of this compliance on end-users and other entities. This data, and information informed methodologies discussed in detail in the following analyses, provide concrete evidentiary support for estimated cost savings from these deregulatory actions. It is worth noting that the majority of estimated cost savings are derived from the revision of three Certification Program requirements: safety-enhanced design, decision support interventions, and real world testing. All three requirements involve regular, typically annual effort for developers to test updated or new modules, provide data and documentation on predictive super intelligence models, and plan, measure, and report on metrics for dozens of certification criteria. These cost savings are also in aggregate across all developers of certified health IT. As of April 1, 2026, there are over 450 developers who participated in the Certification Program. Cost savings are calculated for individual criteria and reporting requirements and reflect the current number of developers and products that adopt the criteria or must comply with a reporting requirement. Therefore, more cost savings may flow to certain developers who currently have Health IT Modules certified to more criteria that will be removed from the Certification Program or who are subject to more extensive reporting requirements that this rule revises. On average, we project that a developer of certified health IT may realize $300,000 to $400,000 in undiscounted savings annually or roughly the equivalent of two data engineer FTEs. Where applicable, we describe estimation methodologies in more detail for individual policy changes.

#### 2. Revisions

2.1 Revision of Certification Criteria.

We have finalized the revision of 5 certification criteria, 2 of which were proposed for removal. For each revised certification criterion, we have estimated the annual savings generated by the finalized revisions. 2.1.1 § 170.315(b)(1) Transitions of Care We have finalized revisions to the certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(1) to reduce burden on developers of health IT by removing the “send and receive via edge protocol” C-CDA requirement and “create” C-CDA requirement in the criterion and by simplifying the remaining criterion requirements to focus solely on validating receipt of C-CDA documents. We originally

proposed to revise the certification criterion with an effective date of January 1, 2027. We have finalized the criterion revisions to be effective as of January 1, 2030. We believe that these revisions remove a substantial proportion of the ongoing effort required to maintain and update the functional requirement of the certification criterion, but we acknowledge that it does not remove requirements to support the C-CDA standard or to update functionality to receive CCDA documents. ### C. Regulatory Flexibility Act

The Regulatory Flexibility Act (RFA) requires agencies to analyze options for regulatory relief of small businesses if a rule has a significant impact on a substantial number of small entities. The Small Business Administration (SBA) establishes the size of small businesses for Federal Government programs based on average annual receipts or the average employment of a firm.89 The entities that are likely to be directly affected by the requirements in this finalized rule are health IT developers.

While health IT developers that pursue certification of their health IT under the Certification Program represent a small segment of the overall information technology industry, we believe that many health IT developers impacted by the requirements in this final rule most likely fall under the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) code 513210 “Software Publishers.”

OMB advised that the Federal statistical establishment data published for reference years beginning on or after January 1, 2022, should be published using the 2022 NAICS United States codes.90 The SBA size standard associated with this NAICS code is set at $47 million annual receipts or less. ### D. Executive Order 13132—Federalism

EO 13132 establishes certain requirements that an agency must meet when it promulgates a finalized rule that imposes substantial direct requirement costs on State and local governments, preempts State law, or otherwise has Federalism implications. Nothing in this finalized rule imposes substantial direct compliance costs on State and local governments, preempts State law,

or otherwise has Federalism implications. We are not aware of any State laws or regulations that are contradicted or impeded by any of the policies in this finalized rule. We welcomed comments on this assessment and received no response.

### E. Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995

Section 202 of the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995 requires that agencies assess anticipated costs and benefits before issuing any rule that imposes unfunded mandates on State, local, and Tribal governments or the private sector requiring spending in any one year of $100 million in 1995 dollars, updated annually for inflation. The current inflation-adjusted statutory threshold is approximately $187 million in 2025. This finalized rule is deregulatory, and, therefore, the estimated potential cost effects of this finalized rule do not reach the statutory threshold. This finalized rule does not impose unfunded mandates on State, local, and Tribal governments, or the private sector.

Comments. Multiple commenters expressed concern that our proposals impose unfunded mandates on State, local, and tribal governments and the private sector. Commenters were concerned that de-regulation shifts the burden of assessing health IT functionality and standards adoption to end users. Two commenters expressed their concerns specifically with the downstream effects of our public health-related proposals (§ 170.315(f)(4), § 170.315(f)(5), § 170.315(f)(6) and § 170.315(f)(7)) which sought to remove the standards-based requirements for electronic case reporting and antimicrobial use and resistance reporting and remove the requirements to support cancer registry reporting and creation of health care survey information.

Response. We thank commenters for their input. The changes in § 170.315(f)(4), § 170.315(f)(5), and § 170.315(f)(7) have not been finalized as proposed—we are maintaining requirements to support cancer registry reporting and creation of health care survey information and keeping electronic case reporting as a standards-based criterion. We have finalized as proposed the removal of standards-based requirements in § 170.315(f)(6), which have not kept pace with reporting requirements and therefore we believe this change will not impose

downstream costs on end-users to submit AUR data NHSN. Given these final decisions, we do not anticipate any significant downstream costs or burdens to be shifted on public health entities. In response to broader concerns that our proposals impose unfunded mandates or that deregulation shifts the burden of assessing health IT and standards adoption to end users, we have chosen to finalize 14 of 34 criteria proposed for removal from the CFR, with many of those removals consisting of criteria with low adoption rates overall. Furthermore, the reduced scope of conditions and maintenance of certification reflect the prioritization of reporting and documentation requirements to focus developer efforts on API criteria and associated requirements, as these are less mature and evolving requirements which necessitate focused developer efforts to monitor and evaluate these functionalities in real world settings. These are necessary program improvements that will enhance the overall performance of the Certification Program and allow it to evolve to meet emerging needs that will benefit the nation as a whole.
