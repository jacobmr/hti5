## III. Health Information Technology Standards, Implementation Specifications, and

Certification Criteria and Certification Programs for Health Information Technology (Part 170)

Reading This Final Rule In this preamble, we present our finalizations in an order that begins with the certification criteria we finalized for removal and subsequently the standards, definitions, and other related Certification Program provisions finalized for removal or revision. This approach should make it easier for readers to follow our finalized Certification Program removals, in part, due to the natural cascading effects of finalizing to remove or revise a certification criterion. Additionally, we finalized the removal of standards when they are no longer referenced by existing certification criteria. In very limited circumstances, we retained a standard that is cross-referenced in a certification criterion that we finalized for removal. We did this because the standard is cross-referenced in another criterion and/or supports health IT interoperability alignment. Further, as noted above, we finalized the removal of certain Conditions and Maintenance of Certification requirements and other Certification Program requirements. As stated above, for the purposes of this final rule, we have reviewed and responded to comments on a narrowed set of proposals from the Proposed Rule. Comments received in response to other Proposed Rule proposals remain under review and consideration. The Proposed Rule proposals not discussed in this final rule may, in the future, be included in subsequent final rules or as revised proposals in subsequent proposed rules.

RFI Feedback

Recently, the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), the Department, and CMSONC issued requests for information (RFIs) related to deregulation. We reviewed comments related to ONC. In response to the CMS-ONC Request for Information; Health Technology Ecosystem (90 FR 21034) (CMS-ONC RFI), commenters expressed a strong desire to modernize the Certification Program to be more modular, API-focused, and less centered on specific EHR functionalities. Commenters recommended simplifying the process, reducing costs, and expanding certification requirements to ensure payers and other health IT systems adhere to the same interoperability standards. There was an overwhelming demand to strengthen, expand, and mandate the use of FHIR-based APIs. Commenters strongly advocated for retiring outdated, less efficient standards to reduce complexity. A central theme from commenters was the need for regulatory modernization to reduce administrative and financial burdens on providers and technology developers. Commenters also expressed concern that existing certification requirements are often too rigid, costly, and not aligned with emerging care models like valuebased care or specialized technologies such as SI and remote monitoring. A commenter that responded to the HHS RFI expressed concerns with the Real World Testing and Insights Conditions of Certification, stating that the requirements are overly burdensome and offer limited value to providers or patients. The commenter also recommended retiring low-value certification criteria requirements that add complexity without improving care. A commenter that responded to the OMB RFI suggested that revisions could be made to the Conditions and Maintenance of Certification requirements that focus on streamlined and meaningful oversight without placing undue demands on developers, especially where those demands offer limited direct benefit to health care provider customers or their patients. The commenter also suggested the removal of “non-interoperability-focused, redundant, or valueless” criteria and a focus on promoting standardized and scalable interoperability. ONC addresses a number of these comments below, specifically the suggestions to remove non-interoperability-focused and nonessential certification criteria. Commenters were strongly supportive of our focus on FHIR-based

standards. Some commenters, however, included suggestions related to new regulatory actions. As noted in the Proposed Rule, these suggestions are out of scope for this deregulatory rule, but we may consider the suggestions for a future rulemaking (90 FR 60979).

### A. Certification Criteria for Health Information Technology

In this final rule we have identified certification criteria for removal or revision. For some of the certification criteria below, we justify removing the certification criteria because they have been “widely adopted” or “widely implemented.” To determine if the certification criterion is “widely adopted” or “widely implemented,” we considered whether and for how long the certification criterion has been included in the Base EHR definition in § 170.102, or the Certified Electronic Health Record Technology (CEHRT) definitions established by CMS in 42 CFR 495.4 and 414.1305. The CEHRT definitions incorporate the Base EHR definition and may also incorporate certification criteria necessary to report applicable objectives and measures under the Medicare Promoting Interoperability Program or the Promoting Interoperability performance category in the Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS), certification criteria determined applicable for an Alternative Payment Model (APM), and other certification criteria as specified. To participate in the Promoting Interoperability Program and the MIPS Promoting Interoperability performance category, eligible hospitals, CAHs, and eligible clinicians must use CEHRT as specified by the applicable definition. In addition, CEHRT is required for the purposes of determinations under 42 CFR 414.1415 and 414.1420 regarding Advanced APM status. ONC tracks and publishes data on the adoption and use of health IT, including CEHRT, by US hospitals and office-based physicians. Our most recent survey data from 2024 shows that 99 percent of hospitals and 91 percent of physicians reported having a certified EHR.13 As discussed in more detail below, we provide this analysis and consider certain certification criteria as “widely adopted” or “widely implemented” because they are included in the Base EHR or

#### 1. Clinical Certification Criteria

##### a. Clinical Decision Support

As of January 1, 2025, the “clinical decision support” (CDS) certification criterion in § 170.315(a)(9) is no longer available in the Certification Program because it expired on January 1, 2025 (89 FR 1237). Therefore, in the Proposed Rule, we proposed to remove the CDS certification criterion and reserve § 170.315(a)(9) with an effective date of a final rule (90 FR 60982).

Comments. Commenters who supported the proposal to remove the CDS certification criterion shared that it would clean up outdated requirements without affecting current certification pathways and promote innovation given that the certification criterion has expired or been superseded by decision support interventions.

Response. We thank the commenters for their support on this proposal. We agree that removing § 170.315(a)(9) from the CFR will clean up outdated requirements without affecting current certification pathways and will promote innovation.

Comments. Of the commenters who were not supportive of this proposal, some expressed concerns that it would weaken patient safety safeguards, reduce transparency and accountability, and shift oversight and implementation burdens to providers and healthcare organizations. Another commenter urged ONC to delay removal until 2028 to achieve cross-agency, harmonized standards. Some commenters conveyed that our proposal for removing the certification criterion would require additional guidance or specification of how transparency requirements would be preserved if § 170.315(a)(9) is removed or if source attribute requirements are removed from § 170.315(b)(11).

Response. We thank the commenters for their feedback on this proposal. In response to comments that appeared to connect our proposal in § 170.315(a)(9) with our proposals in § 170.315(b)(11), we remind readers that in the HTI-1 Final Rule we originally expired the CDS certification criterion effective January 1, 2025 (89 FR 1430). At the time, we declined commenters’ request to extend the expiration date noting that it will not move the Certification Program forward. CDS is an expired certification criterion that is no longer available in the Certification Program and health IT developers have been unable to certify their health IT products to CDS since January 1, 2025. Therefore, removing § 170.315(a)(9) from the CFR will not result in any further substantive effects, and delaying its removal is unnecessary. We appreciate commenters’ requests for guidance. However, we do not think guidance is necessary for the removal of an expired certification criterion that is no longer a requirement for certification.

Comments. Some commenters requested that, despite its expiration, we retain (i.e., not remove) the certification criterion in § 170.315(a)(9), reinstate the certification criterion, or use

the requirements in the CDS certification criterion in § 170.315(a)(9) instead of the narrower DSI requirements in § 170.315(b)(11) for evidence-based CDS in order to preserve transparency.

Response. We thank the commenters for their suggestions. As noted above, we did not propose in the HTI-5 Proposed Rule to reverse our prior determination to remove the CDS certification criterion and/or reinstate the CDS certification criterion. Further, in the HTI-1 Final Rule, we stated that it is suitable to implement the CDS certification criterion in § 170.315(a)(9) in a revised version in § 170.315(b)(11) due to the structure of the certification criterion, and that this approach is more straight-forward than having substantially similar yet separate certification criteria (89 FR 1237). Similarly, we issued the Proposed Rule to reduce burden by eliminating redundant requirements and to promote innovation (90 FR 60971). Consistent with this approach, adopting a separate CDS certification criterion in § 170.315(a)(9) with requirements similar to those in § 170.315(b)(11)(iii), which we have revised but not removed from the Certification Program (see section III.A.2.e), would undermine these goals and would not serve the interests of the Certification Program or users of certified health IT. Rather, it would recreate two substantially similar but separate certification criteria, adding complexity and burden to the Certification Program. Accordingly, we have finalized the removal of § 170.315(a)(9) CDS certification criterion from the CFR and reserved that section as proposed as of the effective date of this final rule.

In section III.A.2.e of this final rule, we note that we have finalized removal of the requirements in § 170.315(b)(11)(iv). As a result, some elements that were similar between § 170.315(a)(9) and § 170.315(b)(11)(iv) have been removed from the Certification Program. As noted in that section of the preamble of this final rule, we will take under consideration for future rulemaking whether such elements of “source attributes transparency” should be restored to the DSI certification criterion.

##### b. Family Health History

In the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60982 through 60983), we proposed to remove the “family health history” certification criterion and reserve § 170.315(a)(12). We explained that Health IT Modules certified to § 170.315(a)(12) must enable a user to record, change, and access a patient's family health history in accordance with the familial concepts or expressions included in, at a minimum, the version of the standard in § 170.207(a)(1) (SNOMED CT®, U.S. Edition, March 2022 Release). We noted that this certification criterion is not included in the Base EHR definition in § 170.102 but is included in the CEHRT definitions established by CMS for the Medicare Promoting Interoperability Program (42 CFR 495.4) and the MIPS Promoting Interoperability performance category (42 CFR 414.1305). Therefore, we proposed a January 1, 2027, effective date for its removal to allow sufficient time for CMS to update the CEHRT definitions.

Comments. Some commenters expressed support for the proposal to remove the family health history certification criterion. These commenters agreed that the family health history functionality is already well established in certified health IT and that it is likely to remain available through existing product capabilities or evolving data standards. Some commenters opined that exchange data standards can replace “functional” certification requirements, and retaining the family health history is duplicative of the requirements in USCDI v6. Another commenter expressed that, with appropriate USCDI and interoperability-based certification policies, removing the standalone family health history certification criterion would not affect the quality or integrity of the Certification Program. Another commenter noted that retaining the certification criterion is unnecessary because it is not cited in the Qualified EHR definition.

Response. We thank the commenters for their support of the proposal. We appreciate the commenters feedback on the removal of this certification criterion from the Certification Program. We have finalized our proposal to remove the family health history certification criterion from the CFR and we have reserved § 170.315(a)(12), with an effective date of January 1, 2027. We address other comments received on this proposal below.

Comments. The majority of comments received on our proposal to remove the family health history certification criterion generally did not support the proposal. Many commenters, including several community health centers, expressed significant concerns that it would reduce structured, standardized capture of family health history and thereby impair interoperability, clinical decision support, preventive care, and population health activities. Some commenters also raised concerns that the proposal is premature, would increase manual burden, lead to inconsistent implementations, and reduce data quality. Commenters also asserted that removal could shift burden to health care providers.

Response. We thank commenters for their feedback. The retention of the family health history certification criterion in the Certification Program would not support our stated goal of advancing interoperability because it is a non-FHIR-based certification criteria (90 FR 60972 through 60973) and only focuses on internal capabilities to record, change, and access family health history according to the SNOMED CT® U.S. Edition standard. However, we note that the removal of the family health history certification criterion does not impact existing functionality within implemented systems, and does not remove the Family Health History data element from use and reference within standards adopted for the Certification Program. Instead, the removal of the family health history certification criterion is consistent with our goal to transition to FHIRbased exchange, as demonstrated by the inclusion of Family Health History data elements in USCDI v614 and the HL7® FHIR® US Core Implementation Guide (IG) 9.0.0 – CI Build.15 Representing these data in FHIR as part of the US Core IG is a more effective way to ensure these data are widely available not only for access, but also for exchange and use to improve patient care. This approach not only maintains but enhances the ability to leverage family health history for clinical decision support, preventive care, and population health activities by promoting standardized, interoperable data exchange.

##### c. Implantable Device List

In the Proposed Rule, we proposed to remove and reserve the “implantable device list” (IDL) certification criterion in § 170.315(a)(14) (90 FR 60983). The IDL certification criterion requires Health IT Modules certified to § 170.315(a)(14) to exchange, record, and allow a user to access a list of Unique Device Identifiers (UDIs) associated with a patient's implantable devices. There is no adopted standard or implementation guide associated with this functionality. The IDL certification criterion is included in the Base EHR definition, and we proposed a conforming revision in § 170.102 to remove § 170.315(a)(14) from the Base EHR definition (please see section III.C.1 of the Proposed Rule). We proposed to make these changes effective as of the effective date of the final rule (90 FR 60984).

Comments. Of the commenters that specifically commented on this criterion, commenters were generally supportive of removal of the certification criterion, noting that the functionality is well-established, included in USCDI, and that removal would streamline the Certification Program without sacrificing interoperability.

Response. We thank the commenters for expressing their support to remove the IDL certification criterion. We have not, however, finalized our proposal to remove the IDL certification criterion (§ 170.315(a)(14)) nor any related proposals such as removing the IDL certification criterion from the Base EHR definition.

Comments. Commenters who were not supportive of removing the IDL certification criterion expressed concerns that removal of the criterion would undermine interoperability, create inconsistency in implementation, and jeopardize patient safety.

Response. We thank the commenters for expressing their concerns, but presume these concerns are moot since we are not removing the IDL certification criterion. Therefore, we are not specifically addressing these concerns.

After publication of the Proposed Rule, CMS proposed, in the FY 2027 IPPS/LTCH PPS proposed rule, to adopt the “Unique Device Identifiers (UDIs) for Implantable Medical Devices” measure within the Public Health and Clinical Data Exchange objective for eligible hospitals and

CAHs participating in the Medicare Promoting Interoperability Program. CMS stated that this measure would foster consistent workflows for capturing device data as discrete EHR elements and strengthen the ability of eligible hospitals, CAHs, beneficiaries, and public health agencies to use interoperable health information to improve outcomes, manage risk, and respond rapidly to device-related safety concerns. (91 FR 19629 through 19631). CMS proposed that an eligible hospital or critical access hospital (CAH) must use CEHRT to electronically capture and store, as one or more discrete data elements within the patient's electronic health record, the complete UDI, which includes the device identifier and, when present on the device label, the production identifier, for each implantable medical device subject to UDI requirements used for patient care delivery. CMS proposed to require eligible hospitals and CAHs to attest to this measure beginning with the EHR reporting period in CY 2027.

In its proposed rule, CMS noted that there are numerous health IT certification criteria that reference UDI, including the IDL certification criterion in § 170.315(a)(14) (91 FR 19631). CMS stated this certification criterion would be required to support the measure. CMS also highlighted our proposal to remove the IDL certification criterion in the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60983) and stated that if ONC finalizes removal of the IDL certification criterion then CMS would not reference the IDL certification criterion as required to support the measure. CMS explained that, separate from the dedicated IDL certification criterion, the UDI is a named data element within the USCDI v3 as the “Unique Device Identifier(s) for a patient's implantable device(s).” CMS noted that UDI is therefore a supported element within the HL7® FHIR® US Core Implementation Guide STU 6.1.0 FHIR IG and Health IT Modules certified to the “standardized API for patient and population services” certification criterion (§ 170.315(g)(10)) must be capable of providing any UDI information available in response to requests for patient data. CMS also identified the standardized API for patient and population services criterion as required to support fulfillment of the measure, which is also part of the Base EHR definition in § 170.102 and thus already incorporated into the CEHRT definition at 42 CFR 495.4. CMS

welcomed comments as to whether other certification criteria should be considered to support this measure.

In the 2027 IPPS/LTCH PPS final rule, CMS adopted the Unique Device Identifiers for Implantable Medical Devices measure within the Public Health and Clinical Data Exchange objective as proposed (91 FR 50055). Many commenters supported CMS’s proposal to adopt the measure, noting that recording UDI in EHRs would improve post-market surveillance and care coordination, reduce patient safety risks, and enable faster, more accurate public health responses (91 FR 50057). However, another commenter expressed concern with ONC's proposal to remove the IDL certification criterion, stating that removal could impact performance on the Unique Device Identifiers for Implantable Medical Devices measure (91 FR 50060).

After consideration of the public comments CMS received, CMS finalized its proposal to adopt the Unique Device Identifiers for Implantable Medical Devices measure beginning with the EHR reporting period in CY 2027 (91 FR 50060). In response to comments regarding potential removal of the IDL certification criterion, CMS stated its belief that health IT functionality related to UDI access, storage, and exchange would continue to be maintained because other certification criteria under the Certification Program continue to reference or support exchange of UDI for implantable devices, including the “standardized API for patient and population services” certification criterion (91 FR 50060). CMS also noted that removal of the specific IDL certification criterion would not preclude health IT developers from continuing to support Access GUDID lookup, device attribute display, validation, or related functionality. CMS further stated that it would continue to coordinate with ONC and monitor implementation to ensure that hospitals and CAHs have appropriate certified health IT capabilities to support UDI capture and exchange (91 FR 50060).

ONC and CMS are committed to ensuring ongoing support for UDI-related functions. In order to more fully support the new Unique Device Identifiers (UDIs) for Implantable Medical Devices measure within the Public Health and Clinical Data Exchange objective in the Medicare

Promoting Interoperability Program, we have not finalized our proposal to remove the IDL certification criterion from the Certification Program. In addition, as noted above, we have not finalized our proposal to remove § 170.315(a)(14) from the Base EHR definition (please see section III.C.1 of this final rule). Therefore, health IT developers will need to continue to certify their health IT to the IDL certification criterion and maintain certification to the IDL certification criterion to meet the Base EHR definition. In addition, eligible hospitals and CAHs attesting to the new Unique Device Identifiers (UDIs) for Implantable Medical Devices measure will continue to have the IDL functionality because, as noted above, the IDL certification criterion is included in the Base EHR definition and thus already incorporated into the CEHRT definition.

Comments. Some commenters noted that finalization of our proposal would require additional guidance or specification of how certified health IT should continue to support functionality such as standardized parsing of UDIs and retrieval of device attributes from GUDID (including SNOMED CT®-encoded information).

Response. Having not finalized our proposal, the IDL certification criterion remains part of the Certification Program and all relevant guidance for compliance with the criterion is still applicable such as the IDL Certification Companion Guide.19

#### 2. Care Coordination Certification Criteria

##### a. Transitions of Care

We proposed to revise the “transitions of care” certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(1) to simplify its requirements, with an effective date of January 1, 2027. We proposed to reduce the scope of the criterion to focus on enabling the receipt of a Consolidated CDA (C-CDA) document as a way to position this criterion for a future evolution to receipt of FHIR-formatted data. ##### e. Decision Support Interventions

We proposed to revise the “decision support interventions” (DSI) certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(11) in order to reduce burden and promote innovation of SI in healthcare (90 FR 60986 through 60988). The DSI certification criterion ensures that users can leverage health IT for clinical decision-making by supporting their selection of both evidence-based and predictive DSIs (PDSIs) and enabling users’ access to transparent information on DSI performance and quality. In particular, the source attribute requirements in § 170.315(b)(11) support “model cards” for PDSIs, which essentially function as nutrition labels and provide important information for providers using a PDSI, such as how a PDSI’s algorithm was trained, its intended use and limitations, and how it performs across different patient groups. However, as discussed in more detail below, we did not find that users of certified health IT have relied on these requirements to review such information or that the requirements have positively impacted patient care (90 FR 60987). We have finalized removal of the requirements related to “model cards” as well as other revisions for the reasons discussed in detail below.

We proposed to remove the requirement in § 170.315(b)(11)(ii)(B) to enable interventions when a patient's medications, allergies and intolerance, and problems are incorporated from a transition of care or referral summary received and pursuant to paragraph § 170.315(b)(2)(iii)(D), and we proposed to redesignate the certification criterion requirement currently in § 170.315(b)(11)(ii)(C) as § 170.315(b)(11)(ii)(B). We also proposed to remove the

parenthetical reference in § 170.315(b)(11)(iii) to “drug-drug and drug-allergy contraindication checking” upon the effective date of this final rule. Additionally, we proposed to revise the requirements in § 170.315(b)(11)(iii)(B), which currently relate to enabling predictive DSIs that use any data expressed in the standards in § 170.213, to limit the application of the certification criterion to just data expressed in § 170.315(b)(11)(iii)(A) as well as data expressed in the Clinical Notes and the Assessment and Plan of Treatment data classes in the standards in § 170.213. #### 4. Privacy and Security Certification Criteria

We proposed to remove all the privacy and security (P&S) certification criteria in § 170.315(d) and the associated Privacy and Security Certification Framework (P&S Certification Framework) under § 170.550(h) as of the effective date of this final rule. We proposed to remove the P&S certification criteria and P&S Certification Framework for a multitude of reasons that led to our conclusion that the costs and burden of these certification criteria and Certification Program requirements far exceed their intended purpose and benefits (90 FR 60989-60990). We reiterate these reasons below.

First, we have found that health IT developers have changed their development processes to build in P&S capabilities to meet these certification requirements rather than attempting to demonstrate that their technology could interface with other health IT that provides these capabilities. This has led to negative experiences for health care providers who have sought to use a best-of-breed, non-certified security technology to work across their deployed enterprise (e.g., a third-party audit log).

Second, certified health IT (which is generally EHR-focused technology) is often implemented in enterprise-wide settings (e.g., group practices, large healthcare systems, multiprovider cloud-supported services). However, it cannot, in isolation or independently, provide the necessary P&S capabilities across all relevant technologies in the enterprise-wide setting for covered entities to comply with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) Security Rule (45 CFR part 160 and subparts A and C of part 164).

Third, while certified health IT has always been “linked” to the Medicare Promoting Interoperability Program and the MIPS Promoting Interoperability performance category, those programs have not required participating health care providers to use or implement the P&S capabilities included in certified health IT for purposes of meeting the security risk analysis

measure. Rather, the health care providers must “have” such certified health IT. As such and as discussed in more detail below (and in the Proposed Rule), health care providers participating in the Medicare Promoting Interoperability Program or the MIPS Promoting Interoperability performance category are required to adopt certified capabilities that they may not use because, for example, they may prefer a non-certified security health IT product to meet their overall security needs, including compliance with the HIPAA Security Rule.

Fourth, many interested parties have overinterpreted the Certification Program’s limited scope and policy purpose with respect to health IT security and instead have assumed that the Certification Program assessed discrete specific security capabilities at significant depths, such as when and how the capabilities are implemented in health care provider or system settings. For example, interested parties have expressed incorrect expectations of certified health IT and developers, such as expectations that the Certification Program required health IT developers of certified health IT to implement and conduct penetration testing of certified health IT or ensure that certified authentication and authorization capabilities protect against known or unknown vulnerabilities. In contrast, to allow for significant scalable deployment flexibility, a certified Heath IT Module must only, for example, demonstrate support for basic authentication and rolebased access control. Fifth, there remains a disconnect between health IT certified to the P&S certification criteria under the Certification Program and the implementation and compliance responsibilities of most purchasers and users of certified health IT with the requirements of the HIPAA Privacy33 and Security Rules. This disconnect has led to the introduction of unnecessary regulatory burden and duplication, as well as unwarranted opportunity costs for innovation. As we noted above and in our prior rulemakings related to the P&S certification criteria, the adoption of health IT certified to the P&S certification criteria does not guarantee compliance with the requirements of the HIPAA Privacy and Security Rules. Further, the adoption of health IT certified to the P&S

These facts alone have created unnecessary burden for health care providers that adopt certified health IT and health IT developers of certified health IT that also meet the definition of a covered entity or business associate, respectively, and are subject to the HIPAA Privacy and Security Rules.35 In fact, we have heard from interested parties both arguing that our certification requirements go beyond the HIPAA Security Rule requirements (75 FR 44619, 80 FR 62707) or do not go far enough in meeting the requirements of the HIPAA Privacy Rule and Security Rule (80 FR 62691, 62706). be “general” and would align with the Office of the Federal Register (OFR) Drafting Handbook. In the Proposed Rule, we noted that the proposed revisions, if finalized, would be effective as of the effective date of this final rule.

WCAG Comments. Comments from developers of certified health IT supported removing the WCAG standard and conformance requirement. The majority of commenters argued that we should not remove the WCAG standard and conformance requirement. Commenters also suggested that developers would stop including accessibility requirements and would charge more for accessibility tools. A commenter asserted that not all developers are subject to the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) obligations. Some commenters contended that removing the conformance requirement would shift burden to health care providers. A few commenters suggested that removal of the WCAG conformance requirement would have a negating impact on patient access and usability.

Response. We appreciate the comments submitted that supported and opposed our proposal. We recognize that the WCAG conformance requirement in the Certification Program served a function distinct from the obligations imposed by Federal civil rights laws because certification provides an evaluation of specified health IT functionality, while the other laws impose obligations on entities covered by those laws. We also considered commenters’ concerns that removing the certification requirement could reduce accessibility for patients with

disabilities or place greater responsibility on health care providers to evaluate the accessibility of the health IT they purchase and use. Overall, we disagree with and are not persuaded by the commenters that opposed our proposal.

As noted in the Proposed Rule, the baseline certification standard of WCAG 2.0 Level A was adopted with the 2014 Edition in 2012 and has been part of the voluntary Certification Program and specifically the VDT certification criterion since that time. The functionality is now sufficiently widespread among certified health IT and health care providers who adopt such technology. This is true because the VDT certification criterion has been part of the CEHRT definitions and specifically supports the “Provide Patients Electronic Access to their Health Information” measures under the Medicare Promoting Interoperability Program and MIPS Promoting Interoperability performance category since the 2014 Edition. By removing the WCAG conformance requirement (WCAG 2.0, Level A or optional Level AA), developers of health IT currently certified to § 170.315(e)(1) will likely not abandon accessibility functionality for that certified health IT. Rather, they will have the opportunity to consider other innovative ways to provide patients with accessible viewing functionality. In short, the purpose of certification under the Certification Program has been achieved in drawing attention to accessibility design and in establishing a baseline functionality for such design. In addition, this final rule does not alter the obligations of any entity under the ADA,36 Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act,37 Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act,38 or any other applicable Federal or State law.

##### b. Patient Health Information Capture

We proposed to remove and reserve the “patient health information capture” certification criterion in § 170.315(e)(3) with an effective date of January 1, 2027. The patient health information capture certification criterion allows health care providers to incorporate unstructured patient generated health data or data from a non-clinical setting into a patient record. The criterion is not currently included in the Base EHR definition; however, the criterion is included in the definitions of CEHRT established by CMS in 42 CFR 495.4 and 42 CFR 414.1305 for programs including the Medicare Promoting Interoperability Program and the MIPS Promoting Interoperability performance category.

We have reviewed and evaluated the patient health information capture certification criterion in § 170.315(e)(3) and sought to identify ways to reduce burden while still supporting our policy goals. As we have stressed in prior rulemaking, we take an incremental approach to improving health IT interoperability and performance, relying on factors such as market

prevalence and industry readiness to harmonize current and future standards and phase out standards and certification criteria as needed (75 FR 2021).

As we mentioned in the Proposed Rule, removing the patient health information capture certification criterion in § 170.315(e)(3) on January 1, 2027, would reduce administrative burden as well as burden for health IT developers of certified health IT and health care providers while still allowing us to achieve our policy goals (90 FR 60991 and 60992).

Comments. Of the commenters who specifically commented on the patient health information capture proposal, they were generally evenly split on supporting or not supporting removal of the patient health information capture certification criterion (§ 170.315(e)(3)) with those in support noting that it would reduce burden by eliminating a criterion they described as widely implemented, poorly defined, or not delivering value.

Commenters not supporting removal (or recommending delay/retention) expressed concerns that removing the criterion could reduce support for incorporating patient- and caregiver-contributed information (including unstructured patient-generated health data) in certain settings, including Long Term and Post Acute Care, weaken patient engagement and accessibility, and create challenges for providers seeking to meet other program and interoperability expectations. Another commenter, who did not support the removal, recommended that we add the patient health information capture certification criterion to the Base EHR definition in § 170.102.

In particular, commenters noted that our proposal for removing the patient health information capture criterion would require additional guidance or specification of transition planning and impact evaluation (including coordination with other Federal program requirements). A commenter stated that ONC would also need to clarify how the shift toward FHIR-based APIs would support family caregiver access, including proxy access mechanisms, delegation workflows, and caregiver-contributed data capture through third-party applications.

Another commenter suggested delaying the removal of the criterion until at least 2028 to support alignment with CMS requirements.

Response. We thank commenters for their input and support of our proposal to remove the patient health information capture certification criterion. We have finalized the removal of the patient health information capture certification as proposed with an effective date of January 1, 2027. As mentioned in the Proposed Rule and supported by a number of commenters, the capabilities described in the criterion are widely implemented and used in health IT at this time, suggesting that these capabilities will remain in health IT products even if the corresponding criterion is removed from the Certification Program (90 FR 60991). We also noted in the Proposed Rule that removing the criterion from the Certification Program could spur greater development and innovation in this area (90 FR 60991). We stated that there are other certification criteria that support patient engagement, such as the “view, download, and transmit to 3rd party” and “standardized API for patient and population services” certification criteria (90 FR 60991). We have seen developers integrate the functionality in the patient health information capture certification criterion as part of other patient engagement features, such as patient portals. With these considerations in mind, we believe removing this criterion from the Certification Program will help reduce burden and costs, while also spurring further innovations in patient engagement (90 FR 60991).

Because the certification criterion in § 170.315(e)(3) is a requirement for CEHRT definitions established by CMS, we proposed to remove the patient health information capture certification criterion in § 170.315(e)(3) effective January 1, 2027 (90 FR 60991), and we have finalized this date for removal in this final rule. Because patient health information capture is a requirement of the CEHRT definition, and both the Medicare Promoting Interoperability program and the MIPS program are on a calendar year rulemaking cycle, it is appropriate to align changes to the certification criteria that are included in the definition of CEHRT to a calendar year cycle as well. In the FY 2027 IPPS/LTCH PPS proposed rule, CMS proposed to

update the definition of CEHRT in 42 CFR 495.4 to remove the cross-reference to the certification criterion in § 170.315(e)(3) (91 FR 19620). CMS finalized this proposal in the FY 2027 IPPS/LTCH PPS final rule, which appeared in the Federal Register on August 4, 2026 (91 FR 50033). CMS made similar proposals for the definition of CEHRT in 42 CFR 414.1305 in the 2027 Physician Fee Schedule proposed rule (91 FR 43842). This final action and proposed policy will (or would in the case of proposed policies) enable CMS to ensure that requirements in the definitions of CEHRT are updated to align with our final policy regarding the removal of this certification criterion. ONC will continue to coordinate with CMS to ensure alignment around changes resulting from the policies in this final rule.

We also appreciate commenters who opposed removal or recommended delaying removal based on concerns about patient- and caregiver-contributed information (including unstructured patient-generated health data) in Long Term and Post Acute Care settings, patient engagement, accessibility, and providers’ ability to meet other program and interoperability expectations. We recognize the importance of enabling patients, families, caregivers, and other authorized representatives to contribute information. However, we do not believe that continued certification to § 170.315(e)(3) is necessary to preserve or advance those capabilities. The removal of the certification criterion does not prohibit health IT developers from continuing to offer these functions, does not prevent providers from using or procuring such capabilities, and does not limit the ability of health IT products to support patient- or caregiver-contributed information. Rather, removal means that this specific functionality will no longer be addressed through a certification criterion under the Certification Program. We acknowledge commenters’ specific concerns regarding family caregiver access, proxy access, delegation workflows, and caregiver-contributed data capture through third-party applications. We agree that these are important use cases. However, § 170.315(e)(3) does not establish detailed requirements for proxy access, caregiver delegation, identity proofing, authorization management, or applicationbased caregiver contribution workflows. Retaining this criterion would therefore not, by itself,

ensure that those more specific capabilities are available or implemented consistently across health IT products. We also decline to add the patient health information capture certification criterion to the Base EHR definition in § 170.102. Because we are finalizing removal of § 170.315(e)(3), adding the criterion to the Base EHR definition would be inconsistent with our determination that the availability of a certification criterion for this specific functionality is no longer necessary. The Base EHR definition reflects certification capabilities that remain necessary as a minimum foundation for certified health IT. Based on the widespread implementation of this functionality, the availability of other patient engagement and API criteria, and the burden associated with maintaining certification to § 170.315(e)(3), we do not believe this criterion should be added to the Base EHR definition. We also considered the recommendation to delay removal until at least 2028. We do not believe a further delay is necessary. The January 1, 2027, effective date allows for regulatory alignment with CMS program requirements under the CEHRT definitions while avoiding the continued burden of maintaining a certification requirement that no longer provides sufficient incremental value. Extending the criterion beyond that date would prolong certification and compliance costs for developers and providers without materially advancing patient engagement beyond what is already supported through existing product functionality, other certification criteria, and applicable Federal policies. ONC has a history of recognizing the importance of patient engagement as a core component of modern healthcare, driving improved outcomes, enhanced safety, and lower costs by leveraging technology and intends to continue doing so with future policies. The ONC Cures Act Final Rule implemented parts of section 4006(a) of the Cures Act to support patients' access to their EHI in a form convenient for patients, such as making a patient's EHI more electronically accessible through the adoption of standards and certification criteria and the implementation of information blocking policies that support patient electronic access to their health information at no cost (85 FR 25644). Specifically, the ONC Cures Act Final Rule supported patient access to their health information by: (1)enabling patients to

securely access their EHI using smartphone applications of their choice through standardized APIs; (2) emphasizing under the information blocking “fees exception” that an actor’s practice of charging for the electronic access to EHI by a patient, their personal representative, or another person or entity designated by the patient, would be inherently suspect under an information blocking review; and (3)permitting actors regulated by the information blocking regulations to educate and advise individuals about privacy and security risks posed by applications they use to steward their information while also fully supporting an individual’s right to choose which application(s) they want to share their EHI with—if any. The HTI-1 Final Rule further supported patients by requiring developers certified to the “view, download, and transmit to 3rd party” certification criterion to support an “internet-based method” for patients to request a restriction on the use or disclosure of their data (89 FR 1299). We will continue to prioritize patient and caregiver engagement and monitor future developments. ONC seeks to further the advancement of patient engagement by promoting electronic access to health data and increasing resources dedicated to curbing the practice of information blocking.39

##### c. Safety-enhanced Design

In the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60995), we proposed to remove the “safety-enhanced design” (SED) certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(3) and the reference to this certification criterion in § 170.550(g)(1) as of the effective date of this final rule. The safety-enhanced design certification criterion specifies the user-centered design testing that must be applied to certain

health IT capabilities submitted for certification. Certification to this certification criterion is conditional as its scope and applicability depends on the other certification criteria for which a Health IT Module is presented for certification.

As discussed in the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60995 and 60996), we anticipated that removal of this certification criterion would reduce costs and burden for health IT developers and health care providers.

Comments. The majority of commenters were not supportive of removing the SED certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(3). These commenters stated that the SED certification criterion remains an important patient safety, usability, and human factors engineering safeguard and expressed concerns that removal of this certification criterion would weaken a baseline certification expectation for user-centered design, increase the risk of technology-induced errors, promote inefficiency and inconsistency, and shift responsibility for identifying and mitigating such risks to providers and purchasers. Some commenters noted that if safety verification became the responsibility of providers and purchasers, then smaller, rural, or low-resource practices would incur the most burden, as they may have limited technical resources and expertise to independently evaluate the safety and security of their software systems. A few commenters who opposed removal of the SED certification criterion expressed concerns that removal may cause health IT developers to revert to engineering-centric interfaces or reduce incentives to prioritize the clinician-user experience and could create a scenario where provider organizations are charged for design techniques that were previously included in health IT products. A few other commenters stated that removal of § 170.315(g)(3) could create an unlevel playing field between established developers and new market entrants, particularly if new entrants are not held to comparable usability and safety expectations. Some commenters who opposed removal also noted that safety-enhanced design and usability testing may become more important as SI-enabled functionality is incorporated into certified health IT and recommended that safety validation approaches evolve rather than be eliminated.

Some of the commenters who recommended retaining the certification criterion emphasized that the SED certification criterion, and related usability requirements, were originally implemented in response to real-world patient safety incidents and that the certification criterion continues to serve as an important safeguard against technology-induced harm. Another commenter expressed concerns that removal without first putting critical safeguards in place could result in unsafe practices and billing fraud.

Response. We thank commenters for their feedback. We have not finalized our proposal to remove the SED certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(3) or the reference to this certification criterion in § 170.550(g)(1). Instead, we have finalized § 170.315(g)(3) with revisions, as of the effective date of this final rule. Specifically, we have finalized the revision of § 170.315(g)(3) to an attestation-based certification requirement by removing the requirements in § 170.315(g)(3)(ii), (iv), and (v), making conforming revisions to paragraph (g)(3), and retaining the reference to § 170.315(g)(3) in § 170.550(g)(1).

We acknowledge comments that recommended we retain § 170.315(g)(3) and stated that removal of the SED certification criterion would not be appropriate without a clearly defined replacement mechanism that preserves baseline expectations for user-centered design and safety validation. We also recognize commenters’ statements that the SED certification criterion, and related usability requirements, were implemented in response to real-world patient safety incidents and that the certification criterion continues to serve as an important safeguard against technology-induced harm. We agree with these commenters that § 170.315(g)(3) continues to serve an important safety function in the Certification Program by maintaining baseline assurance that developers of certified health IT apply user-centered design processes to certain health IT capabilities.

In the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60995), we proposed to remove the SED certification criterion to reduce costs and burden associated with certification for health IT developers and health care providers. Specifically, we identified the testing and reporting requirements

associated with certification to § 170.315(g)(3) as contributing to health IT developers’ burden and stated that removal of the SED certification criterion could allow health IT developers to redirect time and resources toward more innovative approaches to support and improve safetyenhanced design in their products (90 FR 60996). In consideration of commenters’ explanations for why we should retain the SED certification criterion, we reassessed whether our proposal to completely remove the criterion is necessary to achieve our objective of reducing costs and burden. We determined that revising § 170.315(g)(3) to establish an attestation-based certification requirement addresses these considerations by reducing the testing and reporting requirements associated with certification while retaining the certification expectation for the application of user-centered design practices to certain certified health IT capabilities.

Comments. Some commenters recommended that if the SED certification criterion was removed, we should adopt alternative mechanisms to support continued usability and safety oversight. A commenter suggested prioritizing the use of post-market data to identify usability failures. A few other commenters recommended that we provide explicit transition guidance for products currently certified to § 170.315(g)(3) to ensure continuity of safety expectations and avoid gaps during any transition. Another commenter recommended that we provide a model risk assessment framework, particularly tailored to rural and low-resource providers, to support evaluation of health IT usability and safety in the absence of this certification requirement.

Response. We acknowledge commenters who recommended alternatives to our proposal, including prioritizing the use of post-market data to identify usability failures, providing explicit transition guidance, and offering additional tools to support providers in evaluating usability and safety. While we have not adopted these suggested alternatives in this final rule, the revisions that we have finalized to § 170.315(g)(3) take into consideration comments that urged us to continue to support usability and safety oversight certification requirements rather than eliminate them completely.

Comments. A smaller number of commenters expressed support for removing or streamlining the SED certification criterion. Of the commenters who supported removal of § 170.315(g)(3), most stated that current summative usability testing and related documentation requirements are costly and administratively burdensome, have limited practical value, and divert resources from formative usability testing and other product improvement activities. Additionally, commenters who supported removal of this certification criterion agreed that there is widespread market adoption of safety-enhanced design practices. Other commenters recommended that, rather than remove the certification criterion, we should retain and modernize or streamline it. These recommendations included adopting a lean form of SED by narrowing requirements, focusing on higher-risk workflows, adopting a lighter-weight attestation-based approach, reducing prescriptive reporting, and aligning with recognized usability engineering frameworks such as those defined by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). Many of these commenters stated that formative usability testing conducted during development is more effective than the required summative testing model and that removing the current certification requirements would allow resources to be redirected toward more impactful usability practices. Another commenter specified that conducting user-centered design testing after development practices limits the ability to incorporate findings into products and that clients rarely access or use the detailed reports required by the SED certification criterion. Another commenter stated that safetyenhanced design features should be decided by markets.

Response. We thank commenters for their feedback and perspectives on how safetyenhanced design continues to play an important role in supporting safe and effective use of certified health IT but that current summative usability testing and related documentation requirements are costly and administratively burdensome. As discussed above, we have finalized revisions to § 170.315(g)(3) that reduce prescriptive testing and reporting requirements while retaining a meaningful certification expectation for user-centered design. This approach is

intended to balance the need to maintain baseline safety and usability expectations with the goal of reducing unnecessary burden and allowing health IT developers to redirect time and resources towards more innovative approaches to support and improve safety-enhanced design in their products. We refer readers to section VII (Regulatory Impact Analysis) of this final rule for more detailed information on cost estimates of safety-enhanced design and estimated cost savings. We have determined that the current reporting requirements for the SED certification criterion can be reduced to an attestation-based approach that retains a meaningful certification expectation for SED. Therefore, we have finalized the SED certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(3) to an attestation-based certification requirement by removing the requirements in § 170.315(g)(3)(ii), (iv), and (v), making conforming revisions to paragraph (g)(3), and retaining the reference to § 170.315(g)(3) in § 170.550(g)(1). All revisions to the SED certification criterion are effective as of the effective date of this final rule.

##### d. Quality Management System

In the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60996), we proposed to remove the “quality management system” (QMS) certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(4) and the reference to this certification criterion in § 170.550(g)(2), as of the effective date of this final rule. The QMS certification criterion states for each capability that a technology includes and for which that capability’s certification is sought, the use of a QMS in the development, testing, implementation, and maintenance of that capability must be identified and satisfied in one of the following ways: either the QMS used is established by the Federal Government or a standards development organization (SDO) or the QMS used is mapped to one or more QMS established by the Federal Government or an SDO. Per § 170.550(g), this certification criterion is mandatory for all certified Health IT Modules.

As discussed in the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60996), we proposed to remove this certification criterion because industry and other interested parties now widely implement quality management principles in comparison to when this certification criterion was initially adopted.

We determined as a result, that the certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(4), and the related requirement that all Health IT Modules be certified to it, have become duplicative.

Comments. The majority of commenters were not supportive of removing the QMS certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(4). These commenters expressed concerns that removal would weaken baseline safety, quality, and reliability expectations for certified health IT and shift verification responsibilities from developers to providers and purchasers. Many of these commenters also expressed concerns that removal of the certification criterion could reduce assurances for quality practices, increase the likelihood that immature products enter clinical and operational workflows, and result in inconsistent adoption of quality management practices, including by new market entrants.

Some commenters also noted that removal of this certification criterion could introduce new challenges in evaluating health IT developer safety practices and managing risk, particularly for rural or low-resourced providers with limited technical resources and expertise to independently evaluate the safety, security, and quality of their software systems. Some commenters who opposed removal of the QMS certification criterion recommended that, if the criterion was removed, we provide additional guidance on a replacement approach and how safety, quality, and reliability expectations would be maintained through modernization efforts, including API-based ecosystems and other programmatic mechanisms. Several of these commenters also recommended adopting post-certification oversight and transparency mechanisms to assess the impact of removing this certification requirement and to offer providers information about developers’ capabilities and associated costs.

A small number of commenters supported removing the certification criterion or expressed a neutral view, stating that the requirement can be seen as duplicative since many developers already maintain quality management system practices as part of routine operations. We received mixed comments regarding the level of burden associated with the QMS certification criterion and whether removal of the criterion would meaningfully reduce burden.

As noted above, a few commenters who expressed a neutral view or supported removal of this certification criterion stated that many mature development organizations would continue to maintain quality management practices and follow the ISO industry standard regardless of certification requirements. Other commenters noted that the current QMS certification criterion was burdensome for developers and system administrators and recommended that we should modernize or streamline the requirement rather than remove it.

Response. We thank commenters for their thoughtful feedback. We have not finalized our proposal to remove the quality management system certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(4) or the corresponding reference in § 170.550(g)(2).

After considering the comments we received, we believe § 170.315(g)(4) continues to serve an important function in the Certification Program by providing a consistent baseline assurance that developers of certified health IT identify and use a quality management system in the development, testing, implementation, and maintenance of certified health IT capabilities. We recognize that many developers of certified health IT already maintain quality management principles and processes as part of routine operations. However, we believe that maintaining a baseline certification requirement at this time is important to ensure continuity of safety and quality practices and to promote consistent expectations across the market, including for new entrants. We recognize that quality management expectations may continue to evolve as part of broader health IT modernization efforts and that the QMS certification criterion continues to support providers and purchasers that rely on certification as a meaningful signal of developer quality management practices. Our decision to retain § 170.315(g)(4) also supports consistency with the Certification Program’s broader approach to transparency and public reporting regarding the quality management processes used by developers for certified health IT.

Comments. Some commenters who opposed removal of the QMS certification criterion expressed concerns about coordination with CMS program timelines and recommended delaying

removal of the certification criterion to align with CMS program requirements and reduce potential provider compliance risks.

Response. We acknowledge commenters’ concerns regarding coordination with CMS program timelines and related provider compliance activities. However, while the QMS certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(4) impacts certified health IT used by health care providers for compliance with CMS programs, we are not aware of any direct requirements for adoption or use of certified health IT certified to the QMS certification criterion. For instance, with respect to the Promoting Interoperability program and the MIPS Promoting Interoperability performance category, CMS has not specified the criterion in § 170.315(g)(4) as required to support any of the current measures in the program as part of the definitions of CEHRT in 42 CFR 495.4 or 42 CFR 414.1305. Our decision not to finalize removal of § 170.315(g)(4) is not based on CMS program dependency, but rather that this certification criterion continues to serve an important role within the Certification Program as a baseline quality management and assurance expectation, as well as to support transparency and maintain consistent adoption of QMS practices over time. Therefore, we have not finalized our proposal to remove the quality management system certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(4) or the corresponding reference in § 170.550(g)(2).

##### e. Accessibility-centered Design

In the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60997), we proposed to remove the “accessibility-centered design” (ACD) certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(5) and the corresponding reference to this certification criterion in § 170.550(g)(3) as of the effective date of this final rule. This certification criterion requires that all developers of Health IT Modules identify and use a health IT accessibility-centered design standard or law in the development, testing, implementation, and maintenance of each capability for which certification is sought. We stated that, if finalized, removal of this certification criterion would not exempt health IT developers and providers from their independent obligations under applicable Federal civil rights laws, including Section 504 of

the Rehabilitation Act, Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, and the Americans with Disabilities Act.

Comments. Most commenters were not supportive of the removal of the ACD certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(5). These commenters expressed concerns that removal of this certification criterion could reduce accessibility and equitable access to certified health IT for individuals with disabilities and weaken a measurable certification baseline for accountability. One of these commenters emphasized that accessibility is not static and requires ongoing monitoring, updates, and testing as platforms evolve. Another commenter remarked that the impact of this certification criterion has not diminished and stated that there is no guarantee that health IT developers will ensure accessibility-centered design practices continue if the ACD certification criterion is removed.

Many commenters who opposed our proposal also expressed concerns that removal of the ACD certification criterion could reduce transparency and incentives to maintain accessibilitycentered design practices, leading to uneven adoption, particularly among new market entrants, and widening gaps in digital participation. A few commenters also emphasized that accessibilitycentered design requirements support standardized, interoperable access to health information and expressed concern that removal of this certification criterion could undermine accessibility in API-based and SI-enabled environments. Multiple commenters cautioned that reducing accessibility and usability requirements could increase the risk of inefficient workflows and could lead to an increase in clinician workload and cognitive burden. Many of these commenters expressed concerns that removal of § 170.315(g)(5) would shift costs, usability barriers, and responsibility for ensuring accessibility to providers, patients, and caregivers, including the potential for developers to charge additional fees for accessibility features previously included as part of certified health IT. Some commenters recommended that, if we were to remove § 170.315(g)(5), we should adopt a replacement accountability mechanism and provide transition guidance.

Several commenters who opposed removal of this certification criterion stated that applicable Federal civil rights laws do not serve the same function or provide the same measurable accountability as a Certification Program requirement. A few other commenters argued that removing the ACD certification criterion would not meaningfully reduce burden because some developers would maintain accessibility practices regardless of the certification requirement.

Response. We thank commenters for their thoughtful feedback. We have not finalized our proposal to remove the ACD certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(5) or the corresponding reference in § 170.550(g)(3).

We continue to recognize that accessibility-centered design practices are broadly used across industry. However, we determined that this widespread use of accessibility-centered design practices supports retaining, rather than removing, the ACD certification criterion at this time. We have concluded that § 170.315(g)(5) continues to serve an important function in the Certification Program by maintaining a transparent, measurable baseline regarding the use of accessibility-centered design standards or laws by developers for certified health IT capabilities. We also believe that accessibility is not static and that continued certification transparency and accountability remain important.

The requirements in § 170.315(g)(5) support consistency with the Certification Program’s broader approach to transparency and public reporting regarding the accessibilitycentered design standards or laws used in the development of certified health IT. Additionally, retaining § 170.315(g)(5) helps to reduce the risk of uneven adoption or maintenance of accessibility-centered design practices over time, including among new market entrants. Therefore, we have not finalized our proposal to remove the ACD certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(5) or the corresponding reference in § 170.550(g)(3).

Comments. A small number of commenters expressed support for or did not object to the removal of this certification criterion. Most of these commenters stated that this certification

requirement is redundant given that accessibility requirements continue to apply under applicable Federal civil rights laws. Additionally, these commenters noted that removal of an attestationbased certification criterion would not meaningfully reduce burden or impact the Certification Program and that the current certification criterion is overly broad or limited in practical utility. A commenter noted that removal of the ACD certification criterion is a simplification measure for the Certification Program. Another commenter stated that accessibility-centered design features should be decided by markets. Several commenters recommended, if § 170.315(g)(5) was removed, that we explicitly clarify in the subsequent final rule that accessibility expectations for developers and healthcare organizations would remain subject to Federal civil rights laws.

Response. We thank commenters for their feedback and continue to believe that § 170.315(g)(5) plays an important role in the Certification Program by providing a transparent and measurable baseline for how health IT developers apply accessibility-centered design standards or laws to certified health IT capabilities. We have not finalized our proposal to remove the ACD certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(5) or the corresponding reference in § 170.550(g)(3).

##### f. Consolidated CDA Creation Performance

In the Proposed Rule (90 FR 60997), we proposed to remove and reserve the “Consolidated CDA creation performance” certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(6). We also proposed to remove § 170.550(g)(4) which references the certification criterion. We stated that the certification criterion helps to ensure the proper conformance of Consolidated CDAs (CCDAs) for transition of care and referral summaries that are sent to and received from external organizations, and that while C-CDA-based standards are still in use, the industry is shifting toward adoption of FHIR-based standards, which are designed to enable greater interoperability and innovation. We stated that we believe that maintaining the current level of support for this and other C-CDA-based criteria, including putting effort and resources toward testing and certification, can detract from industry efforts to move toward FHIR-based solutions, and that

removing the certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(6) would contribute to reducing the effort health IT developers and health care providers are focusing on C-CDA support and allow for redirection of efforts toward greater use of FHIR-based standards in health IT. We proposed to remove the Consolidated CDA creation performance certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(6) as of the effective date of this final rule.

Comments. Of the commenters that specifically commented on this proposal, the majority of commenters were not supportive of removing the Consolidated CDA creation performance certification criterion, primarily because they believed that FHIR-based alternatives were not yet mature, finalized, or adopted at scale and that no equivalent FHIR-based replacement had been clearly identified, adopted, or deployed. Many commenters stressed that the current exchange environment remains hybrid, and C-CDA-based document exchange is still foundational, predominant, or widely used, particularly in under-resourced settings. Multiple commenters noted specific use cases that depend on C-CDA-based document exchange, including Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS), risk adjustment, care management, clinical registries and quality measurement, referrals, transitions of care, longitudinal aggregation, encounter notifications, public health, legacy EHR interoperability, patient event notifications, and certain legal documentation. Several commenters stated that C-CDA-based document exchange is better for patients, caregivers, and complete record sharing. Some commenters stressed that C-CDA-based document exchange helps bridge the transition to FHIR by supporting hybrid exchange, mappings, and longitudinal records. A number of commenters were concerned that removal of C-CDA-based requirements before a FHIR alternative was in place would create a gap and impact interoperability as well as reduce data quality, consistency, predictability, and usability. Several comments warned that removal of C-CDA-based requirements before FHIR-based alternatives are mature and widely adopted could lead to fragmented workflows and increased costs or data exchange burdens for providers.

Response. We thank commenters for these comments regarding FHIR readiness and the removal of the current C-CDA-based certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(6) prior to having a FHIR alternative in place. We appreciate the concerns expressed by commenters that C-CDAbased document exchange is widely used and there is not a mature FHIR-based alternative available at this time. However, we note that the Consolidated CDA creation performance certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(6) has been a conditional, secondary C-CDA implementation requirement applied to health IT for which certification to a primary C-CDAbased certification criterion is sought. For instance, the transitions of care certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(1) includes C-CDA-based requirements, and each product presented for certification to § 170.315(b)(1) would also need to be certified to § 170.315(g)(6) per its additional conformance requirements. It was this second layer of burden we proposed to remove, and we are finalizing the removal of § 170.315(g)(6). Additionally, as discussed earlier in section III.A.2.a, we have extended the timeline for removing the C-CDA-based standards requirements in § 170.315(b)(1) until January 1, 2030. We believe this addresses commenter concerns regarding the immediate removal of C-CDA-based standards requirements from the Certification Program without a viable FHIR alternative. As stated in section III.A.2.a, we explain that significant advances are occurring in the development and adoption of FHIR-based standards, and we anticipate substantial progress by January 1, 2030. Therefore, we do not believe it is necessary to retain this additional C-CDA-based certification requirement in § 170.315(g)(6) in order to ensure an appropriate transition from C-CDA-based standards to FHIR-based standards in the Certification Program. As such, we have finalized the proposal to remove the Consolidated CDA creation performance criterion in § 170.315(g)(6) as of the effective date of this final rule. We have also finalized the proposal to remove § 170.550(g)(4) as discussed in more detail in section III.E.2.

Comments. Several commenters expressed concerns about patient safety, including concerns that removal of the certification criterion could disrupt continuity of care, care

coordination, patient safety, or patient outcomes. Some commenters noted that certification to the criterion in § 170.315(g)(6) is an important safeguard, accountability mechanism, and testing floor.

Response. We thank commenters for expressing these concerns about patient safety, and we appreciate the importance of ensuring that health information is exchanged reliably during transitions of care and in referral summaries. However, as already noted, the certification criterion in § 170.315(g)(6) is not the only certification criterion in the Certification Program that requires use of C-CDA-based standards and that helps ensure reliable data exchange during transitions of care. As discussed in section III.A.2.a, we have extended the deadline for removal of C-CDA-based requirements in the transitions of care certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(1). We believe this extension will provide enough time for certified health IT to move from C-CDAbased standards to FHIR-based standards without sacrificing patient safety. In addition, we reiterate that the removal of the certification criterion does not necessitate removal of the functionality from health IT systems for users who may have these functions implemented, nor does it prohibit continued use of the referenced standards in existing and new health IT implementation.

Comments. A number of commenters expressed concerns about additional burden that the proposal could place on providers, stating that the removal of C-CDA-based exchange requirements could increase manual workflows, reconciliation, quality assurance, testing, normalization, and other administrative burden. Some commented that the proposal could disproportionately burden smaller, rural, safety-net, tribal, post-acute, behavioral health, and similar under-resourced settings. Another commenter stated that the proposal would not reduce developer burden because developers would still need to maintain C-CDA support for the foreseeable future.

Response. We thank commenters for expressing these concerns about burden, and in particular burden on under-resourced providers and settings. We appreciate the importance of not

imposing additional burden on providers by removing requirements related to C-CDA-based exchange. Again, it is important to understand the context associated with this specific certification criterion, which is secondarily applied to health IT certified to a C-CDA referencing criterion. C-CDA IGs have become more rigorous and less ambiguous, and existing test tools and industry experience with C-CDA is far greater now than when this certification criterion was first introduced. Thus, we believe that the continued need for this certification criterion in the Certification Program has concluded. Regarding the comment about the burden reducing impact of this proposal, we believe that even if a developer maintains C-CDA support, removal of this certification criterion would reduce maintenance efforts for developers, including effort and resources that would be put toward testing and certification to this criterion if the criterion remained part of the Certification Program.

Comments. Some commenters expressed disagreement with the general rationale that health IT developers would continue to support C-CDA-based exchange after removal of CCDA-based exchange requirements in the Certification Program. These commenters were concerned that removal might result in developers charging extra for C-CDA-based exchange functionality once that functionality is no longer part of the certification requirements, and several commenters were concerned that fees to maintain C-CDA-based exchange would harm under-resourced providers and settings. Some commenters had concerns that new entrants to the market might omit core capabilities, implement them inconsistently, use proprietary alternatives, or lack the real-world knowledge needed to support C-CDA-based exchange.

Response. We thank commenters for expressing these concerns about developers, and in particular new entrants to the market, not providing support for C-CDA-based exchange. We appreciate the importance of ensuring that support for this functionality is continued during a transition to FHIR-based exchange, especially for under-resourced providers and settings. As stated above, other certification criteria in the Certification Program also require use of C-CDAbased standards for exchange and can help ensure support during a transition from C-CDA-based

exchange to FHIR-based exchange. We have extended the deadline for removal of C-CDA-based requirements in the transitions of care certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(1) until January 1, 2030. We believe this extension will provide enough time for certified health IT to move from CCDA-based standards to FHIR-based standards without providers being subject to increased fees or reduced developer support for C-CDA-based exchange during the transition. As noted above, we reiterate that removing this certification criterion neither requires removal of the functionality from existing implementations nor prohibits continued use of the referenced standards in new or existing health IT.

Comments. A number of commenters contended that the timeline was too short and that there was no sufficiently clear phased plan, readiness benchmark, or implementation guidance.

Response. We thank commenters for sharing these concerns regarding timelines and for expressing a desire for a phased plan for transitioning from C-CDA-based exchange to FHIRbased exchange. ### B. Standards and Implementation Specifications for Health Information Technology

#### 1. Standards and Implementation Specifications

As discussed in the Proposed Rule, we proposed to remove or revise certain certification criteria in the Certification Program in an effort to reduce burden, offer flexibility to both developers and health care providers, as well as to support innovation. Included in certain certification criteria are references to standards. In those instances where we proposed to remove the criterion, we also generally proposed to remove the standard that is referenced from the CFR. Where the certification criterion is not removed as of the effective date of this final rule but allows time for transition until January 1, 2027, we generally proposed to also remove those referenced standards on January 1, 2027. Only in limited circumstances do we retain a standard that is referenced in a certification criterion proposed for removal. We also specifically requested comment on whether we should retain any of the standards and implementation specifications proposed for removal because the standard or implementation specification should be required for purposes of health IT alignment, for instance, when acquiring, implementing or upgrading health IT systems and products consistent with sections 13111 and 13112 of the HITECH Act. In certain instances, we did not propose to remove a standard even though we proposed to remove the certification criterion where the standard is referenced. As noted, under section 3004 of the PHSA, we may adopt standards independently of associated criteria in the Certification Program. Standards adopted in 45 CFR 170 subpart B may be referenced by other HHS programs and policies besides the Certification Program. For example, standards adopted in 45 CFR 170 subpart B, regardless of whether they are associated with health IT certification criteria, are also

required for use under sections 13111 and 13112 of the HITECH Act when acquiring, implementing or upgrading health IT systems and products in certain instances. We did not propose to remove the standard in § 170.205(o), data segmentation for privacy, because some developers still rely on these standards, which allow health care providers to tag healthcare documents with privacy metadata. We also did not propose to remove the Direct Project transport standard in § 170.202(a)(2) since we realize that the Direct Project standards are widely utilized in various use cases and emphasized that developers may still use these standards even if they are removed from certification criteria.

We proposed a minor revision to § 170.205(d)(2) to add the full name of the HL7 Messaging Standard Version 2.5.1 standard. Lastly, we requested comments on retaining the “Applicability Statement for Secure Health Transport” requirement found in § 170.315(h)(1)(i).

Comments. While many commenters expressed support for the direction and objectives of the Proposed Rule, we also received many comments that expressed concerns with the proposed compliance timelines and transition dates. Some commenters stated that having different removal and transition dates for the majority of certification criteria risks creating discontinuities for providers, potential compromises to operational stability and patient safety, uncertainty, and functional gaps.

Multiple commenters expressed that the proposed timelines were too compressed and did not provide an adequate transition time. Many of these commenters provided suggestions and alternatives that they believed would better reflect vendor development cycles, implementation requirements, workforce constraints, and Federal mandates. sufficient time for developers and customers to plan upgrades, contracting, and validation before any criteria are retired, as well as ensure standards remain available through the full transition to avoid compliance gaps.

Response. We thank commenters for their feedback on the proposed compliance and transition dates. In response to concerns with timelines and compliance gaps, for several certification criteria, we have either revised the effective dates or have not finalized our proposal, such as § 170.315(b)(1), (f)(4), and (f)(7). We refer readers to their respective sections of this final rule for further details. In response to concerns with risks creating discontinuities for providers and functional gaps, we note that the removal of a standard does not require reissuance of a certificate in the Certification Program and the standard can still remain in the product. The removal of a standard from the Certification Program does not necessitate the removal of health IT functions using that standard or preclude the continued use of that standard in Health IT Modules. In future rulemaking when a new standard or a new set of standards are adopted, in particular FHIR-based standards, we intend to consider appropriate timelines for developers to adopt and implement these new standards in any future rulemaking establishing new requirements for the Certification Program.

Because vocabulary standards for representing electronic health information in § 170.207(r) and (s) are referenced in § 170.315(c)(4), which we have finalized our proposal to remove effective January 1, 2027, as discussed in section III.A.3.b., we have also finalized the removal of § 170.207(r) and (s), effective January 1, 2027. Because the transition dates in § 170.402(b)(2)(i) and (ii) had passed, we proposed to revise the Maintenance of Certification requirements in § 170.402(b)(2) to remove the transition dates and only include that a health IT developer that must comply with the requirements of § 170.402(a)(4) must provide all of its customers of certified health IT with the health IT certified to the certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(10) (90 FR 61001).

We also proposed additional conforming edits to § 170.402(b). Because we proposed to remove the requirements in § 170.315(b)(11)(iv) and (v) regarding source attribute support, access, and modification and in § 170.315(b)(11)(vi) regarding intervention risk management for predictive DSIs (90 FR 60987), we also proposed to remove § 170.402(b)(4), which requires health IT developers to review and update as necessary source attribute information and intervention risk management practices (90 FR 61001 and 61002).

Comments. The majority of commenters supported the proposed conforming edits to the Assurances Maintenance of Certification requirements, stating that the proposed revisions would have the benefit of reducing certification/compliance burden and streamlining certification obligations. Some commenters also supported specific technical updates within the Assurances provisions, including removing outdated references related to EHI Export.

Commenters were generally evenly split on supporting or opposing the proposed removal of the Assurances Maintenance requirement in § 170.402(b)(4), with those in support noting that removal of § 170.402(b)(4) would avoid retaining a maintenance requirement for provisions that no longer exist in the related certification criterion. Commenters that opposed the removal of the Assurances Maintenance requirement in § 170.402(b)(4) recommended retaining the associated Assurances Maintenance of Certification requirement in § 170.402(b)(4), consistent with their recommendations for retaining requirements under the DSI criterion for providing source attributes and intervention risk management practices, as described in section III.A.2.e. of this final rule.

Response. We appreciate commenters’ support for our proposal to make conforming edits to the Assurances Conditions and Maintenance of Certification requirements and we finalize, as proposed, the revisions. Because the transition dates in § 170.402(b)(2)(i) and (ii) have passed, we have finalized in the Maintenance of Certification requirements in § 170.402(b)(2) the removal of the transition dates and only include that a health IT developer that must comply with the requirements of § 170.402(a)(4) must provide all of its customers of certified health IT with the health IT certified to the certification criterion in § 170.315(b)(10).

With respect to the comments regarding the DSI criterion, we have finalized, as proposed, the removal of § 170.402(b)(4), which requires health IT developers to review and update as necessary source attribute information and intervention risk management practices. As discussed in section III.A.2.e. of this final rule, we have finalized removal of source attribute information transparency for evidence-based and predictive decision support interventions in

§ 170.315(b)(11)(iv), as well as removal of source attribute access and modification requirements and intervention risk management requirements in § 170.315(b)(11)(v) and (vi), Because we have finalized the removal of certain requirements in § 170.315(b)(11), we no longer believe that a maintenance requirement is necessary under § 170.402(b)(4). A commenter requested that we remove the transparency requirement that certified developers publish their service base URLs publicly in § 170.404(b)(2)). The commenter argued

that a published URL list could reveal a developer’s customer footprint, the identity of its customers, its approximate market position and growth, and where an emerging competitor is gaining traction. Another commenter opposed eliminating the references to, and functionality associated with, the certification criteria in § 170.315(g)(7) and (g)(9). Another commenter opposed any practical weakening of the API Conditions and Maintenance of Certification requirements, stating that third-party application developers already encounter substantial barriers despite the existing requirements.

Response. We thank commenters for their support of our proposals to make conforming revisions in § 170.404. We also appreciate the concerns raised by commenters regarding continued API transparency, availability, documentation, and accountability. The revisions we have finalized in § 170.404 remove expired transition provisions and obsolete cross-references; they do not reduce or otherwise alter the substantive requirements in § 170.404(a) or the continuing Maintenance of Certification requirements in § 170.404(b)(1) and (2). We disagree with the commenter who urged us not to retain the public service base URL publication requirement. We did not propose to eliminate the requirement in § 170.404(b)(2). We recognize that publication of service base URLs and related organization details may reveal customer relationships and permit inferences about a developer’s market presence. We also disagree that the conforming revision to the definition of “Certified API technology” in § 170.404(c) eliminates or requires discontinuation of the functionality associated with former § 170.315(g)(7) and (g)(9). the commenter that stated that third-party application developers should not face practices that impermissibly impede access to or use of certified API technology. We disagree, however, that the conforming revisions finalized in § 170.404 weaken the API Condition and Maintenance of Certification requirements in practice.

Accordingly, we have finalized these proposals as proposed. Because the transition dates in § 170.404(b)(2), (3), and (4) have passed, we have finalized a revision to the Maintenance of Certification requirements in § 170.404(b). We have finalized a revision in § 170.404(b)(2) to remove the date, and we have finalized the removal of paragraphs (b)(3) and (b)(4) entirely as they are outdated and, in the case of (b)(4) because it refers to certification criteria that we have removed. Removal of RWT Plan Submission and Reduced RWT Results Reporting Comments. Several commenters supported our proposal to remove the RWT plan submissions and reduce RWT results reporting, particularly by narrowing reporting obligations to FHIR-based API criteria. These commenters generally argued that the current RWT framework imposes significant administrative and compliance burdens while providing limited practical value. Commenters described the annual testing plans as largely formulistic endeavors that consume resources without meaningfully improving interoperability outcomes. These commenters also emphasized that focusing RWT oversight on key API certification criteria— such as § 170.315(g)(10) and the “prior authorization” API criteria—better aligns with ONC’s “FHIR-forward” strategy and concentrates on the interoperability functions most critical to realworld EHI exchange. A majority of commenters opposed our proposal to remove the RWT plan submissions and reduce RWT results reporting. Commenters asserted that our proposals would weaken developer accountability, interoperability assurance, and patient safety protections (e.g., identifying missing meds, incomplete data). Commenters stressed that publicly available RWT plans and results provide important transparency for healthcare organizations making purchasing and implementation decisions and help smaller providers, rural organizations, and public health agencies assess whether products will function effectively. Commenters argued that reducing

RWT reporting to API criteria was premature because there is not yet a fully mature and stable FHIR-based ecosystem. Commenters also argued that the proposed approach could leave important interoperability gaps undetected, such as with public health reporting.

Response. We thank commenters for expressing both their supporting and opposing viewpoints. We have finalized the removal of the RWT plan requirement and the reduced RWT results reporting as proposed. SVAP Comments. Response. We have finalized with modification our proposed revisions of SVAP. SVAP is a voluntary process that requires affirmative actions by developers to adopt and certify their health IT to a newer version of a standard or implementation specification that is included in certification criteria that are specified in RWT under § 170.405(a). Ultimately, through attestations under 45 CFR 170.406, a developer attests that their certified health IT is conformant to the “SVAP” version of the standard or implementation specification as part of their overall attestation to compliance with 45 CFR 170.405.

Except as follows, we have finalized as proposed our proposed modifications to SVAP. In consideration of the regulatory version updating and attestation processes outlined above and public comment noting that SVAP standards are identified via the CHPL for certified health IT, we have not finalized our proposal to require, as part of RWT results reporting, the identification of any SVAP updates to health IT certified to the non-API RWT certification criteria identified in § 170.405(a) (the non-(g) certification criteria). We believe sufficient assurances and

transparency will be provided by the aforementioned attestation and CHPL listings. This approach avoids redundancies and further reduces burden. We have, however, finalized our proposal that the RWT SVAP reporting requirements would not change for API-focused certification criteria identified as (g) criteria in § 170.405(a). For clarity, we have still finalized the proposed revision to § 170.405(b)(8) to cross-reference paragraph (a) of § 170.405 instead of paragraph (b). This will provide the availability of SVAP for all health IT certified to certification criteria identified in § 170.405(a), which supports advancing interoperability. All revisions will be effective upon the effective date of this final rule. As such, we will implement this approach for the attestation period that begins on January 1, 2027.

Technical Updates in Measure Specification Sheets

In the HTI-1 Final Rule (89 FR 1313), we stated that while the substantive requirements for each measure are defined in rulemaking, we determined that measure specification sheets are a logical and accessible method for the public to also view the technical specification that supports those requirements, and that this is consistent with the approach used by other HHS programs related to measure specifications (e.g., CMS Electronic Clinical Quality Measures (CMS eCQMs)). Additionally, we note that the measure specification sheets provide granular definitions and other information that may be helpful to operationalize the metrics, so they are implemented in a consistent manner across health IT developers.

We also stated that we intend to provide up-to-date measure specification sheets to assist with the regulated community’s understanding of the finalized measures and metric calculations (89 FR 1313). In January 2025, we provided developers of certified health IT updated measure specification sheets for certain measures finalized in the HTI-1 Final Rule, with the flexibility to implement either version 2 or version 4 of the measure specification sheets for the first year of reporting.55 We noted that this flexibility is only for the first year of reporting (until July 2027) and that we expect developers to comply with the most up to date version of the measure specification sheets provided at www.healthIT.gov in subsequent years.
