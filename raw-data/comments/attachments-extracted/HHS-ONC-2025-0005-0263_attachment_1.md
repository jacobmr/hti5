Modification of the Information Blocking Infeasibility Exception  
The proposed rule would modify the Infeasibility Exception under the Information Blocking 
regulations by removing the  "manner exception exhausted condition" , thereby limiting the 
circumstances under which an actor may deny a request for a specific manner of access, 
exchange, or use of EHI after offering alternative compliant manners.  
 
Under the current regulatory structure, actors may assert infeasibility where all available compliant 
manners of fulfilling a request under the Manner Exception have been exhausted and fulfillment 
remains technically or operationally infeasible. This provision recognizes the practical limitations 
that exist in real-world technical environments and provides important regulatory clarity when an 
actor lacks the capability to transmit EHI in the specific manner requested. 
 
In practice, our health system frequently receives atypical or highly specific requests to transmit 
data to third-party applications, portals, or other external platforms that may not be supported by 
our certified technology or that raise legitimate securi ty, authentication, or technical feasibility

concerns. For example, we may receive requests to send a patient’s full medical record to an 
external portal  or application, where the requested destination does not support standard 
exchange methods, does not align with available APIs, or cannot be authenticated in a manner 
consistent with our security controls. 
 
Additionally, in Minnesota, certain categories of care may be accessed by minors without parental 
consent under state law. EHR configurations are carefully structured to respect these legal 
frameworks while ensuring compliance obligations consistent with applicable feder al and state 
requirements. Accordingly, health systems must operationalize complex access configurations , 
which rely on technical limitations and controlled access pathways within our EHR to adequately 
balance federal information blocking and HIPAA Privacy  Rule requirements with state minor 
consent laws. The removal of the manner-exhausted condition creates uncertainty regarding how 
we can maintain these privacy-protective configurations if we are required to fulfill requests in a 
manner not supported by our systems. 
 
Without the ability to rely on the exhausted condition: 
• Providers may be required to manually evaluate and document a substantially greater 
volume of individualized requests; 
• Technical limitations that were previously recognized under the rule may no longer qualify 
as permissible grounds for declining a specific transmission method; 
• Organizations may face increased compliance risk when system architecture does not 
support the requested transmission format or endpoint; and 
• Privacy and security safeguards, particularly in sensitive contexts such as adolescent 
access controls, could be placed in tension with information blocking obligations. 
 
We remain committed to facilitating patient access to and exchange of electronic health 
information. However, the rule should continue to recognize that there are legitimate technical 
and operational limits to how data can be transmitted. Removing the manner exception exhausted 
condition risks creating ambiguity in situations where providers lack the technical cap ability to 
fulfill a request in the specific manner demanded, even when alternative compliant methods have 
been offered. 
 
We respectfully request that ASTP/ONC either retain the manner exception exhausted condition 
or, alternatively, provide clear regulatory guidance addressing how providers may respond when 
requested transmission methods exceed the technical capabilities of certified health IT or conflict 
with privacy-protective system configurations. 
 
 
Removal of Cybersecurity-Focused Certification Criteria

The proposed rule would remove numerous certification criteria within the Health IT Certification 
Program, including criteria that require developers to demonstrate specific privacy and security 
functionalities (e.g., authentication controls, audit logging capabilities,  and other technical 
safeguards designed to protect electronic protected health information (ePHI)). 
 
Although these certification criteria apply directly to developers, they serve an essential 
compliance-support function for providers. Importantly, this rule does not modify or reduce 
provider obligations under HIPAA Security and Privacy Rules, CMS Conditions of Participation, 
or any State privacy and breach notification laws.  Providers remain fully responsible for 
implementing “reasonable and appropriate” administrative, physical, and technical safeguards 
under HIPAA regulations. Removing these criteria would shift additional due diligence, 
compliance, and verification burdens onto providers.  
 
First, providers will no longer be able to rely on certification status as evidence that core technical 
safeguards are present in a product. Instead, health systems will need to independently assess 
whether vendors’ products include sufficient authenticati on controls, access management 
functionality, encryption capabilities, multifactor authentication, and audit logging mechanisms to 
satisfy HIPAA requirements. This often requires deeper technical validation, expanded security 
questionnaires, and more complex contractual protections. 
 
Second, HIPAA Security risk analysis obligations require us to assess the potential risks and 
vulnerabilities to ePHI within our systems. When certification criteria establish uniform technical 
expectations, providers can reasonably assume certain baseline capabilities are in place. Without 
those baseline requirements, each vendor product may differ in architecture and safeguards, 
requiring more granular, time-intensive risk assessments and technical testing. 
 
Third, procurement and vendor management processes would become more resource-intensive 
and burdensome. We may need to expand the scope of our teams tasked with reviewing security 
functionality of applications, c onduct additional independent penetration testing or third -party 
assessments, m onitor vendor updates more closely for changes in security functionality , and 
negotiate more detailed business associate agreement (BAA) provisions. These activities require 
significant operational and financial investment, particularly for large, regional health systems like 
Allina Health that manage numerous integrated technologies. 
 
We are also concerned that  removing these criteria may increase va riability across vendors. 
Certification criteria have historically promoted market -wide consistency in core safeguards. 
Eliminating these requirements risks fragmentation in technical capabilities, forcing providers to 
compare and reconcile disparate security architectures across products that are all still labeled 
“certified.” The removal of federally audited certification safeguards may complicate post-incident 
review and regulatory inquiries. In the event of a breach or security incident, providers may have

Removal of AI Model Card Certification Requirements 
The proposed rule would eliminate certification criteria requiring developers to provide “AI model 
cards” or similar transparency documentation for predictive decision support interventions and 
other AI-enabled functionality. These model cards were designed to include information such as 
model purpose and intended use , training data characteristics, performance metrics, limitations 
and known risks, and update or monitoring practices.  
 
AI Model Cards have functioned as a valuable offering of data transparency  and standardized 
documentation, which Allina Health has increasingly relied on when evaluating AI-enabled clinical 
decision support tools for safety, bias mitigation, and clinical appropriateness.  While developers 
create and control these models, providers remain accountable for clinical quality, patient safety, 
and potential downstream impacts of AI -supported decision -making. Certification -level 
transparency provides critical assurance that baselin e disclosures are complete, standardized, 
and subject to federal oversight. 
 
 Eliminating model card requirements without replacing them with an alternative transparency 
framework increases provider governance burden and liability exposure, particularly as AI 
becomes more deeply embedded in care delivery workflows.  Although intended to reduce 
developer burden, this proposal may slow provider adoption by requiring more extensive 
independent due diligence to validate algorithm performance and limitations.  
 
These requirements represent some of the only existing federal transparency guardrails for 
healthcare artificial intelligence. We respectfully urge ASTP/ONC to retain the clinical decision 
support transparency requirements or replace them with a clearly defined alternative framework. 
 
 
Varying Effective Dates 
The proposed rule includes varying effective dates across deregulatory provisions. Providers 
must coordinate system updates, vendor contracts, compliance programs, and risk assessments 
on defined timelines. Inconsistent effective dates may create uncertainty regarding w hich 
certification requirements remain operative and when compliance assumptions must be adjusted.

Alignment with CMS Promoting Interoperability and Value-Based Care Programs 
Certification policy does not operate independently from CMS program requirements, and 
changes to the Health IT Certification Program must be evaluated in the broader context of 
provider obligations under Medicare and Medicaid payment programs.  
 
Providers participating in CMS programs - including the Promoting Interoperability (PI) Programs 
under the Medicare and Medicaid EHR Incentive Programs , the Merit-based Incentive Payment 
System (MIPS) , and other Advanced Alternative Payment Models (APMs) and value -based 
purchasing initiatives  — must use Certified EHR Technology (CEHRT) to ensure successful 
participation. Providers are reliant on CEHRT capabilities to facilitate secure patient access to 
electronic health information , e xchange data with exter nal providers and networks , r eport 
electronic clinical quality measures, and maintain appropriate safeguards for protected health 
information.  
  
If certification standards are reduced while CMS program requirements remain unchanged, 
providers may face a structural misalignment. CEHRT may no longer be required to include 
certain baseline functionality or safeguards, yet providers will continue to be evaluated, scored, 
or financially penalized based on performance measures that depend on those capabilities. In 
addition, value -based care arrangements increasingly depend on timely, secure, and 
interoperable data exchange across settings of care. Variability in core certified capabilities could 
directly affect care coordination, quality reporting accuracy, and compliance with CMS  
participation requirements. 
 
Because CMS programs incorporate statutory and regulatory references to CEHRT, reduc ing 
certification baselines without coordinated CMS policy adjustment risks creating compliance 
ambiguity. Providers could be put in a position where they are technically using certified 
technology but must independently verify that it continues to support programmatic requirements 
for exchange, reporting, and security. Without a clear, well -articulated plan, the proposals risk 
placing und ue burden on providers in assessing  and verifying compliance responsibilities in 
programs that leverage or require CEHRT.  
 
We respectfully urge ASTP to work closely with CMS to ensure that any changes to certification 
criteria are coordinated with CMS program expectations and do not inadvertently increase 
operational burden or compliance risk for participating providers.

Deregulation Without a Defined Future Framework 
We recognize ASTP/ONC’s interest in modernizing and streamlining the Health IT Certification 
Program. However, the proposed rule removes or narrows several long -standing certification 
criteria without articulating a clearly defined replacement framework or transition plan. 
Certification standards  have historically served as foundational guardrails that promote 
consistency, interoperability, and security across the health IT marketplace . Eliminating those 
guardrails without describing the structure that will replace them creates regulatory uncertainty. 
 
Providers do not experience certification criteria as abstract developer requirements  rather they 
function as practical baselines that inform procurement decisions , risk management programs, 
compliance documentation and governance structures . W hen these criteria are removed , the 
operational and compliance responsibilities associated with those capabilities do not disappear . 
Instead, they shift to providers who must independently determine how to fill the resulting gaps 
while remaining fully accountable under HIPAA, information blocking regulations, CMS program 
requirements, state laws, and more. 
 
A phased modernization strategy that clearly identifies the intended future framework would allow 
providers and developers to align planning, investments, and compliance programs accordingly. 
In contrast , deregulatory changes implemented without a defined endpoint risk creating 
fragmentation in the marketplace and variability in technical capabilities across products that 
remain labeled as ‘certified’. This variability increases administrative burden, complicates vendor 
oversight, and may undermine the predictability that certification was designed to provide. 
 
If ASTP/ONC intends to pursue a restructured or more modernized certification model in future 
rulemaking, we respectfully request that the agency articulate that vision via notice-and-comment 
rulemaking prior to finalizing broad removals of existing safeguards. Maintaining core security and 
transparency protections during any transition period would help preserve market stability  and 
support provider’s ongoing compliance obligations.  
 
Importantly, in the event  of a breach occurring in the absence of baseline cybersecurity 
safeguards, patients and their sensitive health information bear the greatest risk of harm. We urge 
ASTP/ONC to evaluate its proposals with patient protection as a central consideration.  
 
 
Conclusion  
We support thoughtful modernization of the Health IT Certification Program. However, the 
proposed modification of the infeasibility exception, removal of cybersecurity-focused certification 
criteria, and elimination of AI model card transparency requiremen ts collectively represent a
