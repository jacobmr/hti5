Executive Summary 
We provide an overview of our specific comments with a focus on reducing burden, improving 
interoperability and protecting patient safety, as well as some overarching comments that apply to 
the approach to the HTI -5 NPRM as a whole . We note that with regard to  many of the proposals 
related to the Certification Program, the NPRM notes that there will be future proposals to replace 
those that are being significantly revised or removed but provides no tangible proposal to consider 
alongside the complete removal of current criteria and framework . As such,  we cannot 
appropriately evaluate and fully comment on the proposals without understanding the strategy and 
policy that ASTP/ONC plans to propose in a future HTI-6 rule. In some cases, we recommend that 
ASTP/ONC retains the current requirements and proposes changes at such time as an alternative 
is proposed so Oracle Health and other commenters can fully consider the proposals and not leave 
a gap while awaiting future rulemaking. 
Reducing Burden in the Health IT Certification Program 
Oracle Health applauds ASTP/ONC’s efforts to re-examine the program with a fresh perspective 
and propose specific reductions of and revisions to certification criteria to reduce burden. We agree 
that many of the se proposals represent a legitimate burden reduction for certified health IT 
developers without sacrificing the quality and integrity of the Certification Program, but we also 
believe there are significant opportunities to relieve unnecessary administrative burden via changes 
outside of the scope of Certification Program  criteria or C onditions and Maintenance of 
Certification that have not been explored in this rulemaking. We encourage ASTP/ONC to consider 
the following additional burden reduction efforts either via sub-regulatory pathways or in future 
regulations: 
1. Removal of mandatory disclosures requirements at 170.523(k) 
2. Simplifying quarterly attestation requirements at 170.523(m) 
3. Amending the inherited certification status surveillance policy 
4. Simplifying the overall certification testing process with a focus on “evidence -based 
attestation” 
Concerns with Proposals for Criteria Reduction 
While Oracle Health  fully supports burden reduction measures that simplify and streamline the 
Certification Program to allow time and investment to be spent on efforts with tangible value for 
our healthcare provider customers and their patients, we are highly concerned about the potential 
negative impacts on the quality , integrity, and value  of the Certification Program  that some 
proposed changes may carry. Most particularly, we are concerned about the potential for negative 
impacts on patient safety and the privacy and security of health information. 
Standardized interoperability is a key goal of the Certification Program; however, the value of the 
Certification Program to the industry goes beyond interoperability. As stated by ASTP/ONC in the

January 2010 interim final rule establishing the initial set of standards and criteria for the 
Certification Program, “we believe that there will be several benefits from this interim final rule. 
By adopting this initial set, the Secretary will set in motion what we believe will be an iterative 
process to further enhance the interoperability, functionality, utility, and security of health 
information technology and to support its meaningful use. The capabilities required by adopted 
certification criteria will help arm health care providers with tools to improve patient care, reduce 
medical errors and unnecessary tests.”1 
Similarly, in the September 2012 final rule for the 2014 Edition of the Certification Program, 
ASTP/ONC stated that  the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health 
(“HITECH”) Act that provided the statutory basis for the Certification Program  intended to 
“improve health care quality, safety, and efficiency through the promotion of health information 
technology (HIT) and the electronic exchange of health information.”2 
Over the past decade the Certification Program has become a source of confidence and assurance 
for healthcare providers that a system will support the basic needs and expectations of healthcare  
delivery and operations, and a harbinger of patient safety and privacy and security protections in 
health IT. It is critical that the evolution of the Certification Program does not sacrifice that value. 
One of the themes of the proposals for removal of existing criteria, and a viewpoint that has been 
reiterated across some industry stakeholders, is that the removal of criteria, such as the Privacy 
and Security criteria at 45 CFR 170.31 5(d), carries no risk because existing certified health IT 
developers are highly unlikely to remove necessary functions and protections from their software 
even without a requirement to maintain them. And while that may be true to a large extent, it is 
important to remember that these foundational requirements play a critical role in ensuring that all 
certified health IT meet a baseline for quality, safety, and privacy and security, particularly as the 
future of the program lends itself to expanding the scope of health IT developers who may seek 
certification.  
With that perspective in mind, we offer the following set of considerations that should be applied 
before removing a criterion: 
1. Are the requirements of the criterion aligned to the qualified EHR definition?3  
2. Are t he requirements duplicative of or redundant to interoperability -focused requirements 
under the Certification Program? For example, the Implantable Device List criterion proposed 
for removal in this NPRM is effectively duplicative of the Unique Device Identifier(s) for a 
patient’s implantable device(s) enforced through USCDI criteria.

“FHIR-Enabled Future” of the Health IT Certification Program 
Oracle Health strongly supports a “FHIR -enabled future” vision for the Certification Program as 
outlined in the NPRM. Modern RESTful standards-based APIs are essential to enabling real-time 
data exchange and facilitating improved care coordination, analytics, public health reporting, and 
overall delivery of high-quality care. And taking the step to advance the Certification Program 
with subsequent rulemaking to pick up where this deregulatory -focused rule leaves off will be of 
paramount importance to not leave the proverbial cupboard bare. 
Interoperability is critically important, as are patient safety, privacy and security, and data integrity. 
A Certification Program model designed for certification of APIs exclusively, particularly one that 
enables or facilitates uncontrolled third-party write access into the electronic health record (EHR), 
loses sight of the essential role that the EHR system behind the APIs plays in effective 
interoperability, patient safety, cybersecurity, and record integrity. The EHR i s not simply a data 
platform. It is a regulated clinical system of record designed with patient safety and data integrity 
at its core. FHIR APIs are the future of interoperability, but APIs alone do not make an EHR, and 
the Certification Program should reflect this. 
Furthermore, as RESTful HL7 FHIR-based APIs become the focus of the Certification Program, 
it is important not to move away from other interoperability elements, such as HL7 v 2 message-
based standards and HL7 CDA® document -based standards, prematurely when they  are still 
highly effective in their current state  for a number of purposes  and drive current interoperability 
and health data exchange in a meaningful way. 
Our comments to the HTI-5 NPRM, particularly those regarding the “FHIR-enabled future” vision 
for the Certification Program, emphasize the following principles: 
1. Retaining the critical role that the broader EHR system plays in effective interoperability, 
patient safety, privacy and security, and record integrity.  
2. Ensuring a responsible approach to expanding the Certification Program into API write 
requirements that ensures healthcare providers remain in control of their systems and data.  

Page 5 of 37 
 
 
3. Preserving and not prematurely or unnecessarily removing other forms of interoperability 
that remain effective without a suitable FHIR-based replacement. 
4. In pursuing inclusion of new FHIR -based API technologies  under the Certification 
Program, focus on introducing them through specific use -cases employing such 
technologies. 
5. Approaching the future of the Certification Program in a way that distributes the burden 
for advancing the full health IT ecosystem with equal responsibilities for all stakeholders, 
including patient-facing apps. This is particularly true as we presumably progress into more 
advanced and novel areas of FHIR -based API interoperability, such as electronic prior 
authorization (ePA) and write operations.  
To highlight this final point, currently, certified health IT developers shoulder most of the burden 
and cost in facilitating the advancement of the health IT ecosystem and are severely limited in the 
ability to re-distribute some of the burden and cost under the API Conditions and Maintenance of 
Certification requirements.  Other critical players do not have the same responsibilities as they 
operate outside the realm of the Certification Program and its requirements, without conformance 
testing on their side of the interoperability equation. 
This m odel is palatable in the current state , where the scope of API resources and related 
functionality is relatively small and mostly query-focused where the consistency across all sources 
can already drive adoption. But as that scope begins to expand beyond queries as part of the “FHIR-
enabled future” of the Certification Program, as already evidenced by the introduction of electronic 
prior authorization (ePA), it will exacerbate existing challenges.  
The 21 st Century Cures Act requires support for  APIs to access, exchange, and use health 
information without special effort , but that should not mean with no effort by some parties and 
extreme effort and cost for others. 
Information Blocking 
We are generally supportive of the proposals outlined by ASTP/ONC related to information 
blocking. However, we do have some concerns , especially with  the proposed updates to the 
Manner Exception Exhausted condition of the Infeasibility exception and the Manner exception 
itself.  
We believe the Manner Exception Exhausted condition can play an important role in ensuring that 
actors have some ability to remain protected from information blocking complaints when operating 
within the framework. ASTP/ONC is proposing to update language in this condition that we 
believe will lead to more confusion (changing “same” to “analogous”), and we remain concerned 
that the 10-day window afforded by the Infeasibility exception is not realistic. We suggest updates 
that we believe are fair and protect both sides of the access, exchan ge, and use of EHI request 
process.

i. Patient Demographics 
Oracle Health supports ASTP/ONC’s proposal s for  updating the criterion to realign with the 
scoping established via the March 21, 2025, enforcement discretion notice.4 Given that equivalent 
changes have already been established  under the U.S. Core Data for Interoperability (“USCDI”)  
through publication of USCDI v3.1, it is important for the Patient Demographics criterion to adopt 
equivalent changes to ensure consistency. 
We recommend that ASTP/ONC consider full removal of the criterion given that the presence of 
the corresponding USCDI data class could satisfy the need to account for certified health IT 
modules’ ability to manage patient demographics data if ASTP/ONC were to follow our

ix. Decision Support Interventions 
Oracle Health supports ASTP/ONC’s proposal to amend the scope of requirements under the 
Decision Support Interventions (DSI) criterion with some important caveats. Our experience with 
the DSI requirements, particularly those  focused on artificial intellige nce and machine learning 
(AI/ML) systems and models deployed in certified health IT systems (“predictive DSI”), has been 
an overall positive impact on the quality and transparency of AI/ML for the market. However, there 
are certainly some limitations and issues with the current construct of requirements.  
For example,  the imposition of these requirements on exclusively health AI/ML technology 
developers who also happen the be certified health IT developers has created a stark inconsistency 
in obligations and quality assurances between those developers certified by ASTP/ONC and others 
in the same market who are not. More specific to the requirements of the criterion itself, there are 
two key issues that we have encountered in our experience developing an d deploying these 
capabilities to our customers. First, the requirements at sub-paragraph (iii)(B) requiring developers 
to support selection of predictive DSI using any data expressed in the USCDI standard  has 
generated significant confusion for both certified health IT developers and our healthcare provider 
customers. To be effective, the requirement must cite a specific technical standard or methodology 
for how third -party predictive DSI should be enabled for “selection” by certified health IT 
developers’ customers.  
Second, the requirements at sub-paragraph (v) for supporting access and modification of source 
attributes for DSIs has proven to be an over-extension. Since deploying the functionality for end-
users to access and modify source attributes within their workflows in the EHR, we have tracked 
production activity across our customer base and observed that, on average, source attribute 
information is accessed only twice per month 11 at each customer organization, which can consist 
of hundreds or even thousands of individual system end -users. These are extremely low usage 
volumes and match the general feedback we have received from our customers that, while the 
source attribute information itself is highly valuable for assessing AI/ML systems and models for 
implementation, i n its current form it is not valuable for clinicians at the point of care. The 
information is simply too technical and detailed for clinicians to spend time reviewing whilst

xx. Safety Enhanced Design 
Oracle Health believes strongly that the Safety -Enhanced Design (SED) criterion in its current 
state must be revised in order to relieve what is currently a significant and unnecessary burden on 
certified health IT developers without meaningfully advancing patient safety outcomes. However, 
safety of health information technology is critically important to Oracle Health and our customers, 
and we are concerned that a full-scale removal of the criterion could have negative implications in 
that realm. Therefore, we are recommending an approach that revises the requirements to focus 
more on a system -wide vie w of safety and usability, while maintaining clear thresholds and 
standards for safety. 
The existing criterion requires usability testing and reporting artifacts that are prescriptive, 
narrowly scoped, and disconnected from established, risk -based safety engineering practices. As 
implemented, SED encourages a compliance -driven exercise rather than a comprehensive, 
evidence-based evaluation of software safety. This creates duplicative work, constrains appropriate 

Page 21 of 37 
 
 
methodological choices, and diverts resources  away from activities that more directly improve 
safety. 
We believe that usability evaluation of safety -critical health software is best conducted within 
established medical device usability engineering frameworks, such as those defined in IEC 62366-
1 and ISO 14971. These processes are specifically designed to identify hazardous use scenarios, 
evaluate use-related risks, verify the effectiveness of risk controls, and assess residual risk in a 
structured, traceable, and auditable manner. When software functi onality meets the definition of 
Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), these frameworks provide a mature, globally recognized 
approach to safety evaluation that is proportional to risk and grounded in human-factors science. 
By contrast, the SED criterion imposes a rigid and contrived set of testing and documentation 
expectations that do not align with modern usability engineering practice, particularly for complex, 
configurable enterprise systems. The criterion’s emphasis on a narrowly defined summative-style 
usability test and static reporting artifacts fails to account for iterative design, layered safety 
controls, and real-world clinical workflows. As a result, compliance with the criterion often yields 
limited incremental safety insight while adding substantial operational and documentation burden. 
As expressed above, Oracle Health recognizes and supports the policy goal of ensuring that 
certified health IT is designed with safety in mind. However, we believe that goal is better achieved 
by allowing organizations to apply established medical device and human-factors processes where 
appropriate, rather than maintaining a standalone certification criterion that is disconnected from 
those frameworks.  
Accordingly, we recommend ASTP/ONC take the following path in final rulemaking: 
1. Remove the requirement to conduct summative usability testing following NISTIR 7742 
standards for specific criteria and associated actions. 
2. Require that all certified HIT satisfy at least one  of the following three options for 
demonstrating adherence to industry standards and best practices for their user -facing 
software, as applicable: 
a. Demonstrate independently obtained ISO 14971 international medical device 
safety standards certification. 
b. In lieu of a formal certification, submit usability testing evidence of the developer’s 
choosing (either formative or summative) that indicates alignment with either ISO 
14971 or IEC 62366 -1 requirements in the developer’s software design processes 
and controls. 
c. Provide attestation to be reviewed and verified by the applicable ONC - ACB that 
the particular criteria for which  the certified health IT  module is obtaining 
certification to do not include any functions with user-facing aspects.

d. “FHIR-Enabled Future” of the Health IT Certification Program 
As expressed in the Executive Summary of this comment letter, Oracle Health strongly supports 
the concept behind a “FHIR-enabled future” vision for the Certification Program as outlined in the 
HTI-5 NPRM. However, while FHIR-based interoperability becomes a more direct focus, patient 
safety, privacy and security , and data integrity  must remain priorities. The following section 
outlines critical principles to adhere to, and we eagerly await the opportunity to assess and provide 
more explicit recommendations on proposals in the next rulemaking cycle  for the Certification 
Program. 
i. Retain the Critical Role of the Broader EHR System and Specialty Systems  
First and foremost, the transition to a more distinct focus on certification of interoperability 
elements (e.g., HL7 FHIR -based APIs and related capabilities) under the Certification Program 
must not lose sight of the critical role that the system behind those interoperability elements plays 
in both effective interoperability and the broader needs of healthcare delivery  – including 
assurances around patient safety in health IT. Put simply, the EHR system is more than just a set 

Page 29 of 37 
 
 
of APIs. It is an intelligent system of record that provides the ability to reliably document and store 
a patient’s health records in a manner that they can be effectively presented and interacted with by 
clinicians during a care episode and efficiently exchanged downstream.  
We believe that there is a simple manner by which the important role of the system can be 
maintained while still emphasizing interoperability elements as the focus of certification criteria. 
That is by imposing standards -based interoperability requirements  – such as adherence to HL7 
FHIR US Core Profiles – while also expressly mandating that health IT modules certifying to such 
criteria must also support “managing” the data in scope – such as USCDI data elements. Managing 
could be defined as “supporting the ability for the pertinent data to be stored locally and for the 
system’s end-users to view and  interact with the discrete USCDI data while documenting and 
managing their clinical, administrative, and/or financial workflows.”  Importantly, this would not 
mean that the system must be the place where the data originates from directly – the data could be 
sourced from upstream systems and integrated for management in the local system via HL7 v2 
interfaces, RESTful API write operations, or other means. But the system would need to support 
storage of and interaction with the discrete data locally. 
We recognize that what we describe as  managing the data is already a n implicit expectation for 
interoperability-focused criteria under the Certification Program today. However, as ASTP/ONC 
seeks to expand the Certification Program  to new entrants and advertise cer tification of APIs 
instead of EHRs, it is critical that this expectation be explicit as part of regulation (or clear sub -
regulatory guidance), and not just implicit. 
This concept may also help to solve another problem with the current structure of USCDI -based 
interoperability requirements under the Certification Program , which is the ir “all or nothing” 
nature. This has been an ongoing theme in public commentary from Oracle Health and the broader 
certified health IT developer community in recent years – and one we consider critical to the future 
success of the Certification Program.  
Put simply, the lack of recognition that different health IT, including specialty EHRs, need not 
support all data within USCDI has become a burden and a disincentive for some health IT to pursue 
certification and align with industry standards for data exchange. Interoperability is dependent on 
predictable, standards-based exchange, whether using HL7 FHIR, HL7 CDA C -CDA, HL7 v2 , 
NCPDP Script, X12 5010, or other agreed to content syntax in combination with appropriate 
transport methods. It involves at least two parties having varying roles in the interactions: 
sender/receiver, client/server, source/target. Certification in some f orm creates an environment 
where such interactions can be scaled nationally and reduce friction. Therefore, any requirements 
that reduce the ability of various health IT to participate in certification , so their interoperability 
partners have confidence and the ability to connect rapidly is problematic.   
In that context, we suggest that ASTP/ONC allow for health IT modules to certify to only the 
specific scope of USCDI data elements that are relevant for their particular user base or overall 
purpose – i.e., the scope of data they manage as we propose to define it above. The particular scope 

Page 30 of 37 
 
 
of USCDI data elements that a certified health IT module supports could be made transparent to 
the market via clear publication on the CHPL listing, and potential ly even a public disclosure on 
the developer’s website. This approach would enable specialty EHRs and related health IT to avoid 
investing in support for data and functionality that proves irrelevant for their user community and 
to be certified as providing the necessary standards and capabilities when interacting with EHRs, 
consumer health apps, and other health IT, while still ensuring that when healthcare providers 
purchase and implement a product certified for USCDI criteria it provides them with the fu ll, 
relevant spectrum of functionality critical to successful interoperability. 
Recognizing that there may also be a need to establish a common scope that every health IT module 
certified for USCDI criteria supports, an alternate approach may be to establi sh subsets of the 
USCDI that are the minimum requirement for different classifications of health IT – e.g., acute 
EHR vs. individual specialty-focused EHRs vs. laboratory information systems (LIS) vs. imaging 
systems, etc. This alternative is something that would necessitate significant further collaboration 
with industry stakeholders to establish those subsets and the corresponding minimally required 
USCDI data elements as part of rulemaking. 
ii. Ensuring a Responsible Approach to API Write Requirements 
Oracle Health supports, directionally, the need to consider HL7 FHIR-based API write operations 
into health IT by external parties. However, it is absolutely critical that this expansion is 
approached in the context of the many risks that the expansion from read-only to write operations 
entails.  
First, the security risks.  ASTP/ONC has acknowledged the distinct difference in security risks 
between read -only vs. write operations in previous rulemaking  citing the “heightene d risks 
associated with apps designed to “write” data to the EHR database or to run natively within the 
EHR's user interface” 22 in the 21 st Century Cures Act final rule . Thus, whereas current 
requirements under the API Conditions and Maintenance of Certification and Information 
Blocking effectively prohibit certified health IT developers from conducting “vetting” of apps for 
security purposes before enabling connections for read-only interactions, this must be approached 
differently for write operations  and recognize the correlation to obligations for covered entities 
and business associates under the HIPAA Security Rule to perform such vetting activities and – in 
most ca ses (e.g., where an entity is performing a function, such as amending a health care 
provider’s legal record) – enter into a business associate agreement (BAA) with the app developer. 
Second, the patient safety risks. The potential examples which could be c ited here are nearly 
endless. When disparate systems begin to record data directly to a patient’s record , that is then 
relied on to arm clinicians with the right details at the right time and to trigger the right insights, 
one mistake can unravel the entir e process and have potentially catastrophic effects. Medication 
ordering, sepsis detection and treatment, blood transfusion orders, chemotherapy and oncology

Third, the data integrity risks.  EHRs serve as the system of record for health care providers who 
use these tools to document care that they have delivered to patients and meet legal obligations

There is a distinct need to maintain the integrity of the patient’s medical record with clear

The bottom line is this: while incorporating requirements to support the technology of HL7 FHIR-

organizational operations in order to ensure patient safety, security and data integrity. We eagerly 
await specific proposals for this expansion in future rulemaking to provide more explicit 
recommendations to ASTP/ONC. 
iii. Thoughtfully Pursuing a Transition to FHIR 
While HL7 FHIR is undoubtedly the future, the fact is that not everything is FHIR-enabled today. 
There are many examples – such as ePrescribing via NCPDP standards, public health reporting via 
HL7 v2-based standards, and treatment-based exchange via HL7 CDA C-CDA documents – where 
the current non-HL7 FHIR exchange method is either effective in its current form or does not yet 
have a suitable HL7 FHIR-based replacement to warrant a transition. 
While there is very likely a future state where HL7 FHIR can replace all other standardized forms 
of exchange under the Certification Program, that day has not yet arrived. Accordingly, we urge 
ASTP/ONC to strongly consider the current state effectiveness of non-HL7 FHIR interoperability 
mechanisms and readiness of replacement HL7 FHIR-based mechanisms before pursuing any such 
shift. This will help to avoid a situation in the “FHIR-enabled future” wherein one step forward 
results in two steps backwards.

iv. Focusing on Use-Case Specific Requirements 
Finally, as ASTP/ONC seeks to expand HL7 FHIR-based API capabilities under the Certification 
Program, this must be pursued through incorporation of net-new capabilities with use-case specific 
requirements that, where possible, cite associated use-case-specific FHIR implementation guides. 
An exceptional example of this type of approach is the electronic prior authorization (ePA) criteria 
at 45 CFR 170.315(g)(31) and (g)(33) adopted in ASTP/ONC’s HTI -4 final rule. Those criteria 
incorporated HL7 CDS Hooks and HL7 FHIR Subscriptions, respectively, as pa rt of associated 
HL7 Da Vinci implementation guides for ePA. In so doing, those new capabilities are incorporated 
into health IT with a level of assurance that they will be actively used by healthcare providers who 
will immediately see their benefits and seek expanded opportunities to leverage them outside of 
ePA. This will organically push those capabilities forward and allow the market to expose and 
develop implementation guides for the most valuable use-cases that the Certification Program can 
then adopt as requirements moving forward. 
This is in stark contrast to adopting a base standard on its own without clear use-cases to illustrate 
immediate value for healthcare providers. An example where we have seen this type of approach 
become challenging for adoption is the HL7 FHIR Bulk Data Access standard that was 
incorporated into the Standardized API for Patient and Population Services criterion with 
ASTP/ONC’s 21 st Century Cures Act rulemaking. While HL7 FHIR Bulk Data  Access offers 
immense value and can serve a nearly endless scope of possible use-cases, we have observed that 
the lack of specific use -cases with cited implementation guides has resulted in slower ado ption. 
As an illustration, we have observed only fifteen (15) of our 1,000+ customers with the technology 
deployed having used it during the entirety of calendar year 2025.23 
While there are certainly other reasons that have likely contributed to the low utilization to-date, 
including a general lack of easily accessible applications supporting the standards for healthcare 
providers to partner with and challenges with effective establishment and maintenance of 
population groups for identified use-cases (e.g., all patients with a common health plan or common 
diagnosis, test result, etc.), the primary reason is the lack of focused use-cases that include specific 
guidance on how to use the standards, such as the HL7 PDex guidance and Blue Button guidance 
by CMS focused on payers  implementing FHIR Bulk Data Access . As more specific 
implementation guidance is developed for express use-cases we are confident that adoption will 
follow.
