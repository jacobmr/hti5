Comments from Wolters Kluwer on the HTI-5 Draft Rule

Below is Wolters Kluwer’s comments to the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy’s
(ASTP) proposed rule entitled Health Data, Technology and Interoperability: ASTP/ONC
Deregulatory Actions to Unleash Prosperity (RIN 0955-AA09). We appreciate the opportunity to
share our views.

As way of background, Wolters Kluwer is a leading global provider of clinical
technology and evidence-based solutions that assist effective decision-making and outcomes
across the healthcare continuum. Key solutions include UpToDate®, UpToDate® Lexidrug™,
UpToDate® Patient Engagement, Medi-Span®, Sentri7®, Lippincott® Solutions, Ovid®, and
Health Language®. Wolters Kluwer had annual revenue in 2025 of $7.2 billion.
Our comments focus on the draft rule’s proposed clarifications to the definitions of
“access” and “use” within the information-blocking provisions to include automation
technologies such as robotic process automation (i.e., bots) and autonomous artificial intelligence
systems (i.e., agentic AI). Specifically, the draft rule seeks to codify that “access” includes the
ability or means necessary to make electronic health information (EHI) available for exchange or
use, including by bots or agentic AI. Similarly, changes to the definition of “use” would be
codified to include the use of bots or agentic AI to “understand” and “act upon” exchanged EHI.  
We have serious concerns that formally expanding these definitions to include automated
means of access and use such as bots or agentic AI will raise significant legal and compliance
conflicts with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), the Trusted
Exchange Framework Common Agreement (TEFCA) and federal copyright law. Autonomous
access could also result in data integrity issues and patient harm if such tools malfunction. If
finalized as proposed, the draft rule may create legal liability for health-care organizations,
significantly undermine the trust framework that underpins nationwide interoperability, and put
patient safety at risk.

1. Automated AI Access to Health Data Blurs the Line Between “Use” and “Training”
   As ASTP is well aware, automated AI systems, including bots and agentic AI, can
   improve their performance simply by processing new data. Such systems may update internal
   states, embeddings, or heuristics as they handle EHI. If a bot or AI agent requests, receives, or
   routes EHI and becomes more effective as a result, this constitutes “training” in a functional
   sense.
   Under HIPAA, training a n AI solution on protected health information (PHI) is not a
   permitted use unless the activity is explicitly authorized in a Business Associate Agreement and
   performed solely for the covered entity’s operations. If an AI system improves its capabilities by
   handling PHI, even incidentally, the organization may inadvertently engage in an impermissible

secondary use. The proposed HTI-5 definition risks normalizing automated access without
addressing the underlying purpose-limitation requirements that govern PHI.  
2. HIPAA’s Minimum-Necessary and Safeguards Requirements Become Harder to Enforce  
HIPAA also requires that only the minimum necessary PHI be requested and that
safeguards prevent unauthorized use or disclosures. Automated agents may request broader
datasets than a human would, store or cache PHI in ways that are difficult to audit, or generate
embeddings that inadvertently retain PHI. If the AI system’s internal state changes after
processing PHI, the organization may be unable to demonstrate that no impermissible use
occurred. This uncertainty may trigger breach-notification obligations and expose organizations
to HIPAA enforcement actions.
Other potential HIPAA -related violations are likely if agentic AI are given unfettered
access and use of PHI . For example, a patient’s right to privacy under HIPAA , including the
rights to access, amend and receive an accounting of disclosures of their records, all of which
depend on a provider’s ability to track, locate and correct all instances of PHI. Autonomous AI
systems that retrieve, cache or generate derivate copies of PHI would make it difficult to honor
these rights. The HIPAA Security Rule ’s requirement for audit trail sufficiency would likewise
be compromised, with current audit systems unable to track agentic AI collection and use of PHI.  
Finally, it is also worth mentioning that many states have electronic medical record laws
pertaining to storage, retention periods, access, retrieval and offshore prohibitions that will be
difficult, if not impossible, to honor if bots create additional, uncontrolled copies of patient
medical records that cannot be reliably located or destroyed.  
3. Use of Automated Systems to Access and Use Health Data Likely Violates TEFCA’s
Exchange Purpose and Purpose-Fidelity Requirements
TEFCA requires that every request for data specify one of six “exchange purposes” and
that the data be used exclusively for that purpose. The use of bots or agentic AI to facilitate data
exchange complicates these requirements for several reasons. First, training or improving AI
solutions is not currently one of the six authorized exchange purposes. An AI system may
request more data than necessary, request data for mixed or ambiguous purposes, or internally
“learn” from the PHI it processes. If the declared purpose is “care coordination,” but the
system’s internal architecture updates based on the PH I it handles, the agent is performing a dual
use: coordination and model improvement. This violates TEFCA’s purpose-fidelity requirement
even if the organization did not intend the secondary use. The draft rule does not address how
automated systems will certify or constrain their use of EHI to a single, permitted purpose, nor
how organizations will demonstrate compliance when internal model behavior is opaque or
adaptive.

4. Automated Systems that Access and Use Protected Content May Violate Copyright Laws
   As drafted, the proposal to allow autonomous AI agents to access and use PHI without
   limitation seems to equate these tools with human access and use. This is very concerning for
   medical content creators. An individual clinician’s access and use of copyright-protected content
   is limited and contextual, while bot-driven access could enable bulk copying, storage, indexing,
   AI model training, and creation of derivative works. If finalized, we fear the proposal will erode
   copyright and intellectual property protections and increase the likelihood of content misuse
   outside the scope of arms-length, commercial license agreements.  
   Clinical notes, care plans and guidelines, decision support recommendations and other
   narrative components of EHI often contain copyrighted material. If such material is collected and
   subsequently used by autonomous AI, it raises several possible claims of copyright infringement.  
   For example, if an automated AI system ingests such content and uses it to improve its
   performance, that would likely be an infringement. Though developed by federal courts to
   address the use of computers, the RAM Copy Doctrine implies that a machine, or in the case of
   AI, a bot, must make a momentary copy, potentially constituting an additional infringement.
   Bots can also derive patterns from copied material, which would also be an infringement. Courts
   have historically been skeptical of defendants who acquire copyrighted material through
   automated means combined with misrepresentation or over-collection. Given that the collector
   and user of such data is an automated system and not a human, infringement could well occur on
   a broad scale, impacting multiple and varied types of copyrighted content.  
   We appreciate that there is a Licensing Exception to the Information Blocking rule that
   affords some protection for copyright holders, but the draft rule does not address how
   organizations can detect and manage copyright risk when automated systems may ingest
   protected content, and at scale, as part of routine data access.
5. The Draft Rule’s Proposal to Grant Automated Systems the Ability to Access and Use
   Personal Health Data “Without Limitations” Seemingly Invites Violations of Existing Law
   Our concerns outlined above are heightened by the draft rule’s use of the phrase “ without
   limitation” when proposing to include autonomous AI systems in the definition of “access” and
   “use.” It is unclear from the proposed rule whether this lack of limits pertains to the number of
   bots or AI agents that may access a given data set, the amount of data that may be obtained or the
   range of uses these automated systems might apply to the data. Regardless of whether it pertains
   to one, two or all of these aspects, the use of the phrase “without limitation” fundamentally
   conflicts with federal privacy and copyright laws as well as the trusted exchange framework
   ASTP has worked so hard to implement and scale.  
   Agentic AI systems might also operate across mixed‑context data environments in which
   the same data element—such as a name or date of birth —may constitute PHI in one workflow
   but not in another. Bots will almost certainly not recognize or preserve these legal distinctions,
   and their cross‑task memory, caching, and inference capabilities can easily merge or repurpose
   identifiers across contexts. This creates a substantial risk that non‑PHI consumer‑generated data

will be inadvertently linked to PHI, or that PHI will be exported into non‑HIPAA‑regulated
systems, effectively collapsing the legal boundaries that HIPAA and TEFCA rely upon.  
The phrase “without limitation” is not only permissive, it invites and encourages
violations of: HIPAA’s rules on purpose-specific permissible uses, minimum-necessary
constraints and prohibitions on secondary use without authorization; TEFCA’s exchange purpose
and purpose-fidelity requirements; and federal copyright laws prohibiting infringement of
protected content.  
6. Granting Automated Systems Access to PHI Without Limitations Will Create Significant
Risks to Patient Safety
Apart from the varied conflicts with existing laws and contracts, the draft rule’s proposal
to enable agentic AI to access and use PHI without limitation introduces significant patient safety
risks. There are numerous ways an autonomous agent might malfunction while accessing and
collecting PHI. Bots might misread the intent of a query, pull the wrong patient’s records or
combine data from unrelated patients. Agentic solutions are also vulnerable to hallucination,
which could result in fabrication of patients, encounters, lab results or clinical notes. Retrieved
PHI might be re-routed to the wrong internal system or external endpoint, or mistakenly
attributed to the wrong patient or encounter. Suboptimal copying of records or workflows could
easily miss important nuances and edge cases.  
Such malfunctions could corrupt patient data, possibly at scale if hundreds or thousands
of patient records are collected, leading to systemic data‑integrity failures that propagate across
clinical workflows. Care coordination, care management, diagnosis and clinical decision‑making
depend on accurate and reliable information, so even a brief bot malfunction could lead to
erroneous CDS outputs, inappropriate treatment decisions, and direct patient harm. Malicious
actors could potentially introduce exploits that could lead to various security risks. The draft rule
contains no safeguards to account for these harms from autonomous systems. Moreover, the
Information Blocking rule’s Preventing Harm Exception could be effectively neutralized as such
malfunctioning agents are accorded access and use ‘without limitation.’  
7. Proposed Weakening of the Manner Exception Exhausted Condition Will Create New
Exposure to Autonomous AI Risks  
The proposed revisions to the Manner Exception Exhausted Condition of the Infeasibility
Exception will also make it substantially harder for providers, HIEs, and developers to refuse
health data requests from high‑risk autonomous AI. These risks are amplified by ASTP’s
proposal to remove the phrase “similarly situated,” which currently allows actors to distinguish
between human requestors and automated systems whose access patterns, security risks and
operational capabilities are fundamentally different. El iminating this phrase would compel
stakeholders to support manners of autonomous access that are potentially unsafe, while
exposing them to significant regulatory and legal liability for any resulting privacy, security,
copyright and patient‑safety failures.

Conclusion
In sum, the draft proposal to facilitate AI-driven data exchange not only lacks safeguards
to ensure that automated access does not inadvertently violate HIPAA, TEFCA, or copyright
law, it actively invites such violations by use of the phrase “without limitation.” Overriding
codified statutes enacted by Congress such as HIPAA and federal copyright protections falls far
outside ASTP’s regulatory authority and would almost certainly be invalidated by federal courts
if challenged. Moreover, creating direct conflicts with provisions of TEFCA’s common
agreement threatens to undermine the integrity and operability of the national exchange
framework.  
The proposal also does not account for autonomous systems that might malfunction or
cause malicious harm, which, in turn, could result in data integrity issues and threats to patient
safety. Given these multiple infirmities, were ASTP to finalize autonomous access and use as
proposed, we anticipate that stakeholders will be reluctant to deploy autonomous agents for fear
of non-compliance with existing law and/or violation of existing contracts, thereby hindering the
Trump Administration’s effort to spur interoperability and health data exchange. We therefore
urge ASTP to not finalize codification to the definitions of “access” and “use” to encompass use
of bots or agentic AI systems.  
In light of the complexity of the issues outlined above, and the lack of any proposals to
address these shortcomings in the proposed rule, new rulemaking will be necessary to allow
public feedback on proposed fixes for issues such as drawing a bright line between “use” and
“training,” preserving TEFCA’s exchange purpose and purpose-fidelity requirements, accounting
for autonomous agents that could malfunction, strengthening the Licensing, Preventing Harm
and Manner Exceptions to the Information Blocking rule, and establishing safeguards against
inappropriate secondary use and copyright infringement.
