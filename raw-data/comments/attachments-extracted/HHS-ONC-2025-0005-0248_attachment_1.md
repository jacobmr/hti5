  HTI-5 Proposals The CARIN Alliance supports ASTP/ONC’s efforts to move the EHR Certification Program into a new period with less regulatory burden. From a patient, consumer, and caregiver perspective, we need to improve both what information is shared and how that information is shared. We believe that patients and consumers can be supported in making better care decisions, better health decisions, and better purchasing decisions if they have the information they need, rendered by tools that are useful to them. The de-regulation within the CEHRT program, coupled with future action focused on increasing innovation, choice, and timeliness of exchange, can reduce billions of dollars in wasted private sector administrative spending, significant burden on providers, people, and plans, and eliminate needless regulatory headaches.  We support proposals within HTI-5 to: • Remove 34 certification criteria while revising seven others.  o The elimination of more than 50 percent of requirements would significantly reduce compliance burden and streamline the ONC Health IT Certification Program. • Adopt USCDI v3.1 as the required interoperability standard. o Reduction in C-CDA scope to align with USCDI v3.1 and eliminate duplicative content requirements. • Support the future of FHIR adoption: o “We propose to reduce the scope of the criterion to focus on enabling the receipt of a Consolidated CDA (C-CDA) document as a way to position this criterion for a future evolution to receipt of FHIR-formatted data.” o Revisions to terms and definitions rendered unnecessary by removed criteria. o Deregulatory changes to conditions and maintenance of certification, including assurances, APIs, real-world testing, attestations, and insights reporting. • Provide clarity around Information Blocking: o We support proposals that will provide additional clarity to Information Blocking Actors. We support the clarity offered around the definitions of “access” and “use,” revised exceptions, and the TEFCA-related exception. o We continue to advance our call to HHS, ASTP/ONC, CMS, and OIG to finalize additional regulations outlining appropriate disincentives for regulated provider actors. o We also continue to call on HHS, ASTP/ONC, CMS, and OIG to appropriately pursue all necessary Information Blocking complaints, prioritizing complaints made by patients and the consumer-facing tools they use. While CARIN members also have concerns with “contracts of adhesion,” including the disparate bargaining power that can exist between purchasers and offerors of certain products, the power a vendor may exert over activities of the purchaser, and other, “inherent power inequity between EHR developers and various types of requestors" as ASTP/ONC notes in the preamble, we are concerned that the language would undermine other, important activities within health care that do not establish disparate power dynamics or create an expectation of information blocking based on agreement type or vendor/purchaser relationship. In sum, we support ONC’s push to make sure providers have other options beyond their EHRs to use, store, and share their data with patients and others.    Future Rulemaking CARIN appreciates this note from the proposed rule, "We intend to sharpen the Certification Program’s future focus to prioritize FHIR-based APIs that: (1) enhance automation and API performance; (2) move beyond read-only interactions; and (3) expand the scope of data available to support clinical efficiency, patient-centered care, and timely reporting (e.g., public health, quality, government programs) use cases."   Our members believe that there is the dramatic potential to improve FHIR API performance and connectivity, require systems to both send and receive FHIR API data, and other new requirements that will enable true internet-based technology performance of health care data exchange. These changes would enable health systems to rely less on their EHR to send clinical data and instead allow them to use their own cloud-based systems and solutions which will enhance performance, timeliness, and dramatically lower overall costs. While we applaud the efforts of ASTP/ONC and CMS to advance the nation’s FHIR® interoperability roadmap with the new API mandates previous HTI rules, CMS 9115F, and CMS-0057F, there is still much to do. An additional note and recommendation: o A number of EHR software vendors “ship” certified (g)(10) APIs to their customers, but require their customers to complete a number of steps to activate these APIs. These steps are not required to authorize individual app developers or applications, but to enable connections for all app developers. An emerging pattern is for these EHR vendors to take a hands-off approach with helping/making these customers complete these activation steps. Instead, they tell app developers that it is not their responsibility to make regulatory compliance decisions on their customers’ behalf, even though many small practice groups reasonably expect their EHR vendor to support their compliance obligations. Or they tell the app developers they don’t have bandwidth to intercede with a broad customer communication reminding them of the activation steps, and why it matters for these steps to be completed. o Instead, these EHR vendors tell app developers to contact their own customers, to explain the EHR vendor’s activation process. EHR customers are understandably skeptical of following the instruction from the “cold call” of an app developer with whom they do not have a contracted relationship. These customers can be forgiven for thinking that these outreaches are either a veiled sales call, or a phishing attempt. For these reasons, the approach of making an app developer contact individual practice groups is an example of a process imposed by some Certified API Developers that introduces unreasonable “special effort” for its customers and for third-party app developers, and should be called out and eliminated because they interfere with patients’ timely access to their health information through any app besides the EHR vendors’ patient portal. o These practices add manual effort and have a foreseeably low probability of success, compared to efforts that a Certified API Developer could be making directly with its own customers. Cynically, these processes are not being cross-walked to attestations that their customers are making to earn points for providing Patients Electronic Access to Their Health Information under the Promoting Interoperability Performance category of the Medicare Quality Payment Program. o We strongly encourage ASTP/ONC to consider requiring certified HIT developers to “ship” their certified (g)(10) APIs with “default sharing on,” avoiding the disparate requirements of developers to have their customers activate the APIs.

Application registration and onboarding The API Conditions and Maintenance of Certification at 45 CFR 170.404 and the Content and Manner Exception of the Information Blocking Rule at 45 CFR 171.300 et seq. detail the minimum expectations of support that EHR software vendors are required to give third-party app developers that wish to register and connect their applications to certified patient access APIs.  The compliance expectation is straightforward: “A Certified API Developer must publish APIs and allow electronic health information from such technology to be accessed, exchanged, and used without special effort through the use of APIs or successor technology or standards, as provided for under applicable law, including providing access to all data elements of a patient's electronic health record to the extent permissible under applicable privacy laws” (45 CFR 170.404(a)(1)).   Certified API Developers are also required to provide all support and other services reasonably necessary to enable the effective development, deployment, and use of certified API technology by API Information Sources and API Users in production environments (45 CFR 170.404(a)(4)(iii)). The reality is much less straightforward and does in fact require significant effort on the part of third-party app developers.   Right now, policymakers have little awareness of these challenges, and there are few mechanisms within existing regulatory frameworks to hold Certified API Developers or their customers accountable for noncompliance, short of submitting an information blocking complaint. By shedding light on these challenges, we hope ASTP will take steps to encourage Certified API Developers to comply with the word and spirit of their interoperability compliance obligations in support of patient access to their information through their Certified API Technology, and work with its federal policy partners including CMS and the HHS OIG to draw awareness to health care providers participating in Medicare programs about their corresponding compliance obligations. Ongoing API support The Certification Criteria for Patient Access APIs at 45 CFR 170.315(g)(10), and the API Conditions and Maintenance of Certification at 45 CFR 170.404 detail the minimum data, format, transport and technical conformance requirements for certified APIs, including all necessary technical documentation, and the requirements for offering adequate support and related services to their customers and third-party app developers.  Failing to meet the requirements of Section 170.404 is indicative of noncompliance with the Promoting Interoperability Certification Program, due to the Cures Act mandate for Certified API Developer to publish conformant patient access APIs and allow electronic health information from such technology to be accessed, exchanged, and used without special effort to the extent permissible under applicable privacy laws. However, as in the other areas discussed above, various limitations with current regulations or gaps in uniform compliance with those regulations have created challenges for consumer-facing applications and the patients they serve. These include nonconformant APIs:  • Some certified patient access APIs do not support retrieval of data through “public” apps.  • Some certified patient access APIs do not support retrieval of data through browser-based applications. • Some Certified API Developers impose significantly greater burdens on third-party app developers of “public apps” than developers of “confidential apps.” • The certified patient access API for at least one Certified API Developer requires a patient to enter a unique customer identifier, in addition to their patient login credentials. • Certified API Developers don’t follow U.S. Core Data IG for all USCDI parameters.  • An aggravating factor of implementing a nonconforming API is when patient access API endpoints are maintained locally by customers. • The OAuth workflow for some certified patient access APIs require different login credentials than the ones issued to patients for their patient portal account. • The sandbox API conforms to regulatory standards and published documentation, but the customer-specific API endpoints do not.

API Certification and Performance Within the standards discussion, we believe a special discussion on API conformance is needed. TEFCA works perfectly and every provider and payer in the country is connected, even with record location services (RLS) the current CEHRT infrastructure would crumble because the systems are not designed to be high-volume, data exchange platforms. As such, each of the APIs that are certified need to have performance standards attached to them to better enable nationwide data exchange. Some of our partners in the EHR space are leading the way on API conformance and support. Examples of their feedback on API functionality are included here, illustrating how the private sector is leading on this issue and how ASTP/ONC might consider conformance verification as part of new reporting or certification protocols. The performance of APIs can be very complex because APIs can be used in so many ways. From a simple read on a single resource to bulk data for a massive group or population. To properly determine performance, you will need to break reporting or response times and payloads into categories. Some potential categories include single resource read, search with no operations to return the first page, search with operations to return the first page, API writes, $everything, and Bulk Data. These may need to be broken down further because construction architecture and how much data is being asked have a large impact on performance and responses. Here are the proposed averages for each category listed.  • Single resource read - <1 second • Search, no operations, returns the first page - <2 seconds • Search, operations, returns the first page - <3.5 seconds • API writes - <5 seconds

Other areas that will advance consumer access to information and the usability of that information As previously noted in the Leavitt Partners’ White Paper, “Kill the Clipboard,” we again call on ASTP/ONC to align the CERHT program to interoperability principles, including: • ASTP/ONC should change the definition of CEHRT (Certified Electronic Health Record technology) to “API CEHRT” (Application Programming Interface Certified Electronic Health Retrieval Technology). o The ONC is able to redefine API CEHRT based on the original definition in the HITECH Act, which includes the adoption of standards in Section 3004 and defines health information technology as inclusive all of the “hardware, software, . . . or packaged solutions sold as services that are designed for or support the use by health care entities or patients for the electronic creation, maintenance, access, or exchange of health information.”  o This definition would include EHRs, payer systems, API vendors, and cloud-based solutions. • API CEHRT should focus exclusively on certifying the HL7 FHIR APIs that providers and payers send and receive and no longer certify the functionality within the EHRs or EDI X12 transactions. • CMS should then point to the new definition of API CEHRT in their regulations to ensure consistency between providers and payers. • The APIs on ONC’s Inferno Test Kit website should be supported by payers and providers and implemented within the next two years. • Immediately publicly Publish the TEFCA FHIR API endpoint directory, which includes the recommended data elements suggested on the HL7 website, which lists the technical contact information (email and phone) and developer portal information to resolve any connectivity issues, thus streamlining registration and onboarding. This could be required under an accreditation program (such as NCQA Health Plan Accreditation). • Publish all regulated FHIR API endpoints in a publicly available location, regardless of whether those endpoints exchange data with a proprietary EHR or payer solution or not. Options must be made available to providers and health plans to make the APIs available in ways that are customized to that organization’s unique business needs.

TEFCA We appreciate what ASTP/ONC has done to advance TEFCA. We continue to believe that additional changes and updates are needed. These will help advance Individual Access Services use cases and other contemplated exchange under the program: • Eliminate fees for TEFCA-related data exchange for all participants including health plans, providers, consumers, public health, and disability benefits. Fees should reside solely at the QHIN-participant level where they can discuss the value of joining the specific QHIN.  TEFCA exchange itself should feel like “infrastructure,” not a metered utility. • Diversify the governance structure within TEFCA to ensure private and public sector participation from a cross section of stakeholders (payer, providers, patients, etc.) who are both participating and not participating in TEFCA to reduce potential conflicts of interest. We need far more stakeholders representing consumer, public health, and health plan representation (even if they aren’t participating in TEFCA) on the TEFCA governance structure to better define the use cases they are looking to implement. The current governance structure is far too provider and QHIN heavy. Organizations who participate in the governance structure should also be required to disclose their revenue models to indicate how they would benefit financially from decisions they would need to make. • Leverage industry working groups (e.g., HL7, FHIR accelerator programs, etc.) to inform the technology recommendations adopted by TEFCA not solely independent contractors or new invite-only workgroups. • Formalize and publicize voting on specific technology and process recommendations using Robert’s Rules of Order within the TEFCA Governance Process. • QHINs that offer other technical products, like EHR technology for example, should create a pathway for data from other QHINs to be incorporated into those products at little cost. This way, participants can confidently select the best QHIN for their needs, which would incentivize innovation. • Support and fund state-based FHIR early adopter projects that include multiple payers and multiple providers to facilitate the implementation of the CMS-0057-F rule (e.g., OneUtah Digital Health early adopter project is the most advanced example) using a TEFCA-based approach. This should not limit other early adopter pilots including point-to-point FHIR based transactions. Again, we appreciate all that ASTP/ONC has done to advance TEFCA, both in rulemaking and through RCE functions. We encourage ASTP/ONC to continue in those efforts, with these recommendations in mind. Data Quality: We encourage ASTP/ONC to look for ways to incorporate the PIQI Framework, in conjunction with the Inferno recommendation included above. Patient Information Quality Improvement (PIQI) is an emerging open framework for evaluating the quality of electronic patient data. It aims to enhance the usability of shared patient information by ensuring it meets specific criteria for accuracy, conformity, availability, and plausibility. PIQI assesses data against a standard, such as USCDI v3, generates a scorecard, and provides insights into issues affecting the quality score. This feedback enables data sources to make necessary adjustments to meet quality requirements and supports accuracy of records for patient use.  Digital Identity The use of digital identities is advancing, both within and outside of health care. The use of digital identities has broad application for consumers, providers, and others who engage with health data. We encourage ASTP/ONC to work towards policies that both enable and incentivize the use digital identity standards across the ecosystem.   Our digital identity recommendations include:  • Require that any system used for registration and login to access sensitive data on any health care network follow the NIST 800-63-3 digital identity guidelines for identity assurance level 2 (IAL2) and authenticator assurance level 2 (AAL2) for patient and provider identity and authentication. o This can be done by using a vendor on the Kantara certified vendor list or by using vendors that provide those services today as part of an organization’s user access management onboarding processes inside the organization. • Consumer identity for access to health care portals, personal health care applications, health care services (such as digital visits, condition management, and health devices) shall use open standards and support OpenID Connect. These services also shall support Identity Federation and shall accept a NIST 800-63-3 IAL2 or higher certified identity provider as valid for a consumer to access their data and to create a login session using OpenID Connect, with data provided via an openly provided API. • Access to identity federation and open data APIs shall not involve onerous approval or anticompetitive releases and shall not incur undue costs beyond the base costs of operating the system. • Adopt Open ID Federation or UDAP Tiered OAuth protocols to ensure all systems can accept an identity credential.
