Dear Dr. Keane,
ADVION, representing skilled nursing facilities, inpatient rehabilitation facilities, long-term care
hospitals, home health agencies, and the specialized health IT vendors that serve them, appreciates the
opportunity to comment on the HTI-5 proposed rule. Our members provide critical post-acute and long-
term care services to patients with high medical and social complexity, and we depend heavily on
external certified health IT systems-hospital and physician EHRs, health information exchanges,
clearinghouses, and TEFCA participants-for seamless transitions of care and effective care management.
Historically, the LTPAC sector was largely excluded from the federal incentive funding made available
under the HITECH Act, which accelerated certified EHR adoption in hospitals and physician practices but
did not provide comparable support for post‑acute and long‑term care providers. As a result, our sector
enters the HTI‑5 era with significantly less subsidized infrastructure and far more fragmented health IT
capabilities than other parts of the healthcare system.
We commend ASTP/ONC's deregulatory intent to reduce administrative burden, eliminate outdated
requirements, and create space for innovation. However, we write to emphasize that these important
goals must not inadvertently reduce functional interoperability for the vulnerable patient populations
we serve. This letter provides conditional support for many HTI-5 proposals while highlighting areas
where clarification, monitoring, or additional safeguards are essential to protect LTPAC care
coordination capabilities.

1. SUPPORT WITH CONDITIONS: DEREGULATORY ACTIONS IN PART 170
   Concerns Regarding Removal of Privacy and Security Criteria (§ 170.315(d)(1), (d)(2), (d)(3), (d)(7), and
   (d)(8)).
   ADVION has significant concerns regarding the proposed removal of foundational privacy and security
   certification criteria in § 170.315(d), specifically:
   •(d)(1) Authentication, Access Control, and Authorization
   •(d)(2) Auditable Events and Tamper-Resistance
   •(d)(3) Audit Report(s)
   •(d)(7) End-User Device Encryption
   •(d)(8) Integrity
   While we recognize the agency’s deregulatory intent to reduce duplicative testing and administrative
   burden, these provisions represent core safeguards that protect patient data, ensure legal defensibility,
   and maintain trust in certified health IT systems, particularly in post-acute and long-term care (LTPAC)
   settings.
   Clinical and Legal Risk in LTPAC Settings
   LTPAC providers operate in environments where:
   •Multiple disciplines document within shared records;
   •External data is frequently incorporated from hospitals and physician practices;
   •Therapy providers and ancillary vendors may require bidirectional access; and
   •Documentation is routinely scrutinized in survey, audit, and litigation contexts.
   The removal of authentication controls, audit log functionality, and data integrity requirements raises
   concern that newly certified products may enter the market without:
   •Comprehensive role-based access controls,
   •Robust audit trail functionality capable of distinguishing user actions,
   •Tamper-resistant logging mechanisms,
   •Clear integrity safeguards for exchanged or AI-generated data,
   •Encryption protections for end-user devices.
   These are not peripheral capabilities; they are foundational to patient safety, HIPAA compliance, state
   survey readiness, and provider liability protection.
   Intersection with Expanded AI and Automated Access

If certification no longer requires:
•Reliable audit trail documentation,
•Integrity controls,
•End-user device encryption, and
•Robust authentication standards
While simultaneously expanding automated access pathways, the result could be systems that permit
large-scale data access without sufficient traceability, accountability, or governance protections.
For LTPAC providers, who often rely on external EHR platforms and integration vendors, weakened
baseline certification standards may create unacceptable exposure to cybersecurity threats, audit
deficiencies, and legal risk.
The Barrier-to-Entry and Safety Gap Concern
ADVION is concerned that lowering certification requirements in these areas may unintentionally create
a two-tier market:
•Established vendors may voluntarily maintain strong security capabilities; however,
•New entrants may design products that meet minimum certification requirements while
omitting robust authentication, audit, and integrity safeguards.
The rapid pace at which new systems, particularly AI-enabled platforms, can now be developed and
brought to market heightens this risk. Removing these criteria from certification does not prohibit
vendors from maintaining them, but it lowers the regulatory floor in a way that may disproportionately
impact small practices, rural providers, and LTPAC organizations with limited IT capacity to
independently vet security architecture.
Our Request
ADVION respectfully urges ASTP/ONC to:
•Closely monitor post-HTI-5 certification outcomes to determine whether removal of §
170.315(d)(1), (d)(2), (d)(3), (d)(7), and (d)(8) results in measurable degradation of
authentication, auditability, encryption, or integrity protections in newly certified products.
•Establish alternative validation mechanisms such as strengthened conformance testing or
targeted oversight to ensure certified products continue to meet baseline privacy and security
expectations.

ADVION acknowledges that HTI‑5’s deregulatory moves-including descope of Real‑World Testing in §
170.405, scaled‑back Insights measures in § 170.407, and administrative simplifications for ONC‑ACBs-
reduce barriers for specialized LTPAC vendors to pursue certification.
This context is especially important given that LTPAC providers did not benefit from the initial wave of
HITECH incentive payments that helped finance certified EHR adoption in acute and ambulatory care.
The absence of that foundational investment continues to shape LTPAC vendors’ and providers’ ability
to absorb new regulatory and technical requirements today.
This is a positive step, as LTPAC‑focused vendors have historically faced disproportionate burden relative
to their market size and resources when pursuing ONC certification.
The Need for Validation Without Overwhelming Burden
However, ADVION recognizes an important tension: while we support reducing administrative burden,
we are concerned about maintaining trust in certified products.
ADVION questions whether the marketplace adequately scrutinizes real‑world testing results. In
practice, those published results are primarily reviewed by marketing professionals and occasional
competitors. If the industry previously struggled to trust long‑standing certification participants, there is
reason to question whether the marketplace will reliably vet new entrants that have been in the
program only a short time.
ADVION understands the need and rationale for pulling back on certain requirements, but we are
concerned about how the agency will prevent new instances of fraudulent or low‑quality certification,

5. AUDIT TRAIL AND CLINICAL SAFETY FUNDAMENTALS
   While not a primary focus of HTI‑5, ADVION is concerned about the removal of audit trail and other
   foundational clinical safety requirements from certification criteria.
   ADVION believes that clinicians would be reluctant to use an EHR that lacks an audit trail, because they
   would not want to appear in a court of law without that protection. If a provider purchases an EHR
   without such capabilities, that decision rests with them-but smaller practices and therapy providers may
   not know to ask those questions.
   We recognize that HTI‑5’s deregulation does not prohibit vendors from maintaining these features.
   However, we share the concern that lowering the floor may result in products marketed to LTPAC
   settings that lack:
   •Comprehensive audit trails.
   •User authentication and authorization controls.
   •Data integrity safeguards.
   These are not merely “nice to have” features-they are essential for regulatory compliance (including
   state survey requirements), legal defensibility, and patient safety.

ADVION supports modernization of transitions of care standards but is concerned that the proposal to
narrow § 170.315(b)(1) to “transitions of care-receive and validate” may be premature given the current
state of FHIR adoption across care settings.
The Reality Check: LTPAC Is Not Yet FHIR-Ready at Scale
FHIR-based exchange has not yet achieved consistent, scalable adoption across all care settings,
particularly in LPTCA and many other transitions-of-care settings. The healthcare industry is essentially
moving from proven legacy exchange approaches, including C‑CDA via Direct, which remains accessible
and affordable for all providers, particularly those in rural settings, in favor of newer methods that do
not yet have adequate technical infrastructure.
Even the most advanced systems still struggle for full adoption and deployment at scale. Rather than
requiring everyone to move to FHIR immediately, ADVION believes there should be a more gradual
transition with FHIR implementation starting in more isolated use cases-such as public health for
electronic case reporting or immunization-to create an onramp with a defined period of time.
The reality in LTPAC is that many acute and post‑acute connections still rely on fax, phone, and manual
processes. CMS has had to issue regulatory reminders (including a 2024 survey memo from QSOG) to
hospitals about their existing obligation to send timely discharge information to skilled nursing facilities-
this predates any electronic exchange requirement and illustrates how far we are from universal FHIR
adoption.
LTPAC Needs Bidirectional, Complete Data
LTPAC providers do not merely “receive” data-we require bidirectional exchange and complete,
structured summaries at transitions:
•From hospitals to post‑acute: discharge summaries with therapy orders, ADLs/IADLs, wound
care details, equipment needs, SDOH information, and complete medication lists
•From post‑acute back to acute and community: updates on functional status, therapy progress,
changes in condition, and care plan modifications
Narrowing certification criteria without ensuring FHIR‑based TOC standards adequately support these
LTPAC workflows creates risk.

5. USCDI V3.1 ADOPTION AND LTPAC‑RELEVANT DATA

ADVION strongly supports the adoption of updated USCDI versions in § 170.213 and the removal of the
outdated Common Clinical Data Set. We support the process and stakeholder‑driven approach that
created USCDI Versions 1, 2, and 3, which included extensive input from clinical, technical, and patient
communities to identify data elements essential to comprehensive, coordinated care.
However, we must mention USCDI v3.1 specifically. Version 3.1 represents an administrative
modification that narrows previously adopted data elements-particularly related to sexual orientation
and gender identity-based on a policy directive rather than clinical or technical rationale.
The LTPAC Vendor and State Compliance Reality
ADVION understands that some state legislation requires sexual orientation, gender identity, and
pronoun capture within the electronic health record, such as on facility face sheets and care
documentation, even as federal standards change. Vendors serving customers across multiple states and
jurisdictions must maintain flexibility to support varying state requirements, which may exceed federal
minimums. Relegating these data elements to a narrower set creates regulatory conflict between state
requirements and federal certification standards.
From the LTPAC perspective, state surveyors still require that information. Even though the federal
government has taken a specific stance, states conducting surveys in facilities continue to require
gender identity, pronouns, and related information on the face sheet. LTPAC systems must maintain
those data points regardless of changes to federal standards.
Additionally, different states require different things, not just in skilled nursing but also in assisted living
and other settings. As national and international vendors serve customers across different states,
jurisdictions, and provinces with varying requirements, there must be latitude to meet these differences
so each jurisdiction can satisfy its own regulatory needs.
Our Position
•We support USCDI v3 as developed through the collaborative, evidence‑based USCDI process.
•We request that ASTP/ONC allow for discretion and flexibility in data element implementation
to accommodate state‑level requirements and variations in patient populations.
•We oppose administratively narrowing USCDI data classes based on policy directives that
conflict with clinical workflow needs, state regulatory requirements, and the established USCDI
development process.
LTPAC‑Specific Data Needs
Regardless of the v3.1 issue, we emphasize that LTPAC workflows require robust support for:
•Functional status and Activities of Daily Living (ADLs/IADLs)
•Therapy services (PT, OT, ST) including orders, plans, and outcomes

national “onboard once and you’re done” framework. However, making TEFCA the only acceptable

3. AUTOMATION AND AI: ACCESS, USE, AND GUARDRAILS

ADVION comments to the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy/Office of the National Coordinator for Health
Information Technology (ASTP/ONC) | Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability: ASTP/ONC Deregulatory
Actions to Unleash Prosperity (HTI-5) Proposed Rule | February 27, 2026 Page 11 of 13
ADVION supports the explicit inclusion of robotic process automation (RPA) and autonomous AI in the
definitions of “access” and “use” of electronic health information, as these technologies have important
applications in LTPAC workflows, including:
•Automated retrieval of discharge packets and hospital records.
•Risk scoring and predictive analytics for readmission prevention.
•Pre‑admission clinical and financial assessments.
•Care plan generation and clinical documentation support.
Critical Concerns: Security, Accountability, and Transparency
However, ADVION has serious concerns about unfettered RPA and AI access without corresponding
guardrails for security, accountability, and transparency.
There is particular concern about certain types of RPA. If actors can use RPA to access data, they may
violate business contracts intended to protect other parties’ technology from being observed, acquired,
or reverse engineered. Our larger concern is where RPA allowances intersect with third‑party write‑back
capabilities. If autonomous AI engines are writing back to systems, audit trails are compromised and it
becomes impossible to distinguish where human‑entered data ends and AI‑generated content begins.
ADVION is also concerned about incompetent actors, not just bad actors. USCDI and terminology
datasets exist so that people can understand the data in the system. When there is a pathway to
unfettered access-enabling actors to retrieve whatever data they want, there must also be responsibility
to ensure they understand the data they are pulling and acting on.
The “Cart Before the Horse” Problem
ADVION believes that ASTP/ONC is clarifying access rights without first establishing the regulatory
framework for responsible AI use in healthcare. Regulations should be in place before actors obtain
broad access. Those regulations should specify that for AI and RPA use, actors must understand what
data they are pulling and be subject to legal consequences if they misuse it or act negligently. Even AI
professionals have called for reasonable regulation in healthcare. Finalizing HTI‑5 with broad access
rights first creates risk if access expectations outpace the development of clear accountability
frameworks and exposes substantial risk without adequate mitigation.
Patient Consent and State Privacy Laws
ADVION also recognizes the growing complexity of state‑level patient consent and data privacy laws
(e.g., California, Maryland, Washington). RPA and AI access create scenarios where patient consent may
not adequately cover downstream data usage, and vendors may have limited ability to layer consent
controls into automated workflows.

   ADVION reiterates that many of the challenges discussed in this letter are rooted in the LTPAC sector’s
   historic exclusion from federal health IT incentive programs. As interoperability expectations expand
   under HTI‑5 and related rules, ASTP/ONC and CMS should identify funding mechanisms, grants, or
   incentive pathways to support LTPAC providers and the vendors that serve them, ensuring that policy
   expectations are aligned with available resources.
   ADVION appreciates ASTP/ONC’s effort to streamline certification, reduce burden, and promote
   innovation through HTI‑5. We share these goals and offer conditional support for many of the proposed
   changes.

   •Monitor for unintended consequences in LTPAC care coordination, clinical safety, and
   interoperability as requirements are removed.
