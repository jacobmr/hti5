# HHS-ONC-2025-0005-0258 - attachment text

Our Overall Position
We appreciate ASTP/ONC’s efforts to modernize interoperability and expand innovation 
through FHIR-based APIs in its Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability: ASTP/ONC 
Deregulatory Actions to Unleash Prosperity (HTI-5) Proposed Rule. Given the wide range and 
diverse needs of healthcare organizations, clinicians, staff, patients, and families, we agree that 
flexibility is essential for innovation in the health care space. However, we argue that without 
increase specificity the proposed rule could undermine ASTP’s goals of enhancing health data 
exchange and innovation while fostering a competitive environment for health technology.
Why We Need Greater Specificity
Broad principles without clear implementation guidance can result in ambiguity in the market. 
This lack of specific and ambiguity can create a barrier to entry, particularly for start-ups and 
smaller companies, setting up an environment that favors larger companies and technology 
vendors. Larger companies can more easily weather periods of regulatory instability while 
smaller companies have smaller reserves.  Should this occur, choices for health care 
organizations, clinicians, and patients and families become constrained and competition is 
limited. 
The Impact of HTI-5 proposed rule on Patients and Caregivers
Our work at OpenNotes has demonstrated that patients and families report clinically significant 
benefits when they are able to access their healthcare information. However, patient access to 
information has maximum benefit if it supports understanding, decision-making, and action. 
Narrowing patient‑engagement requirements as noted in the proposed rule leaves patients 
with “access” but no meaningful usability – not a real change from today. Patients may face 
confusion and diminished trust without clearer expectations around the patient-engagement 
requirements. These vague access policies also hurt caregivers – AI powered apps could health 
to bridge the well-documented information gap experienced by care partners and clear policies 
around will help to lower barriers to developers choosing to focus on patient and caregiver 
facing applications. 
Impact of HTI-5 proposed rule on clinicians
There is potential for clinicians to benefit from third‑party tools – but fragmentation will 
increase without clear interoperability requirements. These requirements should include 
standards for “write back” functions to allow apps to fully integrate and reduce fragmentation 
and workflow disruptions. In addition, a focus on safety and auditability would ensure that apps 
are not harmful to patients or accessed by parties not authorized to do so. 
Impact of HTI-5 on Startups and Developers

Regulatory ambiguity hurts small, patient‑focused startups that do not have sufficient reserves 
to weather periods of instability. Further, inconsistent APIs, authorization processes, and 
exchange terms create barriers to building and scaling tools. These barriers are particularly 
acute for start-ups and smaller developers, as they create significant burdens when working to 
scale their tools in the market.
Can specificity enable deregulation
We argue that greater specificity can lower friction and reduce transactional costs, which will in 
turn, make it easier for patients, clinicians, and innovators to succeed.  Further, clear baseline 
expectations can support innovation without reinstating heavy regulatory burden. The 
increased ambiguity that could result from HTI-5 will push risk onto patients, caregivers, 
encoding a “buyer beware” environment in which each patient is dependent on their own 
ability to evaluate the quality and safety on a given application.
Recommendations
We propose the following to reduce the risks to patients, families, clinicians, healthcare 
organizations, and developers of the proposed rule.
1. Clearly define minimum expectations for authorization workflows, scope, 
documentation, and error handling.
2. Establish standards for clear, safe, auditable write‑back to support high‑value use cases 
such as corrections, care planning, patient‑generated data, and AI-assisted summaries.
3. Explicitly reference patients and caregivers as primary users of interoperable health 
data.
4. Further clarify API access terms to prevent technical or contractual barriers that block 
legitimate third‑party tools.
Catherine M. DesRoches
Director, OpenNotes
Associate Professor, Harvard Medical School
