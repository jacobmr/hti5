Proposed Removal of Privacy and Security Certification Criteria and Scaling Back of Real World Testing  PFPS US is concerned that the proposed rule would significantly weaken the baseline patient safety, privacy, and accountability expectations embedded in the ONC Health IT Certification Program by eliminating privacy and security certification criteria.  A. Removal of Privacy and Security Certification Criteria The proposal to remove all privacy and security certification criteria, along with associated conditions of certification, represents a fundamental shift in the purpose of ONC certification. While HIPAA and other legal obligations remain in force, ONC certification has historically served a distinct and complementary role: establishing a baseline, testable expectation that certified health IT products demonstrate core privacy and security capabilities as a condition of participation in federally supported health programs. From a patient safety perspective, eliminating these criteria raises several concerns: • Loss of baseline assurance. Certification criteria provide a common floor for auditability, access control, authentication, and security functionality across certified products. Removing these requirements creates variability that is invisible to patients, caregivers, and clinicians. • Increased risk in API-driven environments. As interoperability increasingly relies on open APIs and third-party applications, the absence of certification-level security expectations heightens the risk of unauthorized access, data misuse, and cascading safety failures. • Disproportionate equity impacts. Patients with limited digital literacy, limited choice of health IT products, or reliance on safety-net providers are least able to assess whether systems handling their data meet basic privacy and security standards. PFPS US is particularly concerned that removing privacy and security criteria signals a retreat from patient trust and accountability at a time when health data are being reused for analytics, automation, and artificial intelligence at unprecedented scale. Recommendation: PFPS US urges ASTP/ONC to retain core privacy and security certification criteria—or clearly articulated functional equivalents—within the ONC Certification Program to ensure a consistent, enforceable baseline of patient protections across certified health IT. A patient is discharged from the emergency department after evaluation for chest pain. The following day, the patient and their caregiver attempt to understand what occurred during the visit, what diagnoses were considered, what treatments were administered, and what follow-up is required. Today, this understanding is often supported by a consolidated clinical summary that assembles disparate data elements into a single, encounter-level narrative. Emerging FHIR-based approaches—including document-like “envelope” or packaging mechanisms—hold promise for delivering the same function in more modern, flexible ways. However, in the absence of a required, standardized, and patient-accessible FHIR-native alternative, removal of the Consolidated CDA creation criterion risks leaving patients and caregivers with technically complete but clinically fragmented information. Without a coherent summary view, users must reconstruct the clinical story by navigating multiple discrete resources and timestamps—an approach that is neither intuitive nor safe during time-sensitive care transitions. The safety risk is not tied to the C-CDA format itself, but to the potential loss of a reliable mechanism for presenting a complete, interpretable account of an encounter. The patient safety functions of consolidated summaries—rapid situational awareness, coherent narratives, medication reconciliation, patient comprehension—must be preserved in any transition away from C-CDA.  PFPS US Recommendation:  PFPS US supports modernization toward FHIR-native data exchange and recognizes the progress of emerging FHIR-based “envelope” or document-like approaches that may ultimately surpass C-CDA in flexibility and usability. However, PFPS US urges ASTP/ONC not to eliminate the Consolidated CDA creation criterion unless and until a functionally equivalent, patient-accessible FHIR-native mechanism is required—one that reliably delivers a consolidated, encounter-level summary that supports patient understanding, care transitions, and safety. At minimum, ASTP/ONC should articulate the safety-critical functions currently served by consolidated documents and ensure that any replacement mechanism demonstrably preserves those functions for patients and caregivers. PFPS US appreciates that ONC’s Real World Testing (RWT) program is intended to validate that certified interoperability functions perform as expected in production environments, rather than only in controlled certification testing. Safety-Enhanced Design and Human Factors (SED/HF) A child with asthma is seen in an outpatient clinic. The clinician enters medication orders into the EHR. The interface design places pediatric dosing defaults behind multiple non-intuitive steps and displays dose units in a visually confusing manner. Under time pressure, the clinician selects the wrong dose, which appears visually similar to the correct dose. The child experiences an adverse drug event and requires hospitalization. The error was not due to lack of training or clinical knowledge. It was enabled by interface design that failed to support safe human cognition in real clinical workflows. PFPS US acknowledges ASTP/ONC’s goals to streamline certification requirements and foster innovation. However, we are concerned that weakening Safety-Enhanced Design criteria undermines one of the few formal safety mechanisms addressing how clinicians and patients actually use health IT in real clinical settings. Numerous peer-reviewed studies by human factors researchers have documented clear links between EHR design issues and patient safety events:

• Analysis of millions of patient safety event reports found that specific EHR usability problems — including data entry, alerting, and workflow support — were associated with potential patient harm. These included failures in order activation, improper scheduling defaults, and failure of critical alerts to fire. • Pediatric safety research found that over one-third of medication safety reports involved EHR usability issues, with nearly 20 % resulting in actual harm; the most common problems related to system feedback, visual display confusion, and workflow mismatch. • A detailed review of safety event reports related to medication ordering and administration found that health IT usability issues contributed to a high proportion of errors, many of which reached the patient, with issues clustered around data entry, workflow support, and alerting. • A scoping review on EHR use and diagnostic error identified specific technical, usability, and workflow issues that pose risks at every stage of diagnosis, including poor display design and inability to track or act on test results. • Broader assessments of health IT usability confirm that poor usability contributes not only to errors that reach patients, but also to clinicians’ cognitive burden and frustration — factors that degrade safety and increase burnout. Collectively, this evidence demonstrates that poor human-system interface design in health IT systems is not an abstract concern — it is a direct contributor to many serious preventable harms. Safety-Enhanced Design criteria translate human factors science into measurable expectations within the certification program. They help ensure that certified technologies are not only functionally interoperable, but also usable, efficient, and supportive of clinician and patient cognitive processes. Without such requirements, there is no consistent expectation that health IT developers will address: • Cognitive load imposed by interface layouts • Clarity of visual displays for critical data • Workflow integration that supports safe clinician decision-making • Effective alerting mechanisms that reduce, rather than contribute to, error • Support for tracking and acting on diagnostic data and follow-up tasks These are not optional features; they are preventive controls embedded in technology design that mitigate known patient safety risks. PFPS US Recommendation PFPS US strongly urges ASTP/ONC to retain Safety-Enhanced Design criteria in the ONC Certification Program. If ONC believes refinements are necessary to align with modern technology architectures, any modifications should: 1. Preserve human factors expectations that are demonstrably tied to patient safety outcomes; 2. Require evidence of usability evaluation using realistic clinical tasks and scenarios that reflect the complexity of real practice;

Safety-Enhanced Design in an AI-Enabled Information Ecosystem: Cumulative Patient Safety Risk A middle-aged patient presents to the ED with shortness of breath and vague chest discomfort. An AI-enabled clinical decision support tool integrated into the EHR surfaces a summary synthesized from recent labs, prior visits, and external records accessed automatically through expanded EHI exchange pathways. The summary is accurate but incomplete, omitting a subtle but relevant trend in prior abnormal imaging because of how the interface prioritizes “high-confidence” signals. The clinician relies on the AI-generated summary, not realizing that the underlying interface design makes it difficult to review the full longitudinal data. The diagnostic process is subtly biased toward what the AI surfaces most prominently. A critical diagnostic clue is missed. No single failure caused harm. The risk emerged from the interaction of: (1) automated AI-mediated data access, (2) interface design that shapes clinician attention, and (3) insufficient human factors safeguards ensuring transparency, auditability, and safe cognitive workflows. PFPS US supports ASTP/ONC’s policy goals to reduce friction in data exchange and to enable advanced technologies—including AI-enabled tools—to improve care delivery, care coordination, and patient access to information. PFPS US is also explicitly enthusiastic about the potential of safe and trustworthy AI to enhance patient safety, diagnostic accuracy, and care quality. However, PFPS US is concerned that weakening Safety-Enhanced Design (SED) and human factors (HF) expectations at the same time that the regulatory framework encourages greater automation,

algorithmic mediation of data access, and machine-to-machine exchange of EHI introduces cumulative patient safety risk. Individually, each policy direction may appear reasonable: • Expanded automated access to EHI can improve continuity of care. • AI-enabled tools can reduce clinician cognitive burden. • Streamlining certification can reduce developer burden. But in combination, these changes risk creating a clinical information ecosystem in which: • Clinicians increasingly interact with AI-mediated representations of the patient record rather than raw clinical data; • Interface design and algorithmic prioritization increasingly shape what information is seen, emphasized, delayed, or obscured; • Algorithmic outputs become cognitively privileged in time-pressured clinical workflows; and • The formal mechanisms that require vendors to design for real clinical workflows, cognitive load, error recovery, and sense-making are weakened. This interaction effect is not hypothetical. The more automated and abstracted the clinical information environment becomes, the more patient safety depends on robust human factors engineering and safety-enhanced design. Weakening SED/HF at precisely the moment when AI and automation are becoming embedded into clinical workflows is not merely a neutral policy choice—it risks removing critical safety infrastructure at the point of greatest need. PFPS US does not oppose automation or AI. On the contrary, PFPS US believes that AI can meaningfully improve patient safety. But AI-mediated care environments are only as safe as the interfaces, workflows, and cognitive supports through which humans interact with them. Without strong SED/HF expectations, the risk of automation bias, salience distortion, workflow mismatch, and silent failure modes increases—particularly in diagnostic reasoning, medication ordering, and follow-up of abnormal results. PFPS US Recommendation: Align SED/HF With AI-Forward Policy, Do Not Weaken It PFPS US strongly urges ASTP/ONC to retain and strengthen Safety-Enhanced Design and human factors requirements within the ONC Certification Program, particularly in light of the rule’s broader policy direction toward automation and AI-enabled access to EHI. Specifically, PFPS US recommends that ASTP/ONC: 1. Preserve SED/HF as a core safety requirement, not an optional or discretionary design consideration; 2. Explicitly align SED/HF criteria with AI-enabled workflows, including requirements that support: o Transparency of algorithmic outputs; o Clear provenance of underlying data sources; o Easy clinician verification of source data;

Constructive Alternative Vision PFPS US supports ASTP/ONC’s direction toward a more open, automated, and AI-enabled health data ecosystem. Our vision is not one of restraint, but of responsible acceleration: A future in which AI expands access to EHI and supports clinical decision-making—while Safety-Enhanced Design ensures that humans remain able to safely interpret, question, and act on what technology presents to them. In this future: • Automation reduces friction without reducing situational awareness. • AI augments clinical cognition without silently shaping it in unsafe ways. • Certification requirements evolve to reflect modern technology architectures without abandoning human factors as a patient safety cornerstone. PFPS US believes this alignment is essential if AI is to fulfill its promise as a patient safety tool rather than inadvertently becoming a new source of preventable harm.
