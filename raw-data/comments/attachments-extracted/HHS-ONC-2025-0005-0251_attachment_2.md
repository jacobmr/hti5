III.A.2.B. Clinical Information Reconciliation and Incorporation
We respectfully disagree with the removal of §170.315(b)(2). Clinical information reconciliation and incorporation provides a critical piece when sharing data among providers to update new medical information and significantly reduce provider and staff time.

There are four functions of database operations, which are referred to as CRUD, the ability to create, read, update, and delete. This is health care and medical records are documentation of encounters and therefore not deleted. That said, Direct Secure Messaging Standard is unique because it can be used to create new patient data, support reading documentation in both human and machine-readable electronic formats and update existing information via the reconciliation process.
For example, we offer a Patient Intake workflow where the patients is sent a link to complete a registration questionnaire. The patient answers these questions at their leisure 24/7/365 and then hits send. We convert their answers into USCDI proper codes such as LONC, RxNorm, and SNOMED and deliver this information to the patients chart inside of the EHR in the physician normal workflow in real time. This information is then reconciled by staff and incorporated into the patient chart. Our clients report savings of 35 to 45 minutes in the reduction of staff time by reducing the time spent in data entry and has increased the ability of their providers to see 1 to 2 additional patients per day by automating the processes of reconciling incoming medical data. Further, we do this via our RESTful API and Direct Secure Messaging.

III.8.a. Direct Project
We respectfully disagree with the removal of the Direct Project §170.315(h)(1) from the base definition of an EHR. The Direct Project and Direct Secure Message is a fundamental building block for many use cases including care coordination, referrals, patient intake, electronic case reporting to Public Health (eCR), event notifications via Direct which is required by Medicare, eligibility requests, prior authorization, and communication with community care providers.  
We respectfully disagree that health IT developers will continue to support Direct Secure Message when it is no longer a requirement for Certification. This is because there are so many requirements that health IT vendors will focus on those which are required.
The fact that Direct Secure Messaging is widely adopted is a testament the ASTP/ONC for its years of hard work to get to this point and to usefulness of Direct Secure Messaging. Further, it is exactly because Direct Secure Messaging is widely adopted that it should remain a part of the Base definition of an EHR. Having Direct Secure Messaging widely adopted was the entire point. We need an industry standard that could be used nationwide. Now, that we have wide adoption, now, we should kept it.
Further, we believe that removing Direct Project / Direct Secure Messaging will result in a decrease in interoperability and provide an excuse for bad actors to engage in data blocking.
This is equivalent to stating that 5010 X.12 (the standard used for filing and processing claims) should no longer be required for claims filing and processing because it is widely adopted.

Removal of §170.315(d)(12) Encrypt Authentication Credentials
We respectfully disagree with the removal of §170.315(d)(12) Encrypt Authentication Credentials. Digital Identity of patients, providers, payers, and devices play a major role in improving proof of identity and matching patients across organizational boundaries and reducing data breaches, fraud, and abuse in healthcare. This process begins with identity proofing of the patient, provider, payer, or device and binding the identity with a trust credential such as a Direct Address. The Direct Address identifies the owner of the credential, what policy was used in identity proofing the patient, provider, payer or device, whether or not the Direct Address is valid, what level of assurance the identity has, cryptographic key pairs for digital signing of the transaction and for encryption and decryption of the content.
The digital signature is attached to every transaction, and an audit trail is kept of every transaction. The data is secured because the data is encrypted. For this reason, one cannot spoof or spam a Direct Message because the sender and receiver is always known. Further, if a transaction is changed, it will break the encryption key, and render the message unreadable. Having a digital trust credential helps to automate and streamline and help ensure that only authorized patients, providers, payers and devices get access to PHI and PII. Further, a trust credential improves interoperability across organizational boundaries.  
Let’s look at an example of travel on a plane. People need a trust credential prior to travel on a plane. This trust credential can be a driver’s license, passport, or other government issued ID. On the day the person travels on the plane, they must present this trust credential to prove their identity. In our example, the person presents a driver’s license. A driver’s license is universally accepted when traveling from state to state such that a driver’s license issued by any state is trusted by all states. The driver’s license is checked by a TSA agent and if valid, the person is allowed passage into the airport. This example illustrates two points 1) know digital identity check every time you go through a TSA checkpoint (this in healthcare is known as zero trust architecture) and 2) a drivers license is a federated identity that is trusted by all states.  
Your identity establishes your rights. Patients can get their own medical records because they have the individual right of access. Providers can see patient’s medical records if they are the treating provider. Payers can get the patient’s medical records for dates of services in which they were the payer of their insureds claim.
