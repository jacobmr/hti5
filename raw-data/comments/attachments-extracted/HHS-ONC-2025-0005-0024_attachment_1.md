Dear ASTP Representative:

I submit this comment based on my professional experience as both a clinician and a legal professional. As a nurse practitioner and attorney my passion has always been patient safety. I have been honored to serve on the Board of Directors of the Pennsylvania Patient Safety Authority since 2017.

The integrity and security of electronic health information is not an abstract technical issue to me—it directly affects patient safety, clinical decision-making, billing integrity, regulatory compliance, and legal accountability.

Electronic medical records must accurately reflect what occurred in patient care, when it occurred, who created or modified the information, and how the data was generated. When electronic health information cannot be trusted to be complete, accurate, reproducible, and transparent, patients are harmed, clinicians are exposed to risk, and accountability systems fail.

Before any deregulation of health IT certification is considered, standards governing data creation, collection, preservation, and reproducibility must be updated. Data must be capable of being reproduced in a usable, searchable, and sharable format. Deregulating certification in the absence of modernized standards would weaken safeguards at a time when medical records are more complex, automated, and consequential than ever.

An updated audit trail standard is essential to the security and integrity of electronic health information. Audit trails are a foundational control, not an optional feature. Without robust audit trails, it is impossible to verify record integrity or meaningfully deter and detect billing fraud. Weak or opaque audit trails enable post hoc manipulation of records, conceal improper documentation practices, and undermine enforcement efforts.

Current standards do not reflect how medical records are created or used today. The most recent audit trail standard was published in 2018. Since then, electronic health records have expanded to include artificial intelligence-generated content, automated clinical decision support, medical device data, remote monitoring, and high-volume electronic messaging. Standards must keep pace with the evolving content and uses of medical records.

Updated standards must explicitly address transparency in data creation and use by artificial intelligence, medical devices, and messaging systems. It must be clear whether data was human-entered, machine-generated, auto-populated, copied forward, overwritten, or modified by AI, and under what authority those actions occurred.

Despite legal requirements for access and transparency, patients still do not have meaningful access to their complete electronic health information. Missing metadata, unavailable audit trails, and incomplete disclosures prevent patients from understanding their care, identifying errors, and protecting their rights. Access to electronic health information must include access to how that information was created and altered.

Audit trails must identify the software used in patient care and in the creation and modification of the electronic medical record. Software materially affects data generation, presentation, modification, and retention. Failure to identify software and system components in audit trails obscures accountability and impedes investigation.

Software used in patient care must be interoperable for safe functionality within healthcare facilities and for sharing patient health information. Interoperability is essential for patient safety and continuity of care and is necessary to comply with the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement (TEFCA). Fragmented, non-interoperable systems increase risk, delay care, and obstruct lawful information exchange.

Data retention requirements must be clearly defined and enforced. Electronic health information must be retained for the duration of patient care, for the duration of investigations, while litigation holds are in place, and in compliance with state, federal, and institutional requirements. Destruction, degradation, or inaccessibility of data during these periods undermines care, oversight, and the rule of law.

Audit trails are foundational—not optional—to billing integrity, patient trust, and legal accountability. Updated standards must require audit trails, including those related to artificial intelligence, to be transparent, non-proprietary, and complete. Audit trails must reveal corrections, deletions, copy-forwards, overwrites, and automated actions. Deregulation will not address billing fraud and may instead facilitate the practices that allow hundreds of billions of dollars to be lost through fraudulent and distorted use of electronic medical records.

Strong standards protect patients, clinicians, and the public. Weak standards protect systems at the expense of accountability. I urge HHS to modernize and strengthen electronic health information standards before considering any deregulation of certification.

Respectfully submitted,

Veronica A. Richards
