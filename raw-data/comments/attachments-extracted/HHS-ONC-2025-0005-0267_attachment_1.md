Dear Assistant Secretary Keane, 
 
On behalf of OCHIN, thank you for the opportunity to comment on the proposed rule, Health Data, Technology, 
and Interoperability: ASTP/ONC Deregulatory Actions to Unleash Prosperity (HTI‑5). We appreciate the 
Administration’s leadership in advancing a modern, patient‑centered health data ecosystem that reduces 
friction and cost in care delivery, expands access while improving the quality of care, and supports innovation at 
scale. We share the Administration’s commitment to eliminating unnecessary administrative burden, killing the 
“clipboard,” and to driving affordability, transparency, and trusted information exchange that improves both 
patient experience and system performance. 
 
Trusted, secure exchange is foundational. It sustains patient confidence, enhances performance of extended 
care teams, and allows interoperability to function as intended across clinical and non‑clinical settings. As HTI‑5 
advances a deregulatory framework that shifts governance, oversight, and accountability away from centralized 
requirements, expectations for safe stewardship and appropriate use of electronic health information do not 
diminish but shift to providers.  
 
For rural and other low‑resourced providers, this redistribution of responsibility creates significant new, 
unfunded operational obligations at a time of acute financial and workforce strain. Absent mitigation policies 
and shared supports, these pressures will erode providers’ ability to sustain interoperability with the rigor, trust, 
and responsiveness required for safe and effective data exchange. Efforts to absorb these responsibilities 
independently will divert scarce clinical, operational, and IT capacity away from care delivery, with direct 
consequences for patient access, provider participation, and long‑term financial sustainability. At the same time, 
patients continue to view their providers as the primary and most trusted stewards of their information—even 
as downstream data flows, automated access, and secondary uses become increasingly opaque and difficult for 
providers to oversee. 
 
As certification requirements are reduced and API access expands, a broader set of nontraditional vendors, 
AI‑enabled systems, and intermediaries will be able to connect directly to clinical systems. While this evolution 
promises to accelerate innovation, it also introduces new administrative costs and complexity for providers 
that often lack in-house security expertise, AI governance capacity, and third‑party vetting resources. Absent

clear guidance shared trusted supports, and defined integration boundaries, this section of the delivery system 
is left to independently manage a growing volume of access requests, interfaces, and automated connections, 
diverting limited capacity away from patient care. 
 
To ensure that HTI‑5 unleashes prosperity while preserving a viable care delivery infrastructure, we respectfully 
recommend that ASTP and ONC pair deregulatory actions with practical implementation supports that 
prioritize affordability, scalability, and trusted exchange for rural and low-resourced providers to avoid 
undermining the sustainability of this segment of the delivery system. 
 
RECOMMENDATIONS  
We recommend ASTP:  
• Provide additional time for compliance for rural and low‑resourced providers. Existing workforce, financial, 
and operational constraints require sufficient time to train staff, adjust clinical and IT workflows, and safely 
operationalize concurrent changes across certification, information blocking, and FHIR‑based access. Clear, 
sequenced timelines reduce the risk of crisis implementation that diverts scarce resources away from 
patient care. 
• Invest and provide sufficient time and flexibility for testing, validation, and vetting of FHIR‑based and 
API‑enabled access. Rural and low‑resourced providers require adequate time to test API performance, 
response times, error handling, and conformance with applicable Implementation Guides across real‑world 
clinical workflows before being held accountable for production‑level access. This includes time to validate 
security controls, identity and authorization models, write‑back behavior, and automated or high‑frequency 
requests, as well as to assess operational impact across multiple sites. Without sufficient testing windows 
and phased expectations, providers are forced into production deployment without the ability to identify 
safety, privacy, or reliability risks, increasing the likelihood of downstream harm and service disruption. 
• Establish safe harbors and explicit categories of enforcement discretion for rural and low-resourced 
providers. As responsibility shifts from federal certification to provider‑level judgment, providers remain 
accountable for safety, stewardship, and appropriate use of electronic health information. The proposed 
narrowing of information‑blocking exceptions increases exposure for providers making reasonable, 
resource‑constrained decisions. Clear safe harbors and enforcement discretion are essential to enable 
good‑faith participation in FHIR‑based exchange without assuming liability that threatens provider viability. 
• Coordinate and target federal investment in health IT workforce development and training to support 
rural and low-resourced providers. Providers need support across interoperability and FHIR‑based access, 
cybersecurity and privacy, data stewardship and compliance, and AI governance, including the operational 
demands created by continuous, high‑frequency automated and agentic access requests. This is not about 
adding headcount, but about equipping existing staff to manage increased demand without pulling 
resources away from care delivery. 
• Explicitly recognize and support trusted shared services partners and models. Shared services are the most 
affordable and scalable mechanism for rural and low‑resourced providers to achieve compliance, 
interoperability, and robust data governance. These models and partners with established track records 
enable consistent onboarding and vetting, ongoing monitoring, and defensibility for FHIR‑based and 
AI‑enabled access that individual providers cannot sustain alone.

through Regional Extension Centers and workforce development entities, focused on interoperability, 
cybersecurity, data stewardship, and operational demands created by automated and AI‑enabled access. 
 
Taken together, these recommendations are critical to ensuring that rural and low‑resourced providers are not 
pushed out of interoperability compliance by an evolving health IT ecosystem that assumes capabilities they do 
not have individually. With thoughtful refinement, HTI‑5 can both unleash prosperity and preserve the safety, 
trust, and accountability necessary for a thriving national health data ecosystem.

APPENDIX 
 
CUMULATIVE IMPACT OF PROPOSED INFORMATION BLOCKING EXCEPTION CHANGES ON RURAL AND 
LOW-RESOURCED PROVIDERS 
The proposed information blocking changes in HTI-5 would have significant cumulative impact on rural and 
low-resourced providers. While the proposal is framed as deregulatory, the information blocking provisions 
would, in practice, introduce new operational costs, complexity, compliance risk, and administrative burden at a 
time when providers are already facing acute workforce and financial constraints. 
 
The proposal does not assess the foreseeable implementation effort, staffing demands, and operational costs 
that rural and low-resourced providers would need to shoulder to operationalize these changes safely and 
consistently. 
 
As certification requirements are reduced and the volume of requests increase via varied and different 
pathways, responsibility for interpreting and operationalizing information blocking expectations shifts to 
providers. For rural and low-resourced organizations, this shift requires substantial new effort to reassess 
existing practices, revise internal policies, renegotiate and monitor agreements with third parties, and 
continuously adjudicate access requests under narrower and less predictable exceptions. These activities divert 
limited clinical, operational, and IT capacity away from care delivery and threaten the sustainability of 
interoperability participation for the providers least equipped to absorb additional burden. It also assumes that 
all providers have access to technically skilled staff to perform these functions quickly.  
 
The timing and immediacy of these changes raises concern. If finalized as proposed, rural and low-resourced 
providers would be expected to comply without sufficient staff, funding, transition time, clarity, or centralized 
guidance, increasing exposure to enforcement risk before organizations have the ability to search for and secure 
funding and technically trained staff, safely adapt workflows, governance structures, and contractual 
arrangements. In shared EHR and shared services environments, a single interpretation or compliance decision 
may affect dozens or hundreds of provider sites simultaneously, amplifying both operational disruption and 
liability exposure. 
 
These concerns are compounded by concurrent proposals related to automated access, third-party 
modification, and narrowing of infeasibility and manner exceptions. Taken together, these changes create 
uncertainty about what safeguards remain permissible and place rural and low-resourced providers in the 
position of balancing access obligations against patient safety, data integrity, and legal accountability without 
adequate support while facing debilitating potential penalties for information blocking. 
 
The sections below describe how these cumulative pressures manifest operationally for rural and low-resourced 
providers and outline practical safeguards needed to ensure information blocking policy remains feasible, 
scalable, and trusted.  

 5 
a. Provide Additional Time and Phased Implementation That Can Be Met Among 
Resource-Constrained Providers 
HTI‑5 introduces simultaneous changes to certification, information blocking, interoperability pathways, 
and oversight expectations. As certification requirements are reduced and FHIR-based API access 
expands, rural and low‑resourced providers will be expected to independently validate technical 
feasibility, security, and safety characteristics that were previously assured through certification. 
 
Operational impacts for rural and low-resourced providers include: 
• Training clinical, operational, and IT staff on new access models and compliance expectations 
• Redesigning workflows to accommodate new API-based and automated requests 
• Validating third-party tools, including clinical decision support and AI-enabled functionality 
• Reviewing and documenting access decisions under narrower exceptions 
• Coordinating changes across multiple sites simultaneously 
 
These activities introduce new and unfunded operating costs and administrative burden that cannot be 
absorbed on short timelines without reducing access to care, increasing implementation inconsistency, 
or accelerating service reductions and further destabilizing rural providers. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should provide additional time and phased implementation timelines, 
clear operational guidance, technical assistance, and appropriate enforcement discretion aligned with 
the capacity of rural and low‑resourced providers. 
 
b. Establish Safe Harbors and Explicit Categories of Enforcement Discretion for Rural and Low-
Resource Providers.  
The proposed changes to the Manner and Infeasibility Exceptions materially reduce the safe harbors 
providers rely on to make timely and defensible access determinations. While HTI‑5 advances a 
deregulatory intent, narrowing these protections increases administrative burden, documentation 
requirements, and liability exposure for rural and low‑resourced providers making good‑faith, 
resource‑constrained decisions without providing corresponding clarity or operational certainty. 
 
As FHIR‑based access becomes the default pathway for exchange, providers are required to make 
real‑time determinations regarding access scope, limitations, and safeguards in the absence of prior 
certification guardrails or centralized vetting. The responsibility for operationalizing these decisions, 
including managing safety, privacy, and security risk associated with downstream use, falls squarely on 
providers, even when they lack visibility into how data will be used once released. (They will also face 
state and private right of action claims for any privacy breaches.) 
 
Under the proposed framework, providers must independently and continuously: 
• Ensure access pathways remain reasonable, standards‑based, and operationally feasible 
• Apply access limitations consistently and proportionally to documented risks 
• Maintain documentation for every denial, delay, or modification of access 
• Adjudicate requests involving automated, API‑based, and AI‑enabled tools 
 

 6 
These expectations significantly increase operational and legal risk, particularly for providers with 
limited staffing, legal support, and data governance capacity. Absent clear safe harbors and 
enforcement discretion, providers will still comply with interoperability requirements, but will do so 
defensively, resulting in slower response times, more restrictive access controls, inconsistent 
determinations, and increased cost, all of which undermine the speed, scalability, and trust that HTI‑5 is 
intended to advance. This also exposes them to heightened risk of enforcement actions under 
prohibitions to information blocking.  
 
The proposed removal of the TEFCA Manner Exception further increases cost and complexity by 
requiring providers to evaluate the sufficiency of multiple exchange pathways without centralized 
guidance or predictability. This risk is magnified in shared EHR and shared services environments, where 
a single access decision may affect hundreds of provider sites simultaneously. Providers should not be 
penalized for applying reasonable and necessary safety, privacy, or security controls while implementing 
the rule’s deregulatory objectives. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP and ONC should establish clear protections for rural and low‑resourced 
providers acting in good faith under information‑blocking and HIPAA requirements, including explicit 
safe harbors and categories of enforcement discretion. ASTP/ONC should define reasonable and 
defensible criteria for applying exceptions and standardize documentation expectations to ensure that 
mandatory interoperability is implemented in a manner that is affordable, scalable, and trusted. 
 
c. Coordinate Federal Agency Investments in Health IT Workforce Development and Skilling 
As responsibilities shift from certification requirements to provider‑level oversight, rural and 
low‑resourced providers are expected to assume new roles in interoperability governance, security 
validation, and oversight of automated and AI‑enabled tools. Many lack access to staff with the technical 
expertise and funding required to perform these functions. 
 
Workforce gaps include: 
• Interoperability and API governance expertise 
• Cybersecurity and privacy risk assessment capacity 
• Data governance and compliance skills 
• AI and automation oversight, including monitoring and update management 
 
Without targeted federal and coordinated workforce investment, providers will struggle to safely 
implement API‑enabled access and rapidly evolving health IT systems and tools in a manner consistent 
with ASTP’s desired speed of EHI exchange and patient trust, security, and continuity of care. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should actively partner with HHS federal agencies and the U.S. 
Department of Labor to coordinate and rapidly target funding for and focus on health IT workforce 
development and training to scale impact while driving affordability, interoperability, cybersecurity, data 
governance, and AI oversight, with particular emphasis on rural and low‑resourced providers that are 
virtual. 
 

 7 
d. Fund Shared Service Compliance and Interoperability Governance Models 
The proposed changes shift operational, security, and governance responsibilities to individual providers 
at a time when capacity is constrained. Shared governance becomes particularly critical in a FHIR-first 
environment, where providers must support a growing number of third-party applications, write-back 
requests, and automated data flows that cannot be safely managed site by site. Shared service models 
reduce duplicative effort, promote safer adoption of new tools, and provide consistent onboarding, 
access controls, and defensibility across diverse care settings. 
 
Shared‑service models support providers by offering: 
• Centralized vendor onboarding and due diligence 
• Standardized security and privacy review processes 
• Coordinated incident response and breach notification workflows 
• Consistent documentation and defensibility for access decisions 
• Networkwide enforcement of safeguards as new tools are introduced 
 
As federal safeguards recede, these functions become more important, not less. Without shared 
governance, providers must independently assess multiple exchange pathways and technologies 
without adequate resources, increasing fragmentation, burden, and risk both for non-compliance and 
closing their doors. 
Recommendation: To spur trusted exchange at scale, ASTP/ONC should work with sister agencies to 
ensure limited federal resources are targeted to fund shared‑service compliance and interoperability 
governance models that reduce burden and support rural and low‑resourced providers as they 
implement HTI‑5 requirements. 
 
e. Leverage State Technical Assistance Through the CMS Rural Health Transformation Program 
As HTI-5 shifts responsibility for interoperability, governance, and oversight to providers, state-level 
technical assistance will be critical to preventing increased administrative burden from reducing access 
to care. State-funded technical assistance is especially important as providers transition to FHIR-based 
access models that require new skills in API governance, vendor vetting, and ongoing monitoring. CMS 
can ensure states through, at a minimum, available funding mechanism drive alignment across federal 
technical guidance, state applications, and workforce investment to avoid fragmented and costly 
implementation. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should work with state partners, in coordination with CMS, to encourage 
and align state applications under the CMS Rural Health Transformation Program to fund health IT 
technical assistance and workforce skilling aligned with HTI-5 implementation, including interoperability, 
cybersecurity, data stewardship, and FHIR-based access, and to support CMS efforts to advance state 
applications that strengthen provider capacity rather than shifting additional unfunded responsibility to 
rural and low-resourced providers.

ADDITIONAL DETAILED IMPLEMENTATION CONSIDERATIONS 
 
AUTOMATED ACCESS, ROBOTIC PROCESS AUTOMATION (RPA), AND AGENTIC AI REQUIRE CLEAR LIMITATIONS 
AND SAFEGUARDS 
OCHIN supports innovation that enables automated access to electronic health information where such 
technologies are deployed safely, transparently, and with appropriate governance. However, we have concerns 
that expanding automated access without clear limitations may materially increase cost, administrative burden, 
operational complexity, and risk particularly for rural and low-resourced providers and shared service networks 
that will need substantially more investments and resources.  
 
Automated access technologies differ fundamentally from standards-based API integrations. In many cases, only 
the requestor understands how an RPA or agentic system functions, while providers and shared service 
organizations lack visibility into downstream behavior, error propagation, or unintended data use. Changes to 
underlying systems or workflows can cause automated tools to behave unpredictably, creating risks to data 
integrity, patient safety, privacy, security, and system performance. These risks are amplified in shared EHR and 
shared services environments, where a single automated tool may affect many provider sites simultaneously. 
This should also be understood in the context of Critical Access Hospitals, community clinics, and other low-
resourced providers who do not have the governance and staffing and oversight capability to safely manage 
these requests currently. We are in the early stages of this segment of the delivery system with regard to AI 
systems and few have capacity or resources to address agentic risks. 
 
Also, OCHIN is concerned that language suggesting automated access or use of EHI “without limitation” creates 
ambiguity about whether reasonable and necessary safeguards remain permissible. Rural and low-resourced 
providers require clarity that appropriate limitations may be applied to protect patient safety, system integrity, 
intellectual property, and legal accountability. 
 
In practice, providers and shared service networks need the ability to: 
• Establish clear contractual terms that allocate responsibility and manage liability when automated 
technologies malfunction or generate errors 
• Protect intellectual property from unauthorized retention, reuse, or secondary model training 
• Limit automated access to appropriate use contexts consistent with the intended purpose 
• Apply reasonable technical, operational, and governance controls aligned with existing information blocking 
exceptions 
 
OCHIN is also concerned that sub-regulatory guidance addressing automation technologies will not be treated as 
binding or enforceable in the absence of clear regulatory standards and implementation guidance. 
 
Recommendation: At a minimum, ASTP/ONC should provide clear guidance affirming that automated access 
and use of EHI may be subject to reasonable and necessary limitations, particularly to support safe deployment 
in rural and low‑resourced settings. Clarifying the permissibility of contractual protections, intellectual property 
safeguards, and liability allocation would help providers operationalize automated access in a manner that is 
consistent, defensible, and sustainable. Further, significant investment is needed to train and provide capacity 
for providers in rural and low-resourced areas to meet these requirements.

RETAIN THE THIRD-PARTY SEEKING MODIFICATION “USE” CONDITION UNDER THE INFEASIBILITY EXCEPTION 
OCHIN has concerns regarding the proposed removal of the Third-Party Seeking Modification “Use” condition 
under the Infeasibility Exception, particularly for rural and low-resourced providers that rely on this safeguard to 
protect the integrity, accuracy, and reliability of their clinical databases. 
 
This condition allows providers to decline requests that would modify, overwrite, create, or delete electronic 
health information when the provider lacks sufficient insight into the validation, provenance, or safety of the 
proposed change. In practice, providers often have limited visibility into how third-party tools generate or 
validate data that would be written into the record, especially when requests originate from automated or 
externally developed systems. 
 
Removing this condition will significantly increase compliance burden and operational uncertainty by requiring 
providers to accept modification requests without adequate safeguards against data corruption or unintended 
overwriting of authoritative records. These risks are heightened in shared EHR and shared services 
environments, where a single modification pathway may affect many provider sites simultaneously. 
 
The interaction of this proposal with expanded automated access and third-party write-back further compounds 
injection and provenance risk for providers least equipped to absorb it. 
 
In addition, rural and low‑resourced providers face significant and growing liability exposure under an expanding 
set of state privacy and consumer health data laws as third‑party systems modify or write data into the clinical 
record in addition to HIPAA. Providers are expected to support timely, low‑friction information exchange, yet 
they remain the primary accountable entity at the state and federal level when inappropriate disclosures, data 
integrity failures, or downstream misuse occur, even when those actions originate outside the provider’s direct 
control. This creates a particularly challenging position for rural and low-resourced providers, who must balance 
rapid information sharing with heightened legal and financial exposure, including state‑level enforcement 
actions and private rights of action, without the staffing, legal, or governance capacity to independently manage 
these risks at scale. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should maintain the Third-Party Seeking Modification “Use” condition as a 
practical and necessary safeguard that allows rural and low-resourced providers to manage data integrity, 
provenance, and patient safety risks as automation and third-party system interaction increases. If this condition 
is modified or removed, ASTP/ONC should ensure that rural and low-resourced providers receive clear 
alternative protections, including additional implementation guidance and targeted resources to support 
downstream data governance and compliance obligations. 
 
IMPACT OF REMOVING MANNER EXCEPTION EXHAUSTED CONDITION UNDER THE INFEASIBILITY EXCEPTION 
OCHIN has concerns that removal of the Manner Exception Exhausted condition would drive substantial new 
costs and undermine the ability of rural and low-resourced providers and shared service networks to rely on 
standards-based interoperability while managing limited implementation capacity. 
 
The Manner Exception Exhausted condition provides clarity when a requester declines an offered alternative 
that would meet the request using a supported, standards-based manner. Without this condition, providers will 

 10 
be required to support non-standard or one-off solutions that increase variability and divert scarce resources 
away from scalable interoperability approaches. 
 
For rural and low-resourced environments, maintaining predictable, supportable exchange pathways is essential 
to managing cost, security, and operational risk across multiple sites. Any unique request would require its own 
interface and entail ongoing maintenance. This would be multiplied many times over. Today, it is narrowed to 
vendors that have registered their API and apps through an EHR vendor with a single pathway that is clearly 
delineated and streamlines upfront vetting costs that would not revert to every provider. Eliminating this 
condition would introduce ongoing support obligations shifting to individual providers and erode the benefits of 
consensus-based standards. This would present a significant new set of costs and resource allocation obligations 
to rural and low-resourced providers. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should retain the Manner Exception Exhausted condition to support scalable 
interoperability and allow providers to prioritize standards-based solutions that can be safely and consistently 
supported across diverse care settings. At a minimum, this exception should be retained for rural and low-
resourced providers or new funding targeted to assist them with the vetting and maintenance of oversight 
programs.  
 
AVOID WEAKENING THE MANNER EXCEPTION EXHAUSTED CONDITION THROUGH REDEFINITION 
OCHIN has concerns that proposed revisions to the Manner Exception Exhausted condition could significantly 
weaken its practical protections, particularly for rural and low-resourced providers. 
 
Changes such as replacing “substantial number” with “any,” removing consideration of whether a requestor is 
“similarly situated,” or replacing “same” with “analogous” may expand circumstances in which providers are 
compelled to support non-standard or unsustainable access methods, even when reasonable standards-based 
alternatives exist. 
 
For rural and shared service environments, the ability to pilot, test, and selectively scale solutions is critical. 
Requiring indefinite support for limited or site-specific implementations would discourage innovation and 
increase long-term operational burden and, again, drive new costs and need for additional technical staff. 
 
Similarly, removing the “similarly situated” consideration limits providers’ ability to manage meaningful 
differences among requestors, including automation capability, security posture, and operational risk. 
Ambiguous terminology such as “analogous” further increases compliance uncertainty and incentivizes 
defensive over-accommodation. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should retain existing definitions and guardrails within the Manner Exception 
Exhausted condition and address any identified misuse through targeted clarification rather than broad 
definitional changes that increase burden, cost, complexity and reduce operational feasibility for rural and 
low‑resourced providers. 
 
ENSURE THE MANNER REQUESTED CONDITION REMAINS OPERATIONALLY FEASIBLE FOR RURAL AND 
LOW-RESOURCED PROVIDERS 

 11 
OCHIN has concerns that proposed changes to the Manner Requested condition could significantly increase 
transaction burden and operational complexity, and ongoing cost for rural and low-resourced providers and 
shared service networks responsible for responding to access, exchange, or use requests at scale. 
 
Rural providers and shared service organizations rely on standardized contractual terms and predictable fee 
structures to support timely, consistent responses to third-party access requests. These organizations often 
manage requests across many sites with limited legal, technical, and administrative staffing. Requiring 
individualized contract negotiation or complex fee analysis for each request would introduce delays, create new, 
recurring cost obligations, and divert scarce capacity away from patient care, while requiring additional staff 
time. 
 
OCHIN is particularly concerned that applying highly technical or healthcare-specific valuation frameworks, such 
as Stark Law fair market value concepts, to health IT access arrangements will inject complexity and uncertainty 
into information blocking compliance. Rural and low-resourced providers typically lack the resources to engage 
legal and financial expertise or conduct repeated market analyses and depend on straightforward, commercially 
reasonable terms to operationalize access at scale, without creating a standing compliance function that must 
be staffed and maintained. 
 
When combined with narrowing of other infeasibility and manner protections, additional contracting and pricing 
constraints will make it impractical for providers to rely on the Manner Requested condition in real-world 
settings. Providers may respond by slowing responses, narrowing supported access pathways, or escalating 
decisions for legal review, resulting in defensive compliance behaviors that undermine the speed and reliability 
of information exchange and drive additional administrative and staffing costs. 
 
For shared EHR and shared services environments, standardized contracts and predictable terms are often the 
only feasible mechanism for managing access across dozens or hundreds of provider sites. Limiting the 
availability of standardized agreements or creating ambiguity around what constitutes sufficient negotiation or 
acceptable pricing, could disproportionately burden these models by shifting interface maintenance, contract 
management, and dispute resolution costs onto individual providers and eroding the efficiencies of 
consensus-based standards. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should ensure that the Manner Requested condition can be operationalized in a 
manner that preserves predictable, supportable exchange pathways for rural and low‑resourced providers. If 
additional contracting or pricing requirements are pursued, ASTP/ONC should explicitly account for the recurring 
operational, legal, and staffing costs imposed on providers and pair such changes with guidance and resources 
that prevent these costs from being absorbed by individual provider organizations. 
 
BALANCE API EXPANSION WITH SAFEGUARDS THAT PROTECT PROVIDERS AND PATIENTS 
As certification criteria are removed, including enhanced safety design, quality management system (QMS) 
expectations, and security and AI-related requirements, responsibility for determining technical feasibility and 
safety shifts from developers to providers. This shift is especially challenging for rural and low resourced 
providers that do not have the resources or staffing to validate modern, API driven tools at scale. The removal of 
34 certification criteria creates a material shift in responsibility from certified developers to providers. 

 12 
 
FHIR-based APIs are a primary mechanism through which access to electronic health information is expanding 
under HTI-5. While often described as a single standard, FHIR operates in practice as a heterogeneous 
ecosystem with version variability, optionality, differing implementation guides, and uneven maturity across 
third-party applications. Many applications can obtain FHIR client credentials through relatively low technical 
barriers, including public app registration processes, placing providers in the position of managing downstream 
risk after access is granted. 
 
In this API-driven environment, providers increasingly rely on governance mechanisms such as client identifiers, 
vendor registration, and contractual controls as primary safeguards for FHIR-based access. 
 
In a more open, API-driven ecosystem, providers must now independently validate: 
• Security controls and authentication practices  
• Vendor safeguards and breach response capabilities  
• Data use restrictions and redisclosure compliance  
• Interoperability behavior across tools that may not undergo uniform testing 
 
For CAHs, FQHCs, and other rural and low-resourced providers, these tasks introduce new operating costs and 
increase exposure to cyber and privacy vulnerabilities. Without transitional guardrails or guidance as well as 
federal and state targeted investments in their governance capacity, deregulation risks shifting responsibilities 
to providers least equipped to manage them. 
 
The proposed removal of the TEFCA Manner Exception compounds these challenges by eliminating a reliable, 
regulator-defined pathway for secure exchange. As TEFCA becomes “one option among many,” providers, 
especially low-resourced rural organizations, must evaluate a broader set of exchange pathways with uneven 
security maturity, governance controls, and logging capabilities. This shift expands the attack surface, increases 
exposure to impersonation or misconfiguration, and forces providers to support additional third-party 
connections without standardized safeguards. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should issue transition guidance outlining baseline expectations for API connected 
tools and provide a model risk assessment framework tailored to rural and low resource providers as well as 
work with sister agencies to provide ongoing targeted investment in governance capacity and oversight through 
shared service models for rural and low-resourced providers. 
 
PRESERVE TEFCA AS A RELIABLE AND INCENTIVE-ALIGNED PATHWAY FOR RURAL AND LOW-RESOURCED 
PROVIDERS 
OCHIN has concerns that removing the TEFCA Manner Exception without a clear transition strategy would 
eliminate one of the few regulator-defined, network-based pathways available to rural and low-resourced 
providers to manage information blocking risk and control interoperability costs at scale. 
 
As certification criteria are reduced and API-based access expands, providers increasingly rely on trusted 
exchange frameworks to reduce the need for individualized technical, contractual, and security evaluations. For 
rural providers, Critical Access Hospitals (CAHs), Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs), and shared service 

 13 
networks, TEFCA has the potential to serve as a predictable and consistent exchange pathway that limits 
fragmentation and reduces governance burden, by avoiding the need to build and maintain multiple bespoke 
interfaces. 
 
While TEFCA has achieved early momentum, participation remains uneven, particularly among smaller hospitals 
and less-resourced clinics. Removing the TEFCA Manner Exception without aligning participation with 
meaningful risk-mitigation value may discourage adoption by the very providers that would benefit most from a 
trusted network-based exchange option. In the absence of such incentives, providers may be forced to support a 
growing number of point-to-point access pathways with uneven security maturity, governance controls, and 
logging capabilities. 
 
From the perspective of rural and low-resourced providers, TEFCA participation could function as a practical 
form of information blocking risk mitigation if it reduced the need for repeated evaluation of disparate access 
methods. A framework that recognizes TEFCA participation as a mechanism for managing interoperability risk 
would support broader adoption while reducing operational burden on providers with limited governance 
capacity. 
 
Absent a reliable and incentive-aligned TEFCA pathway, providers may experience increased exposure to 
misconfiguration, impersonation, and downstream data use risk as they are required to evaluate and manage an 
expanding set of non-networked exchange options. This outcome would increase cost and complexity without 
improving patient access or care coordination. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should preserve TEFCA as a viable and meaningful pathway for rural and 
low-resourced providers by ensuring that participation aligns with reduced information blocking exposure and 
governance burden. Approaches that incentivize participation by CAHs, FQHCs, and other less-resourced 
providers would help advance nationwide interoperability while maintaining a trusted, scalable exchange 
environment that does not shift disproportionate evaluation and compliance responsibility onto providers least 
equipped to absorb it. 
 
ESTABLISH ACCESSIBILITY SAFEGUARDS REMOVAL OF WCAG ACCESSIBILITY CERTIFICATION CRITERIA 
(§170.315(e)(1)(i)) 
HTI-5 proposes to remove Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) conformance requirements from health 
IT certification on the basis that accessibility functionality is now sufficiently widespread and that civil rights 
statutes continue to apply. While OCHIN agrees that accessibility obligations remain in force under the American 
Disability Act, Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, removing 
WCAG from certification eliminates a consistent, upstream mechanism for validating accessibility prior to 
deployment. 
 
In the absence of certification-based verification, responsibility for assessing, documenting, and defending 
accessibility conformance shifts to providers, including rural and low-resourced organizations that lack the 
technical expertise and procurement leverage to independently evaluate WCAG compliance across complex, 
shared EHR environments. In networked and shared services models, a single accessibility deficiency can affect 
hundreds of provider sites simultaneously, amplifying operational disruption and remediation burden. 

 14 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should provide transition guidance or baseline expectations to ensure that 
accessibility requirements remain consistently implemented and verifiable across certified health IT, and that 
responsibility for accessibility assurance does not shift disproportionately to providers least equipped to manage 
it. Further, federal and state funding should be targeted to such providers to assist them with these new 
responsibilities.  
 
CLARIFY AND MODERNIZE WRITE-BACK AUTHORITY THROUGH ENFORCEABLE SAFEGUARDS 
While OCHIN supports enabling write-back through standardized APIs to reduce administrative burden, such 
functionality must be accompanied by enforceable safeguards to protect data integrity, patient safety, and 
provider liability. 
 
Our concern related to third party write-back is in combination with the proposed changes to no information 
blocking exceptions, autonomous/agentic access, and recurring automated queries. The concern is not 
interoperability itself as OCHIN is one of the largest movers of health information in the nation, but the potential 
for data injection and provenance risk if write back is expanded without explicit safeguards. 
 
By “injection risk,” we mean scenarios in which third party applications, potentially operating autonomously, are 
permitted to write data directly into the EHR without sufficient controls around validation, attribution, and 
review. For example, a care coordination or AI enabled application could write structured data elements such as 
risk scores, care gaps, referral status, or even problem list entries directly into the legal medical record. If that 
data is inaccurate, biased, or maliciously manipulated, it becomes difficult for providers to distinguish it from 
clinician entered information or to trace responsibility back to the source system. 
 
This risk is materially heightened by the HTI 5 proposals that: 
• Remove the “Third Party Seeking Modification Use” condition under the Infeasibility Exception (No 
Information Blocking) 
• Explicitly recognize autonomous AI systems and recurring automated queries as permissible “users” 
• Contemplate minimizing friction for agentic or automated access. 
 
Taken together, these changes could be interpreted by vendors as permission for high volume, automated, 
bidirectional data flows, including write back, without corresponding requirements for human in the loop 
review, source labeling, or certification level audit controls. 
 
As a concrete example: an external application authorized by one health center could use automated FHIR write 
back to insert algorithmically generated “high risk” flags or care recommendations into structured fields across 
multiple charts. If those outputs are trained on non-representative data, misconfigured, or intentionally 
manipulated, the resulting data could influence clinical decision making, quality reporting, or billing workflows 
across many organizations before the issue is detected. Without strong provenance tagging, audit logs, and 
developer accountability, the burden of detecting and remediating such an incident would fall almost entirely on 
providers and hosting organizations. 
 

 15 
This concern is compounded by the proposed removal of privacy and security certification criteria and DSI 
transparency requirements. In effect, a certified product could facilitate write back and autonomous access 
while no longer being required to demonstrate baseline capabilities for auditability, tamper resistance, or 
disclosure of algorithmic limitations. From our perspective, that combination meaningfully increases cyber risk, 
data integrity risk, and downstream liability for CHCs and shared service organizations. 
 
Recommendation: Specifically, ASTP/ONC should clarify that write-back by third-party applications is 
permissible only when: 
• The providers retain full control over authorization, scope, and revocation 
• All write-back data are clearly labeled with source attribution and method of generation (human, 
rules-based, or algorithmic) 
• Clinical write-back is subject to provider review or approval prior to incorporation into the designated record 
set 
• Certified developers are required to support audit logs, tamper resistance, and role-based access controls 
for all write-back activity 
• Liability for defects, bias, or security failures remains with the certified developer or application vendor, not 
solely the provider 
 
ASTP/ONC should also explicitly prohibit the use of write-back functionality to introduce algorithmic risk scores, 
utilization flags, or care recommendations into structured EHR fields without transparency, validation, and 
clinical governance, as such practices effectively bypass Decision Support Intervention safeguards and shift 
medico-legal accountability to providers who do not control model design or training data. 
 
Without these guardrails, expanded write back authority risks transforming the EHR from a trusted clinical 
record into an unregulated aggregation point for unverifiable third-party data, disproportionately exposing 
safety net providers to legal, financial, and patient safety harms. 
 
CLARIFY ACCOUNTABILITY FOR AI ENABLED TOOLS OPERATING WITHIN SHARED ENVIRONMENTS 
With AI related certification requirements removed, it is critical that ASTP/ONC clarify responsibilities for 
deployment, monitoring, provenance, updates, and downstream consequences. Without clear expectations, 
shared provider networks risk becoming de facto decision makers for tools and behaviors they did not design or 
control. Many AI-enabled tools ingest data via continuous or near-real-time FHIR API connections, further 
shifting responsibility for monitoring, update management, and safety oversight to providers as certification 
guardrails are removed. 
Providers will need to monitor: 
• Model updates, deprecation, and reweighting 
• Runtime monitoring for drift or unsafe outputs 
• Provenance and context of AI‑generated recommendations 
• Assignment of responsibility when AI contributes to unsafe outcomes 
Rural and low resourced providers lack the AI governance capacity to oversee these functions at scale. 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should define responsibility boundaries for AI deployment and monitoring and 
publish expectations for transparency, update notifications, and safety oversight. Further, funding should be 

 16 
targeted to rural and low-resourced providers to ensure that they have capacity through shared service models 
and trusted partners to manage new governance and risk at scale.  
 
STRENGTHEN OVERSIGHT OF DOWNSTREAM DATA USE AS AUTOMATED ACCESS EXPANDS 
OCHIN has concerns that as API-based exchange and automated access expand under HTI-5, providers face 
increasing exposure to downstream data use risks without clear guidance or practical safeguards, particularly in 
rural and low-resourced settings. 
 
FHIR-based API exchange significantly expands access to electronic health information (EHI) beyond the 
originating electronic health record (EHR). As more tools access EHI via FHIR APIs: 
• Providers face reduced visibility into how data is combined, analyzed, or commercialized once it leaves 
the originating system. 
• Privacy and breach risks expand data compromises originating from third-party systems that may be 
attributed, legally or reputationally, to providers. 
• Patients may lose confidence in the trustworthiness of digital health systems if they perceive 
uncontrolled data flows. 
These challenges are amplified when access is automated or agentic, as providers often cannot observe or 
meaningfully constrain downstream behavior in real time. 
 
For rural and low-resourced providers, downstream data use risk presents both operational and trust 
challenges. Privacy and security incidents originating in third-party systems may still be attributed legally or 
reputationally to providers, even when providers lack insight into how data is handled after release. Patients 
frequently return to their provider with concerns about how their data is being used, particularly when data 
moves into environments that are no longer subject to HIPAA protections. Without clear boundaries and 
transparency, expanded access risks undermining patient confidence in digital health systems rather than 
strengthening it. 
 
In a deregulated certification environment, providers must rely heavily on contractual mechanisms, 
enforcement provisions, and ongoing monitoring to manage downstream data use, even though many rural and 
low-resourced providers lack the capacity to perform these functions consistently. Because many FHIR‑based 
API requests are pull‑based and automated, providers often have limited ability to apply purpose‑based or 
Minimum Necessary constraints once data leaves the originating system. 
 
As patient-facing applications proliferate, providers increasingly face expectations to support access methods 
that clinic staff are not equipped to evaluate or explain. While patient empowerment is a central and shared 
goal, many patients have not been adequately apprised that their data will be moved by a third-party vendor 
into environments that are no longer HIPAA covered, and trust concerns often return to providers instead of the 
vendor. Training, education, and opportunities to engage patients in meaningful dialogue are critical so that 
expanded access is safe and responsible and does not shift the burden back onto providers. Expanding loosely 
defined API obligations to providers is likely to increase confusion and frustration for patients rather than 
improve meaningful access. 
 

 17 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should provide clear guidance on reasonable downstream data governance 
practices in an API‑driven and increasingly automated ecosystem. This guidance should address redisclosure 
expectations, secondary use transparency, and provider responsibility when third‑party systems handle patient 
information, while recognizing the capacity constraints of rural and low‑resourced providers. Clarifying these 
expectations would support patient trust, reduce provider liability exposure, and help ensure that expanded 
access remains safe, responsible, and sustainable. 
 
MAINTAIN CQM FILTERING CAPABILITY TO AVOID UNFUNDED REPORTING BURDEN AND AUDIT EXPOSURE 
(§170.315(c)(4)) 
OCHIN is concerned that removing the “Clinical Quality Measures (CQM) – Filter” certification criterion would 
shift essential reporting and stratification functionality from certified developers to providers and shared service 
networks, while underlying federal reporting obligations for CHCs through the Uniform Data System (UDS) 
remain unchanged. Rural and low-resourced providers rely on certified CQM filtering capabilities to meet 
required quality reporting for federal and state programs efficiently and accurately. 
 
Absent certified filtering functionality, providers may be forced to rely on manual chart review, custom report 
development, or third-party data extraction to fulfill reporting requirements. These approaches increase 
administrative burden, elevate error risk, and introduce new and unfunded costs for organizations that lack 
dedicated analytics staff or the ability to procure additional reporting platforms.  
 
Removal of this criterion also creates audit and defensibility risk. Providers remain accountable for the accuracy, 
completeness, and timeliness of quality reporting submissions, even as certification no longer ensures that 
underlying systems support standardized measure stratification and generation. This misalignment shifts liability 
and compliance exposure to providers that do not control system design and lack the capacity to independently 
validate reporting logic at scale. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should retain §170.315(c)(4) as a certification floor (or provide an equivalent 
enforceable pathway) to prevent cost-shifting, preserve reporting reliability, and reduce the need for duplicative 
third-party tooling that disproportionately burdens rural and low-resourced providers. 
 
RETAIN A MINIMAL DECISION SUPPORT TRANSPARENCY FLOOR TO SUPPORT SAFE DEPLOYMENT AND 
GOVERNANCE (§ 170.315(a)(9) and § 170.315(b)(11)) 
OCHIN is concerned that eliminating decision support transparency expectations entirely, particularly 
information that helps providers understand what a tool is, how it is intended to be used, and its key limitations, 
will further shift AI safety and governance burdens to providers and shared networks that do not design or train 
these tools. 
 
Rural and low-resourced providers increasingly rely on AI-enabled decision support for both clinical and 
operational decision-making. Without access to standardized transparency information, including source 
attributes such as data origin, intended use, known limitations, and update behavior, providers lack the 
information necessary to assess whether tools are appropriate for their patient populations or clinical 
workflows. This challenge is amplified in shared EHR and shared services environments, where decision support 
tools may be deployed across multiple sites simultaneously. 

 18 
 
OCHIN recognizes that transparency information is most valuable at the point of implementation and 
configuration, where providers and shared service organizations make enterprise-level governance and 
deployment decisions, rather than during routine clinical workflows. In practice, transparency artifacts such as 
source documentation or model information are rarely reviewed by end users at the point of care. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should retain § 170.315(a)(9) Clinical Decision Support and instead remove § 
170.315(b)(11) Decision Support Interventions to better achieve its deregulatory objectives while preserving a 
minimal, standardized transparency floor for evidence-based decision support. This approach reduces 
certification burden, avoids confusion regarding the scope of predictive decision support requirements, and 
preserves an appropriate safeguard without interfering with clinical workflows. 
 
While certification requirements may be reduced, providers remain accountable for clinical decisions influenced 
by decision support tools. Eliminating transparency expectations without a standardized replacement risks 
misalignment between deregulatory intent and ongoing clinical, legal, and operational responsibility, particularly 
for rural providers and shared service networks that lack the capacity to independently absorb these governance 
functions. 
 
AVOID FUNCTIONALITY GAPS IN STRUCTURED FAMILY HEALTH HISTORY DURING TRANSITION 
(§170.315(a)(12)) 
HTI-5 proposes to remove the Family Health History certification criterion, which currently ensures that family 
history information is captured in structured, exchangeable data fields. While future standards-based 
approaches may support this functionality through FHIR-based exchange, removing the existing requirement 
before an equivalent replacement is fully operational risks creating an interim gap in data structure and 
interoperability. 
 
Rural and low-resourced providers rely on structured family health history data to support preventive care 
workflows and population-level identification of patients who may require earlier or more frequent screening. In 
the absence of standardized structured capture, providers may be forced to rely on manual chart review, 
keyword searches within free-text documentation, or additional third-party tools to identify clinical risk factors. 
These approaches increase administrative burden, elevate the likelihood of missed information, and divert 
limited workforce capacity away from patient care. 
 
In shared EHR and shared services environments, loss of structured data requirements affects multiple provider 
sites simultaneously and cannot be mitigated through local configuration. While certification requirements may 
be reduced, providers remain accountable for preventive care delivery and care coordination outcomes that 
depend on reliable, structured data exchange. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should maintain a structured family health history requirement until an 
equivalent, standards-based replacement is fully implemented and broadly supported or provide clear transition 
guidance and sufficient lead time to avoid interim gaps that undermine interoperability, preventive care 
workflows, and population health management.

REDUCE DUPLICATIVE C-CDA TESTING BY RETAINING § 170.315(b)(1) AND REMOVING REDUNDANT 
REQUIREMENTS FROM § 170.315(e)(1) 
OCHIN supports deregulatory approaches that reduce duplicative testing burden while preserving consistent 
interoperability standards relied upon in real-world clinical workflows. 
 
Consolidated Clinical Document Architecture (C-CDA) exchange remains widely used for transitions of care and 
other provider-to-provider interoperability use cases and is expected to persist for the foreseeable future, 
particularly in rural and safety net settings. Maintaining standardized C-CDA creation and sending requirements 
within § 170.315(b)(1) Transitions of Care supports consistency and predictability in provider exchange. 
 
At the same time, § 170.315(e)(1) View, Download, and Transmit to Third Party includes extensive and highly 
duplicative testing of C-CDA creation and transmission, with dozens of permutations that materially increase 
certification burden without corresponding interoperability benefit. Removing C-CDA testing requirements from 
§ 170.315(e)(1), rather than from § 170.315(b)(1), would achieve greater burden reduction while preserving an 
appropriate standardization anchor for provider-to-provider exchange. 
 
This approach aligns with ASTP’s deregulatory intent by reducing unnecessary duplication, maintaining 
consistency across certification criteria, and avoiding fragmentation that would otherwise shift testing and 
validation responsibility to provide and shared service networks least equipped to absorb it. 
 
Recommendation: ASTP/ONC should retain standardized C-CDA creation and sending requirements within § 
170.315(b)(1) Transitions of Care and remove duplicative C-CDA testing requirements from § 170.315(e)(1) 
View, Download, and Transmit to Third Party. This approach would achieve greater certification burden 
reduction, maintain consistency across certification criteria, and avoid shifting additional testing and validation 
responsibility to providers and shared service networks least equipped to absorb it.

IMPACT OF REMOVING SECURITY & AUDIT CRITERIA FROM CERTIFIED HEALTH IT  
Collectively, the removal of these security, audit, and integrity criteria from Certified Health IT does not 
eliminate the underlying requirements. Instead, it redistributes them from vendors and certification programs to 
individual providers and shared service networks. For rural hospitals, rural clinics, and low-resourced providers,

oversight and reduce false fraud signals. As CMS expands analytics driven and AI enabled oversight under the 
proposed CRUSH, weakening baseline certification requirements for security and audit functionality risks 
increasing false positives, audit burden, and access disruption for legitimate rural and safety net providers.
