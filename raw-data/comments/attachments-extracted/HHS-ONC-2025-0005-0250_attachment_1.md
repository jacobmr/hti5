The Confidentiality Coalition appreciates the opportunity to comment on the “Health Data,
Technology, and Interoperability: ASTP/ONC Deregulatory Actions to Unleash Prosperity (HTI-5)
Proposed Rule,” published in the Federal Register on December 29, 2025, by the Assistant
Secretary for Technology Policy and the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information
Technology (ASTP/ONC) within the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS).
The Healthcare Leadership Council (HLC) is an association of CEOs and C-suite executives from all
sectors of healthcare working to shape the future of the U.S. healthcare system. HLC is the
exclusive forum for the nation’s healthcare industry leaders to lead on major, sector-wide issues,
generate innovative solutions to unleash private sector ingenuity, and advocate for policies to
improve our nation’s healthcare delivery system. Members of HLC – hospitals, academic health
centers, health plans, pharmaceutical companies, medical device manufacturers, laboratories,
biotech firms, health product distributors/ wholesalers, post-acute care providers, homecare
providers, group purchasing organizations, and information technology companies – advocate for
measures to increase the quality and efficiency of healthcare through a patient-centered approach.
The Confidentiality Coalition is a diverse alliance dedicated to balancing the protection of confidential
health information with the need for efficient, interoperable systems that enhance healthcare
delivery. The Coalition’s aim is to safeguard the privacy of healthcare consumers while improving the
essential flow of information necessary to deliver high-quality, timely, and effective care and
facilitating the development of innovative medical interventions.
The Confidentiality Coalition supports the stated goals of the HTI-5 Proposed Rule to reduce
administrative burdens and lower compliance costs for health information technology (IT) developers
and providers. We applaud ASTP/ONC's effort to promote innovation by eliminating duplicative or
outdated requirements in the Certification Program and refocusing the industry on interoperable, AI-
enabled health data exchange.
While we support this deregulatory direction, we urge ASTP/ONC to ensure that burden reduction
does not come at the expense of essential privacy protections, operational stability, or critical
interoperability infrastructure. Our comments therefore recommend targeted retention of certain
certification criteria—including for Direct messaging, AI sourcing information, and specific audit
functionalities—where removal would create unintended consequences.

2
Consistent with the cross‑sectoral recommendations outlined in a recent HLC report, we are
encouraged by the rule's focus on advancing a foundation for AI-enabled interoperability solutions.1
We believe AI holds immense promise for clinical care—from enhancing diagnostics to alleviating
workforce burnout—and should be incentivized through clear, risk-based federal frameworks that
prioritize transparency, accountability, and the reduction of bias. Our comments recommend
maintaining baseline transparency requirements for high-risk clinical decision support tools while
ensuring any federal AI framework remains agile and proportionate to risk.
We offer recommendations to strengthen the information blocking provisions, including requests for
further clarity on key definitions such as "unconscionable terms" and "analogous" access,
maintaining the manner exception exhausted condition, and revising the Trusted Exchange
Framework and Common Agreement (TEFCA) exception to encourage adoption by smaller
providers.
Finally, while we support the direction of this rule, we have concerns regarding the proposed
timeframe for implementation. Meaningful compliance will require adequate time for stakeholders to
assess and address the technical, workflow, and design implications of these changes. The final rule
should ensure that innovation does not come at the expense of operational stability, system
development, or patient safety.
Below we provide specific comments on these themes and the primary proposals included in the
HTI-5 Proposed Rule.
Balancing Patient Protections and Deregulation in the Certification Program
Reducing Administrative Burden by Eliminating Duplicative and/or Unnecessary Regulations
While Maintaining Those That Offer Value
The Confidentiality Coalition supports the primary objective of the HTI-5 Proposed Rule: to reduce
administrative burdens and lower compliance costs for health (IT) developers and providers. The
proposed rule signifies a marked shift in federal health IT policy by reducing existing compliance
obligations to unleash prosperity and innovation.
We support the rule's intent to shift the federal framework away from rigid, prescriptive oversight
toward increased market competition while still maintaining guardrails that promote operational
stability. By reducing barriers to entry for the certification of health IT, the agency is granting
providers greater flexibility and product choice, ensuring that competition—rather than federal
mandates—drives the next generation of healthcare solutions.
By proposing to remove unnecessary or duplicative certification criteria and revise others,
ASTP/ONC is taking a significant step toward modernizing the Certification Program. We agree that
many of these requirements are no longer necessary to advance interoperability and, in many cases,
may impede innovation by requiring developers to design prescriptive, outdated functionalities rather
than current market needs.

Certification Criteria and Core Requirements That Should Be Retained as Foundational
AI Transparency and Clinical Decision Support  
The Confidentiality Coalition is encouraged by the HTI-5 Proposed Rule’s focus on advancing AI-
enabled interoperability solutions. We agree that AI holds immense promise for revolutionizing
clinical care—from enhancing diagnostics and drug discovery to alleviating workforce burnout.
However, as echoed in the recent report released by the Healthcare Leadership Council2 referenced
above, we believe that fostering scalable, responsible AI adoption requires a careful balance
between rapid innovation and risk-based guardrails, including transparency and clarity in
accountability.
In the HTI-5 Proposed Rule, ASTP/ONC proposes to narrow the scope of the clinical decision
support interventions (DSI) criterion by removing certain AI “model card” source attribution
requirements. While Coalition members have observed that some prescriptive transparency features
such as end‑user source‑attribute displays are rarely used in practice, the Coalition strongly
supports risk-based transparency for predictive models.  
Rather than finalizing the proposed removal of the expired clinical decision support criterion
(§170.315(a)(9)) and substantially narrowing the decision support interventions (§170.315(b)(11))
criterion, the clinical decision support (a)(9) criterion should be maintained so evidence-based
decision support tools retain meaningful transparency requirements. This would reduce burden
without sacrificing needed transparency.
Additionally, we emphasize that any federal AI regulations including transparency requirements must
be risk-based, focusing requirements on high-risk clinical applications while avoiding undue burden
on lower-risk administrative tools. We encourage ASTP/ONC to actively support the development of
and alignment with consensus-based, industry-led implementation guides and standards for AI
transparency in accordance with the National Technology Transfer and Advancement Act (NTTAA)
such as the configurable AI “Nutrition Label” referenced in HLC’s AI report. This would create a
nationally recognized risk-based disclosure framework used to communicate bias safeguards,
privacy protections, and performance metrics in a consistent format. This approach also fosters
innovation while building the trust necessary for adoption.
Privacy and Security Safeguards
The Confidentiality Coalition believes that safeguarding patient privacy and data security is the
bedrock of a trusted, interoperable healthcare system. Our members remain fundamentally
committed to building upon the legal and regulatory framework established by HIPAA, which
continues to serve as the "gold standard" for protecting identifiable health information.
ASTP/ONC proposes to remove all 13 privacy and security certification criteria currently codified in §
170.315(d) on the grounds that these technical requirements have become duplicative of the HIPAA
Security Rule and that market competition is now a stronger driver for security innovation than
federal certification. ASTP/ONC also proposes an alternative approach that would retain certain
certification criteria to support the identification of fraud and abuse. The Coalition supports this

Direct Messaging
Direct messaging has become a foundational, nationwide method for secure health information
exchange, due in large part to certification requirements that promoted consistent implementation
and broad adoption. ASTP/ONC’s proposal to remove Direct Project (§170.315(h)(1)) and Direct +
Edge + XDR/XDM (§170.315(h)(2)) certification criteria risks signaling to the market that Direct is
optional, which could degrade its reliability. Direct messaging is a reliable mechanism key to payer-
to-payer alignment, as illustrated by its adoption for the health plan industry commitment to reduce
burden around prior authorization.  
Transition of Care
While the Confidentiality Coalition strongly supports the long‑term transition toward Fast Healthcare
Interoperability Resources (FHIR)‑based Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) for
transitions‑of‑care exchange, we urge ASTP/ONC to ensure that interim revisions to §170.315(b)(1)
do not undermine the reliability of existing exchange mechanisms before FHIR‑based approaches
are mature and widely implemented. While we agree that FHIR-based APIs represent the future,
Consolidated Clinical Document Architecture (C‑CDA) creation and receipt should be retained during
the transition period as it remains the predominant and most reliable mechanism for exchanging
comprehensive clinical information across care settings today.  
Electronic Case Reporting
The proposal to remove the standards‑based certification option in § 170.315(f)(5)(ii) and rely solely
on the functional requirements in § 170.315(f)(5)(i) represents a step backward from established,
consensus‑based approaches to electronic case reporting and risks reducing consistency and
interoperability in public health data exchange. We encourage ASTP/ONC to retain electronic case
reporting standards.
Data Standards and Consistency
The Confidentiality Coalition supports refining and streamlining the United States Core Data for
Interoperability (USCDI) to create a more focused and manageable dataset for exchange. A
consistent, national baseline for data elements is critical for true interoperability. As the Common
Clinical Data Set (CCDS) substantially overlaps with USCDI v3.1, USCDI should serve as the sole
baseline to reduce complexity.

in identifying fraud.

Information Blocking
Clarifying Definitions and Providing Examples  
In our comments on the HTI-23 Proposed Rule, the coalition asked for more clarity and improved
specificity regarding information blocking and exceptions. While we are encouraged that ASTP/ONC
has provided some clarity in these areas, the Coalition believes there remain areas of ambiguity that
could use further clarity.  
We agree that much of today's health data exchange occurs through automated processes.  
Clarifying that these activities fall within the existing information blocking framework provides
welcome certainty for developers, payers, and providers without creating new affirmative obligations.
However, the Confidentiality Coalition is concerned that including autonomous and agentic AI
systems without limits in the definitions of "access" and "use" will threaten clinical safety and could
lead to unintended consequences.  
For example, a robotic process automation (RPA) or other autonomous AI could set incorrect
medication doses for thousands of patients simultaneously, forcing clinical and administrative staff to
manually fix the errors. That said, there are appropriate and safer use cases for these tools, such as
testing new workflows as they are implemented.  
To protect developers' intellectual property (IP) and maintain clinical safety, ASTP/ONC should
include explicit clarification that limiting unsafe or unvalidated AI tools is not information blocking,
indemnify developers when other developers' tools are used on their products, and clarify that
autonomous AI tools can access EHI but not IP such as screens and workflows. We also urge the

ASTP/ONC proposes defining “unconscionable terms” as those that are excessive, unreasonable, or
shockingly unfair or unjust. We request ASTP/ONC adopt a clearer definition of “unconscionable
terms” because the proposed language remains ambiguous and open to subjective interpretation. To
reduce ambiguity and support consistent application of the Manner Exception, ASTP/ONC should
identify presumptively permissible categories of commonly used, good‑faith industry contract terms
that would not be considered unconscionable.
Similarly, ASTP/ONC proposes to modify the Manner Exception and the manner exception
exhausted condition by using “analogous” instead of “the same” access, exchange, or use of
electronic health information (EHI). We believe the term “analogous” is ambiguous and creates
uncertainty for actors like health information networks (HINs) and health information exchanges
(HIEs) trying to determine whether different access mechanisms (e.g., batch file transfer v. real-time
API, different FHIR versions, different authentication methods) would be considered “analogous.”
We ask ASTP/ONC to provide additional guidance surrounding this definition modification, including
more examples, of when access methods are “analogous” versus sufficiently distinct so as not to
trigger information blocking.
Maintaining the “Manner Exception Exhausted” Condition and a Revising the TEFCA Manner
Exception
ASTP/ONC proposes to remove or revise the “manner exception exhausted” condition from the
Infeasibility exception. The Coalition recommends maintaining this condition and instead addressing
its concerns that actors can easily abuse information blocking via clearer guidance, such as FAQs or
rule preambles that describe the efforts actors should demonstrate that they satisfy the exception.
ASTP/ONC also proposes to require actors to consider "all" possible alternative manners rather than
just two. We appreciate ASTP/ONC’s goal of preventing abuse but are concerned that the “all”
requirement is operationally infeasible for certain stakeholders like HINs/HIEs.
ASTP/ONC also proposes removing the TEFCA Manner Exception, arguing that TEFCA is widely
adopted enough to no longer need it. TEFCA has the promise of solving the last-mile problem of
interoperability. With government sponsorship and diligent work to expand use cases, TEFCA can
become the single on-ramp to nationwide interoperability. However, by proposing its wholesale
removal from the information blocking regulations, ASTP/ONC is removing an important tool at its
disposal to encourage adoption by smaller healthcare providers. ASTP/ONC argues that TEFCA is
already seeing high adoption, but this mostly comes from large health systems. ASTP/ONC should
refocus the TEFCA Manner Exception to specifically apply to smaller healthcare providers, including
Critical Access Hospitals and rural healthcare facilities.
Information Blocking Recommendations:
• ASTP/ONC should provide further clarity on certain definitions within the proposed rule
including “unconscionable terms” and “analogous” access.
• Regarding revised “access” and “use” definitions, ASTP/ONC should provide guardrails and
implementation guidance regarding the inclusion of autonomous and agentic AI. These
should include clarification that limiting unsafe or unvalidated tools is not information blocking

and clarification on how the information blocking framework should be applied to protect

Ensuring Implementation Timelines Promote Operational Stability
While the Confidentiality Coalition supports the direction and objectives of the HTI-5 Proposed Rule,
we are concerned regarding the feasibility of the proposed implementation timelines. Although the
rule aims to reduce burden, our members emphasize that the transition itself requires substantial
time and resources to ensure that modifications to health IT products do not compromise operational
stability or patient safety.
The agency proposes an effective date for many removals and revisions immediately upon the
publication of the final rule, with other major transitions (such as for Transitions of Care and Clinical
Information Reconciliation) set for January 1, 2027. We believe these timelines are not operationally
feasible. Even when removing certification criteria, health IT developers must navigate workflow,
design, testing, and deployment implications to ensure their products remain functional and
interoperable in a "FHIR-forward" environment.  
While this rule is largely deregulatory in nature, some elements will create new workstreams and
present implementation costs, such as removing certain information blocking exemptions.  
ASTP/ONC characterizes this rule as a "reset" of the Certification Program to establish a new
foundation for FHIR-based APIs and AI-enabled solutions. Such a fundamental shift requires
developers to re-evaluate their product roadmaps, conduct rigorous testing, and deploy updates to
thousands of provider sites. Even if this represents a reduction in data elements, any change to the
baseline standard requires a development and validation cycle that cannot be completed
instantaneously.
We also urge the agency to ensure that HTI-5 compliance dates are harmonized with other federal
requirements to avoid strain on developer and provider resources.  
Implementation Timeline Recommendations:
• ASTP/ONC should consider a phased approach rather than having the majority of
certification removals take effect immediately. This would allow developers time to retire old
functionalities in a controlled manner that aligns with their customers’ upgrade cycles.
• ASTP/ONC should provide at least six months from the publication of the final rule before
any changes to information blocking take effect.
