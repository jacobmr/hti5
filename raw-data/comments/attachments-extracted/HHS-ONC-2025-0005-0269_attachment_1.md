solutions on the rest of the health IT industry. ASTP should:  
 
a. Maintain the Infeasibility Exception’s Manner Exhausted Condition. 
b. Revise its proposal so that actors can, with certainty, fulfill requests through standards-based 
interoperability, rather than custom, one-off connections or non-standard proprietary 
connections, especially when non-standard options compel disclosure of proprietary IP.  
c. Maintain existing conditions of this exception, such as “similarly situated,” that provide important 
frameworks for actors to account for meaningful differences when provisioning access.

I. Certification Program 
 
ASTP should finalize most of its proposals to remove requirements in the Certification Program. Some of the 
requirements proposed to be removed were never effective. Many others have outlived their usefulness because 
healthcare organizations expect health IT developers to include the functionality in their software , regardless of 
the Certification Program requirements. The removal of these requirements will provide compliance cost savings 
because certified health IT developers will not have to present the features for certification testing.  
 
In two instances, we suggest that ASTP retain a certification requirement that it proposes to eliminate. The 
requirement related to clinical decision support (CDS) in § 170.315(a)(9) should not be removed. Rather, it should 
be retained and § 170.315(b)(11) can be removed instead. This achieves ASTP’s goals related to revising § 
170.315(b)(11) – reducing burden and permitting innovation, while preserving important transparency on 
evidenced-based CDS for the industry. In addition, ASTP should reconsider its proposal to eliminate the 
accessibility-centered design requirement. The proposal does not reduce burden and the requirement contains 
important accessibility expectations.  
  
ASTP should move forward with its proposal to eliminate the current privacy and security framework in the 
Certification Program and replace it in future rulemaking. While privacy and security are key areas of focus in 
healthcare, ASTP correctly identifies problems with the current structure in certification. For instance, certain 
security features required to be part of an operating system should instead be required in an application. Further, 
the current framework includes resource-intensive testing protocols that do not effectively assess the privacy and 
security of health IT software. ASTP is headed in the right direction with its plans through future rulemaking to 
replace the privacy and security framework with criterion-specific privacy and security requirements that will be 
incorporated into the testing of a particular feature.  
 
In some instances, ASTP should take an alternative approach to its proposal. As an example, ASTP should remove 
C-CDA sending test cases from § 170.315(e)(1) rather than from § 170.315(b)(1). This will retain helpful 
standardization of C-CDA formatting in § 170.315(b)(1) while reducing burden by removing exceptionally 
duplicative test cases of that functionality in § 170.315(e)(1). ASTP should not revise § 170.315(f)(5) related to 
electronic case reporting; the proposed revision would inadvertently penalize developers who have already 
invested in the advanced standard proposed to be removed. 
 
Although not proposed, ASTP should additionally reduce burden and promote standards-based interoperability in 
the Certification Program with updates to ASTP’s Standards Version Advancement Process (SVAP) – ASTP’s 
method to permit developers to certify to newer standards than are identified in regulation . Below, we suggest 
two updates to the process that will decrease burden and encourage investment in advanced standards. 
 
A. Terms and Definitions 
 
We appreciate ASTP’s diligence in reviewing the cross references and updating terms and definitions where 
appropriate. This helps ensure clarity for the Certification Program. 
 
We also appreciate ASTP’s coordination with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) to adjust its 
references to the Certification Program. It is important for CMS, ASTP, EHR developers, and participants in CMS 
payment programs to be on the same page about the expectations for use of certified EHR technology (CEHRT).

B. Revised and Removed Criteria 
 
1. § 170.315(a)(5) Patient demographics and observations 
 
ASTP’s proposal to align § 170.315(a)(5) with USCDI v3.1 and recently released enforcement discretion is sensible. 
 
This criterion has been in the Certification Program since its inception as part of the “Qualified EHR” definition. 
Now that USCDI includes patient demographics, it may be an option to remove this criterion and have the 
“Qualified EHR” definition satisfied by USCDI. 
 
2. § 170.315(a)(9) Clinical decision support 
 
We propose retaining § 170.315(a)(9) and instead removing § 170.315(b)(11); the current proposal would remove 
the expectation that evidence-based decision support have the ability to display source attributes. Reverting to § 
170.315(a)(9) seems to more effectively accomplish ASTP’s deregulatory goals. 
 
If our suggestion is not adopted, removing § 170.315(a)(9) from the Certification Program is logical since this 
criterion expired on Jan. 1, 2025, as set by the HTI-1 Final Rule. 
 
3. § 170.315(a)(12) Family health history 
 
ASTP proposes to remove this well-established criterion, and we concur. This feature will not be removed from 
products even if it is removed from the Certification Program. Additionally, family health is included in USCDI v6. 
 
4. § 170.315(a)(14) Implantable device list 
 
ASTP proposes to remove this broadly adopted criterion to reduce burden , and we concur. Support for unique 
device identifiers (UDIs) will remain in our products, and UDIs are also a required data element in USCDI v3.  
 
5. § 170.315(b)(1) Transitions of care 
 
We propose a different revision to this criterion that we think will accomplish ASTP’s deregulatory and burden 
reduction goals while preserving important standardization in interoperability.  
 
We do not agree with ASTP’s assessment that C-CDA exchange is diminishing. CDA-based exchange is widely 
used today and will be used for many years to come. To maintain consistency in interoperability, preserving 
testing creation and sending of C-CDAs in § 170.315(b)(1) is important. To reduce burden, however, we suggest 
removing testing of § 170.315(e)(1) “download and transmit,” which includes over 50 different permutations of 
testing C-CDA creation and sending functionality. Removing § 170.315(e)(1) will reduce burden much more than 
removing § 170.315(b)(1) would. 
 
Within § 170.315(b)(1), we suggest that ASTP remove “(ii) Validate and display.” The functions in that section, 
including displaying errors in received documents or changing the display of each received document , are 
minimally used. Removing them will reduce the burden caused by testing.

9. § 170.315(b)(11) Decision support interventions  
 
We propose retaining § 170.315(a)(9) instead of removing it and revising § 170.315(b)(11). ASTP’s proposal would

Reverting to § 170.315(a)(9) seems to accomplish ASTP’s deregulatory goals while preserving source attribute s. 
 
We strongly support transparency around predictive decision support interventions (PDSI) and will continue to

However, it remains the case that the PDSI requirements have created significant confusion regarding the scope

We agree with ASTP’s assessment that end users rarely view or interact with source attributes in the system. 1 We

While it is sensible to remove a rarely used feature from certification, the industry should continue to focus on 
transparency for predictive decision support.

12. Privacy and Security Certification Criteria 
 
ASTP proposes to remove the privacy and security criteria from the Certification Program since these criteria 
impose unnecessary burden to demonstrate, provide limited practical assurance, and often generate confusion 
among stakeholders regarding what protections these criteria provide. We agree with ASTP’s approach to 
incorporate privacy and security requirements directly into use-case specific certification criteria such as has been

We favor retiring the current framework and incorporating relevant privacy and security requirements into use-
case specific certification criteria over ASTP’s proposed alternative of retaining only the § 170.315(d)(2) criterion. 
Audit events, like other privacy and security features, are best incorporated into use-case specific certification 
criteria rather than remaining in a standalone framework.

II. Information Blocking  
 
In the bipartisan 21st Century Cures Act, Congress expressly delegated to the Secretary of the Department of 
Health and Human Services (HHS) the authority to “identify reasonable and necessary activities that do not 
constitute information blocking [emphasis added].” In the HTI-2 Proposed Rule, ASTP proposed to codify certain 
practices as “interferences,” an essential element of information blocking. In our comments on the HTI-2 Proposed 
Rule, we raised a concern that ASTP’s proposal to define an essential element of what activities do constitute 
information blocking went far beyond the outer boundaries of its delegated authority from Congress. We 
appreciate that ASTP formally withdrew its proposal in its withdrawal notice on Dec. 29, 2025.2  
 
We caution that the preamble of the HTI-5 Proposed Rule again crosses the boundaries of ASTP’s authority when 
it states what “may implicate” or be “problematic” in relation to information blocking. In another instance, ASTP 
states certain practices “likely constitute an interference.” ASTP has authority only to determine reasonable and 
necessary practices that do not constitute information blocking; it was not given the authority to determine what 
activities do constitute information blocking.   
 
A. Regulatory Process 
 
ASTP’s proposed changes to information blocking requirements are not deregulatory and instead will increase 
burden on Certified Health IT developers. ASTP did not conduct a cost analysis of the proposed changes as 
required by Executive Orders (EO) 12866, 13563, and 14192; the Regulatory Flexibility Act; the Paperwork 
Reduction Act; the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act; Office of Management and Budget (OMB) guidance; and 
Supreme Court precedent. OMB should not grant expedited review, and ASTP should sever and withdraw its 
information blocking proposals. A new proposal should be made only if justified by rigorous cost-benefit analysis.  
 
1. Proposal is Not Deregulatory and Cost Analysis is Missing  
 
ASTP proposes new regulatory requirements for Certified Health IT developers and entirely fails to consider the 
costs – including the costs of compliance – of its proposed revisions to the information blocking regulations. 
Therefore, the calculation of total costs for the impact of the overall Proposed Rule is materially incomplete. ASTP 
states that it does “not expect costs to be associated with these deregulatory proposals.” 3 Further, it states that 
“No proposals create net new requirements or additional burden to comply with existing requirements.” 4 ASTP 
provides no support for its conclusion that there are no net-new requirements or additional burden and does not 
provide any reasons as to why it expects no costs associated with the information blocking section  of the HTI-5 
Proposed Rule.  
 
There are significant costs associated with the proposed increased regulatory requirements for the information 
blocking rules. Following the finalization of the initial information blocking regulations in 2020, regulated actors 
took affirmative steps to conform their business practices to what ASTP determined was “reasonable and 
necessary” (i.e., the regulatory exceptions). It follows that changes to these exceptions, particularly changes that 
narrow the scope of and even eliminate protected “reasonable and necessary” activities, will lead to significant 
costs, requiring regulated actors to review their current practices in light of the new requirements, potentially 
restructure contractual arrangements for tens or hundreds of counterparties, and make other costly operational 
changes to conform to the new rules.

Many changes proposed in the HTI-5 Proposed Rule will cause regulated actors to incur costs. The information 
blocking provisions increase compliance requirements and contribute to the “ever -expanding morass of 
complicated Federal regulation” that EO 14192 was meant to eliminate. Such changes include, but are not limited 
to, the following: 
 
• Proposals to narrow the application of the Manner Exception Exhausted Condition: The proposed 
revisions would increase the minimum number of alternative manners that an actor must offer a 
requestor. More significantly, the proposals to revise “substantial number” to “any” and remove “similarly 
situated” could require an actor to make non-standard manners of access available to any requestor if 
that manner has ever been provided to a single customer or third party. This disregards material and 
legitimate differences between differently situated requesters that affect feasibility, support burden, IP 
exposure, and other risks associated with specific manners of access to EHI. These changes increase the 
burden of using this exception, as it could require actors to consider whether any of the hundreds or 
thousands of solutions it offers to any number of customers could be considered “analogous” to the 
request.  
 
• Proposal to eliminate the Manner Exception Exhausted Condition: ASTP introduced this condition in direct 
response to actors’ concerns about the substantial technical and financial resources, including significant 
opportunity costs, of having to respond to demands for non-standard technology.5 Actors have been 
relying on this exception for more than two years to avoid this drain on resources.6 Eliminating the 
exception will force actors to incur the costs that the exception was designed to prevent.  
 
• Proposal to remove “contracts of adhesion” from protection under the Manner Requested Condition of 
the Manner Exception: Under the proposed revision, use of a standardized contract, which is otherwise 
legally enforceable, would render an actor ineligible for protection. Compliance with the condition would 
instead require individual, custom contracts for every request an actor agrees to fulfill in the manner 
requested. Further, the proposed revision lacks clarity as to when negotiation is considered sufficient to 
meet the condition, potentially adding to the length and complexity of negotiations. For health IT 
developers, the transaction cost would increase exponentially.

the negotiated price between physician and provider is fair market value and the negotiated price is not based on 
the volume or value of referrals and does not take into account other business generated between the parties. 7 
This fact-intensive work has spawned an entire industry, known as the “valuation community” 8 that inspects a vast 
multitude of payment arrangements between physicians and providers that dramatically increase costs and 
creates an additional layer of oversight and bureaucracy policing parties’ contractual arrangements. 9

2. ASTP Should Delete the Words “without limitation” From its Proposal 
 
While we have no inherent objection to automated technologies being able to access, exchange, or use EHI, there 
should be limitations. Health IT developers should be able to limit access, exchange, or use if it is reasonable and 
necessary to do so (i.e., an exception applies) or the developer’s IP is at stake. Further, if a healthcare provider opts 
to use this type of technology, health IT developers should be able to limit their liability when things go wrong. 
 
RPA solutions are inherently fragile and can lead to safety, data integrity, privacy, security, and performance issues. 
Compared to an API-based integration where both caller and callee agree on a specification , only the requestor 
understands how its RPA solution functions. There is risk that any changes to the actor’s underlying software could 
unintentionally cause the RPA to function in unexpected ways, potentially leading to data corruption or other 
issues. Over the years, we have witnessed health system disruptions due to RPA-caused corruption that have 
required hundreds of hours of Epic staff helping health systems clean up the problems. For example: 
 
• In just a matter of minutes, RPA set incorrect medication doses on nearly 73,000 medications, 
necessitating urgent cleaning of over 44,000 patient records. 
• An RPA solution added notes to the wrong patient roughly 90% of the time. The organization needed to 
urgently disable the solution and perform clean-up. The organization also had to evaluate if a patient saw 
other patients’ notes and consider patient breach notification, which included the added expense of hiring 
outside auditors and lawyers. 
• An organization needed to make an unexpected $1,000,000 investment in infrastructure to prevent system 
performance issues from a single RPA clinical documentation improvement solution. 
 
Even the government acknowledges the risk of automated access. As an example, Login.gov’s rules of use provide 
that “automated access to Login.gov is strictly prohibited to ensure security and integrity. This includes automated 
account creation, authentication, data scraping, form submissions, and circumventing security. Login.gov is for 
human interaction, and users must access it manually to maintain a secure environment.” 25 
  
ASTP should delete the term “without limitation.” It could be interpreted to mean that there are no reasonable 
limitations to the access or use of EHI by RPA or autonomous AI systems. While it may not be ASTP’s intent, the 
term “without limitation” is ambiguous as to whether a developer could make use of all the information blocking 
exceptions. With any manner, there are reasonable terms under which an actor can provision access or use. For 
example, the health IT developer should be able to require terms that make clear it is not responsible for mistakes 
that other developers’ automated technology makes. Further, health IT developers should be able to include terms 
that protect their IP, especially with the unique threats created by RPA and autonomous AI systems. Autonomous 
AI systems should not be permitted to train on, or retain, IP. Health IT developers should be allowed to require 
appropriate limitations, such as only using data and IP accessed for in-context learning, not exposing it to other 
parties, and not performing subsequent training of models on a developer’s software.

a. ASTP should not eliminate the condition.

flexibility for actors with multiple tools for access, exchange, or use of EHI to decide which tools actors scale to

intentions since the enactment of the 21st Century Cures Final Rule. These are among the most economically 
consequential interventions an agency can impose, and thus require clear statutory text evincing Congressional 
intent. In the rare scenarios where courts have permitted this kind or regulatory encroachment into the 
marketplace (such as the telecommunications or energy sectors), they have done so only when Congress expressly 
imposed those requirements in detailed statutory text, defining covered parties, scope, and procedural protections 
with specificity. The existence of those precise legislative mandates, such as 47 U.S.C. § 251 of the

2. The Definition of “Market Rate” Should Not Use the Stark Law Fair Market Value Definition 
 
ASTP proposes that the Manner Exception will not apply to any contract or agreement related to access, 
exchange, or use of EHI that is not “at market rate.” ASTP proposes to define “market rate” using paragraph (1) of 
the definition of “fair market value” found in the Stark Law regulations. Under the Stark Law, fair market value in 
paragraph (1) is the value in an arm's-length transaction, consistent with the general market value of the subject 
transaction. With respect to the purchase of an asset, general market value means the “price that an asset would 
bring on the date of acquisition of the asset as the result of bona fide bargaining between a well-informed buyer 
and seller that are not otherwise in a position to generate business for each other .” 
 
While we don’t object to market rate requirements to use the exception, ASTP should not use the Stark Law 
definition of fair market value. The Stark Law definition is ill-suited for the types of transactions ASTP seeks to 
regulate. By applying the Stark Law definition of fair market value and eliminating parties’ ability to consider 
“other business generated,” ASTP is chilling otherwise reasonable business practices. For instance, it is perfectly 
reasonable for an actor to charge a premium for its brand because of its reputation for providing a superior 
product or services, and for a buyer to pay that premium. The buyer may be about to generate more business by 
virtue of its association with the premium brand. This is free market economics. Under ASTP’s proposed definition, 
an actor could not take advantage of its premium brand and would have to be able to prove that it actively 
ensured that the price it negotiated did not take into account the buyer’s ability to generate business as a result of 
the transaction. 
 
The underlying policies behind the Stark Law and what is reasonable and necessary in health IT transactions that 
are not information blocking are totally unrelated. The Stark Law fair market value definition is based on the 
premise that parties in a position to generate business for one another are “interested parties” and any agreement 
between them does not, by definition, reflect fair market value. 26 Courts interpreting the Stark Law’s fair market 
value standard have reasoned that interested parties can utilize negotiated agreements as a way to disguise 
excessive payments, with an unspoken understanding that these excessive payments are provided in ex change for 
a steady stream of referrals.27 In other words, it assumes that the subject transaction—the payment to the 
physician for a particular service from the entity to which the physician sends business —will be used as a vehicle 
to make excess payments for the purpose of inducing referrals of business. The policy concern is that these “extra” 
payments will skew clinical decisionmaking and financially incentivize the payee to make unnecessary referrals of 
services that are paid for by federal healthcare programs. While the Stark Law’s fair market value definition might 
have a place regarding physician referrals, it has no place in company-to-company free market negotiations where 
no clinical referrals are being made.

“market rate” proposed to be required to qualify for the Manner Exception. The Stark Law definition of fair market 
value was supplied by Congress and drafted specifically to ensure that the payments exchanged between

3. ASTP Should Delete the Proposed Prohibition of Contracts of Adhesion Element From The Manner 
Exception 
 
ASTP should permit standard contracts as long as terms are not unconscionable and there are market-based fees. 
Contracts of adhesion, or standardized contracts, are generally frowned upon when they contain unconscionable 
terms. When standardized contracts do not contain unconscionable terms, they allow contracting at scale in an 
efficient and timely manner. Indeed, the federal government is the world’s largest buyer of goods and services and 
routinely uses contracts built on standardized clauses. This is important because of the significant scale required—
in 2025, Epic executed over 6,000 consultant and outsourcer agreements and over 300 new vendors enrolled in 
our Vendor Services developer program.  
 
Further, if ASTP maintains that standardized contracts are not permitted, ASTP has not provided any clarity on 
what level of negotiation is “sufficient.” This creates new regulatory uncertainty to determine whether an actor 
engaged in sufficient negotiation. Many actors will maintain firm commercial positions on key areas in their 
contracts. A counterparty’s dissatisfaction with these terms should not render the actor ineligible for reliance on 
the Manner Exception unless the terms are unconscionable. However, ASTP’s proposal forces an actor to guess 
whether it has provided sufficient opportunity to negotiate and encourages parties who are dissatisfied with 
commercially reasonable contract terms to express their unhappiness as frivolous information blocking complaints. 
 
Finally, it is impossible to reconcile this prohibition with the transparency conditions under the API Condition of 
Certification. Those transparency conditions require certified API developers to publish all terms and conditions 
for certified API technology via a publicly accessible hyperlink. That is only possible if a Certified health IT 
Developer can offer that technology under standardized terms. 
 
E.  Removal of TEFCA Manner Exception 
 
Instead of proposing to eliminate the TEFCA Manner Exception, ASTP should adjust the exception to give small 
provider practices and less-resourced organizations, including Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHC) and 
CAHs a regulatory incentive to join TEFCA. In the past, these less-resourced organizations often did not participate 
in nationwide interoperability frameworks, such as Carequality. Without incentives  to join TEFCA, the patients they 
serve will not benefit from a nationwide interoperability floor.  
 
Specifically, ASTP should: (1) limit the exception to rural health clinics, CAHs, and providers located in a rural or 
health professional shortage area;33 (2) eliminate the condition that the exception only applies if both the actor 
and the requester participate in TEFCA; and (3) remove the requirement that the requestor is capable of EHI 
exchange via TEFCA (because that condition creates a perverse incentive to not support TEFCA). 
 
TEFCA has achieved early momentum. At ASTP’s annual meeting in 2026, the TEFCA community celebrated the 
milestone of more than 500 million documents exchanged. Compared to Carequality, which took twelve years to

applications are not regulated by the information blocking rules. The risk is compounded by the recent TEFCA 
anti-bidirectional exchange policy, where applications are not required to respond to data requests on TEFCA.

providers, including those who use Epic, an expanded exchange network with organizations who currently do not
