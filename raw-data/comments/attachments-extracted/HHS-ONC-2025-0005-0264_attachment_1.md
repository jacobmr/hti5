On behalf of the Alliance for Health Information Operations and Standards (AHIOS), we appreciate the
opportunity to submit comments on the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy and the
Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology’s (ASTP/ONC) proposed rule, Health
Data, Technology, and Interoperability (HTI-5): Deregulatory Actions to Unleash Prosperity.  
AHIOS' mission is to strengthen and enhance health information management and access while promoting
excellence in the exchange of confidential patient data. Member companies are dedicated to improving
the efficiency, effectiveness, and security of health information exchange solutions and are responsible
for providing critical health information access directly to patients, to their providers, and to authorized
third parties. We believe that access to comprehensive health information is foundational to a modern
patient-centered health system.
Our member organizations serve most healthcare provider organizations in the country. Because the
Release of Information (ROI) process is complex, and the liability risks are substantial, many hospitals and
health care facilities outsource this function to AHIOS member companies to minimize administrative
burden and maintain legal and regulatory compliance. Thus, AHIOS is uniquely situated to speak to the
operational aspects of facilitating timely and appropriate access to a patient’s protected health
information, while ensuring security and privacy consistent with state and federal regulations.  
AHIOS appreciates ASTP/ONC’s continued commitment to advancing interoperability, patient access, and
innovation while reducing unnecessary regulatory burden. We are broadly supportive of the deregulatory
direction of HTI-5, particularly where it promotes clarity, flexibility, and proportionality in the regulation
of health information exchange. At the same time, we urge ASTP/ONC to ensure that the HTI-5 Proposed
Rule remains consistent with the Congressional intent of the 21st Century Cures Act to empower patients,
foster competition and innovation, and enable secure access, exchange, and use of electronic health
information (EHI) without undermining patient privacy rights.

Health IT Certification Program  
In general, AHIOS supports ASTP/ONC’s proposal to remove, consolidate, or descope certification criteria
and maintenance requirements that have proven to be duplicative, low-value, or operationally
burdensome. Our members work closely with hospitals and providers across care settings and routinely
observe the strain that layered certification and reporting requirements place on smaller and resource-
constrained organizations.
We particularly support proposals to:
 Reduce certification criteria that no longer align with modern, API-based interoperability;
 Eliminate outdated concepts and redundant requirements; and
 Descope reporting obligations which provide limited actionable insight while imposing significant
compliance cost.
AHIOS members appreciate the Administration’s continued commitment to modernizing the health IT
regulatory framework and reducing unnecessary administrative burden across the health care ecosystem.
Consistent with this approach, our members generally support thoughtful deregulation and efforts to
streamline certification requirements. However, we are concerned about the substantial reduction of
privacy and security certification criteria, including the proposed removal of key safeguards within the
Privacy and Security Certification Framework. Although the HIPAA Privacy and Security Rules provide a
foundational framework, they do not offer sufficiently specific technical direction to certified health IT
developers regarding how information collected, maintained, and exchanged through their systems
should be protected in practice. Historically, certification criteria have supplied this needed specificity by
establishing clear, uniform expectations for authentication, auditability, and related safeguards.
Eliminating these criteria risks introducing inconsistent security practices across certified systems,
increasing uncertainty for providers and technology partners, and shifting additional due-diligence,
liability, and associated compliance and documentation burdens to downstream disclosure
intermediaries, including AHIOS members.
At the same time, HTI-5’s broader push toward FHIR-enabled interoperability and AI-supported data
exchange could accelerate electronic record retrieval and automation within ROI operations, creating
efficiency gains but also heightening exposure to breach risk, misdirected disclosures, and audit
complexity in the absence of standardized guardrails.  
Taken together, these changes create a growing regulatory gap between outcome-based HIPAA
obligations and the retreat of prescriptive ONC certification controls, underscoring the need for targeted
retention of audit, authentication, and disclosure-integrity protections to preserve program integrity and
trust in health-information exchange.
Accordingly, AHIOS recommends that ASTP/ONC at a minimum retain authentication and auditability
certification criteria—even if other lower-value privacy and security requirements are removed—and
consider establishing a transitional compliance attestation requirement to ensure that certified health IT
developers maintain baseline security assurances during the transition away from prescriptive
certification controls.

AHIOS encourages ASTP/ONC to continue evaluating certification requirements through a real-world

APIs, Automation, and Artificial Intelligence (AI)-Enabled Access
AHIOS supports ASTP/ONC’s emphasis on FHIR-based APIs and the proposal to explicitly recognize that
access, exchange, and use of EHI may occur through automated means, including robotic process
automation and emerging AI systems. Clarifying that automation is within the scope of these definitions
provides needed regulatory certainty as health information workflows evolve.
However, AHIOS strongly urges ASTP/ONC to maintain flexibility in how covered entities and business
associates select and implement API solutions. While certification and standards adoption should remain
voluntary as a matter of law, AHIOS remains concerned that cumulative regulatory pressure across HHS
rules may create expectations that providers rely exclusively on certified technologies or specific exchange
frameworks, limiting competition and innovation.
Additionally, AHIOS reiterates that security, authorization, and privacy guardrails must remain central to
API-enabled exchange. Patients frequently do not fully understand the downstream privacy implications
of authorizing EHI transmission to non-HIPAA-covered third parties. Policies that accelerate API adoption
should continue to recognize the essential role of intermediaries, such as ROI vendors, in ensuring patient
intent, minimum necessary standards, and lawful disclosure.
At the same time, AHIOS believes API policy must more directly address real-world performance, data
usability, and patient protection. Current API certification standards verify the existence and
responsiveness of APIs but fall short in ensuring the quality, usability, completeness, and consistency of
the data returned, undermining interoperability at the point of care and often placing undue burden on
patients and providers to reconcile fragmented or incomplete medical histories. The availability of APIs
likewise does not guarantee meaningful data access for treatment or care coordination, and APIs alone
do not ensure HIPAA-aligned privacy protections once information leaves covered entities.  
To improve API performance while preserving patient trust, AHIOS recommends advancing outcome-
based interoperability, comprehensive data access, real-time exchange, stronger enforcement of API
access requirements, and enhanced privacy, identity verification, and minimum-necessary protections.
Together, these steps would help ensure that expanded API adoption delivers meaningful interoperability
supported by strong security and privacy safeguards.
Decision Support Interventions (DSI) and AI Transparency
AHIOS notes that HTI-5 proposes to scale back the Decision Support Interventions (DSI) certification
criterion, including the removal of AI transparency requirements such as source attributes, risk
management documentation, and model card disclosures. While AHIOS appreciates the Administration’s
interest in reducing prescriptive requirements that may lack demonstrated clinical utility, we urge
ASTP/ONC to retain baseline AI transparency and auditability standards. As AI-enabled tools become
increasingly integrated into health information workflows—including record retrieval, disclosure
management, and data reconciliation—the ability to audit algorithmic outputs, understand data

Information Blocking
AHIOS appreciates ASTP/ONC’s efforts in HTI-5 to refine and clarify the information blocking framework,
including proposals to remove or revise exceptions that have proven susceptible to misuse.
In particular, we call out two proposed changes to infeasibility exceptions:
Third-party modification use condition.  
ASTP proposes to remove this condition because it believes it has been misused. AHIOS appreciates ASTP’s
clarification of the use of these exceptions as well as the use of exceptions prioritizing security and
preventing patient harm.  
The proposed removal of the third-party seeking modification use condition within the infeasibility
exception would have meaningful implications for AHIOS members that function as ROI intermediaries
and compliance stewards for covered entities. Historically, this condition provided a narrow pathway to
deny or limit access where a requesting third party sought the ability to modify EHI within certified health
IT in ways that could create integrity, liability, or governance concerns. Eliminating this condition signals
a policy shift toward broader downstream permissibility of EHI use and integration, thereby reducing the
discretion historically exercised by intermediaries to manage risk associated with unfamiliar or competing
third-party actors.  
Manner exception exhausted condition.  
ASTP proposes to significantly tighten, or possibly eliminate, the manner exception exhausted condition
of the infeasibility exception, because it believes the condition is being misused to block access to existing
APIs and other interoperable methods. AHIOS supports the intent of these proposed changes to prevent
misuse of the exception as a tool for anticompetitive behavior or unjustified access denials but would
caution against revisions that reduce flexibility for covered entities and business associates who must
operationally safeguard PHI and comply with HIPAA and state law. ROI workflows and third-party access
requests are fundamentally different from developer-to-developer interoperability, and the rule should
not unintentionally force unsafe or impractical access pathways.
AHIOS members frequently manage complex, non-standard, or resource-intensive disclosure requests
that require manual processing, customized formatting, or reconciliation across disparate source systems.
Under the current framework, the ability to demonstrate that reasonable alternative manners were
offered, and that agreement could not be reached, has served as an important safeguard against
operationally infeasible or disproportionate requests. We would request ASTP clarify intent when they
state that this change in the manner exception exhausted condition would continue to allow actors to
deny requests that are unsafe, unlawful, or genuinely infeasible.  
AHIOS urges ASTP/ONC to continue emphasizing that information blocking determinations must account
for the reasonableness of an actor’s practices in light of privacy, security, and legal obligations. Overly

TEFCA-Related Proposals
AHIOS supports the proposal to remove the TEFCA Manner Exception and to eliminate subpart D of 45
CFR part 171. TEFCA was intended by Congress to be a voluntary framework, not an exclusive or preferred
pathway for all health information exchange. Removing a narrowly tailored exception that has created
confusion or unintended market distortion aligns with TEFCA’s maturation and continued private-sector
investment.
AHIOS emphasizes that removing this exception should not be interpreted as diminishing the importance
of TEFCA, but rather as reinforcing that TEFCA must coexist alongside other lawful, fit-for-purpose
exchange mechanisms that serve patients and providers.
Conclusion
AHIOS appreciates ASTP/ONC’s thoughtful approach in HTI-5 to recalibrate federal health IT policy toward
clarity, proportionality, and innovation. Our comments reflect decades of experience balancing the
imperative for access with the equally critical obligation to protect patient privacy and maintain public
trust. Our members support the modernization of certification criteria, the advancement of FHIR-based
interoperability, and the refinement of the information blocking framework, including removal of the
TEFCA Manner Exception consistent with TEFCA’s voluntary design. At the same time, we urge ASTP/ONC
to retain targeted safeguards—particularly authentication, auditability, and AI transparency
requirements—that protect program integrity without imposing disproportionate compliance burden.
AHIOS stands ready to serve as a resource and operational partner as ASTP/ONC finalizes this important
rule.
