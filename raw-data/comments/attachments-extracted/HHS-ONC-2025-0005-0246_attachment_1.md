Burden Reduction of Certification Criteria Revisions & Removals

Altera commends ASTP/ONC on efforts to reduce burden for health IT vendors and other stakeholders
of the certification program, including clinicians and other staff of provider organizations whose user
experience of their health information technology solutions is dictated in part by the certification
requirements. Altera independently and through the comments submitted by the Electronic Health
Record Association submitted feedback to several different Requests for Information that sought ideas
for the most effective deregulatory options available to ASTP/ONC, CMS, the CDC, the FDA and HHS
more broadly, and we appreciate that several of our ideas are reflected in the proposed rule.

In evaluating the HTI-5 NPRM, we have determined that several of the proposals to remove certification
criteria will result in a positive or neutral impact on burden, and we applaud those actions. However, the
language describing the removal of some certification criteria overstates the burden reduction impacts,
as many will not reduce any burden; some of the proposals would likely increase burden, in fact. Below
are our high-level observations, to be followed by additional supporting detail.

Impact of Certification Criteria Removal/Revisions on CMS Promoting Interoperability Program
Participants

Altera has two areas of concern related to the touchpoints between the ASTP/ONC certification criteria
and the CMS Eligible Hospital/Critical Access Hospital and Eligible Clinician Promoting Interoperability
reporting/performance (PI) programs.

First, Altera has great concerns about the impact of the removal and revision of some certification
criteria on participants in the PI programs. It is unclear from the HTI-5 proposed rule whether
ASTP/ONC has collaborated closely enough with CMS on the changes that, if or when finalized, will
impact Promoting Interoperability measures for hospitals and providers.

The following CMS Promoting Interoperability measures relying upon certification criteria that are under
discussion here.

Second, Altera has concerns about the likely impacts for our clients who participate in the CMS
Promoting Interoperability programs. ASTP/ONC has proposed that the effective dates for the removals
and revisions of certification criteria would be the effective date of the final rule or January 1, 2027
(called out as needed for transition time). Neither date provides CMS with adequate time to determine if
PI measures require changes and or updates and for health IT vendors in turn to support those required
changes. If changes are required, which is likely, it would be nearly impossible for health IT vendors to
have the changes in place by the 1/1/2027 start of the 2027 PI reporting/performance year. That would
result in significant additional burden for hospitals and providers participating in these programs.  
Further, if any changes result in updates to the PI measure calculations, the process for how and when
the technical specifications will be available to health IT vendors has not been established. It is unlikely
all could take place in enough time prior to 1/1/2027 for health IT vendors to deliver updates to clients
prior to the start of the reporting/performance year. More time is necessary.

Impact of These Proposals Associated with New Market Entrants

Altera has concerns about inadvertent risks to both patient safety and the efficiency of health data
exchange that could stem from some of ASTP/ONC’s proposals. ASTP/ONC asserts in the NPRM that
certified solutions will continue investment in and maintenance of functionality and processes that will no
longer be required because they are widely adopted. However, any vendor who chooses not to allocate
the necessary resources to those steps, who doesn’t modernize previously certified functionality or
foregoes safety steps they’ve previously attested to, will risk introduction of failures into the system as a
whole. Other software that does not make those choices to scale back still has to exchange data
successfully with that EHR even if it becomes more inefficient or places more burden on the two
connected provider organizations. Additionally, the average provider will likely be entirely unaware of
changes made by their software partner to functionality or processes they rely on to protect the safety of
their patients.

Additionally, the removal of such a large number of certification criteria, many of which are fundamental
to standards-based data capture and exchange, could mean that new health IT vendor entrants to the
market simply never incorporate important functionality into their products or choose to take proprietary,
non-standards-based approaches within their new offerings. This could again pose risks to both
successful information exchange efforts between different EHRs and the ability to rely on the data
captured in the patient’s record, and we also note that some of the criteria proposed for removal are
critical to the capture of countable events for calculation of the PI program measure reports.

An example of this is exhibited by the Support Electronic Referral by Sending Health Information
measure that is included in the PI program. When this measure was created, it was widely based on
sending C-CDAs via Direct messaging. If a new EHR market entrant is not required to support the C-
CDA format during certification or name a relied-upon technology during that process, the score for a PI
program hospital or provider/practice participant could be negatively impacted and mean incurring the
Medicare payment penalty or a decrease in reimbursement. The reality is that it is unclear that the
average small provider practice would know enough about the difference between what can confidently
be expected from a system that has been certified for 15 years vs. a new EHR offering assurances but
not the functionality previously required by certification

It is also unclear if a new market entrant would know enough (if the criterion is removed) about the real-
world creation of a C-CDA and what has been required in the g.1./g.2.report to make the connection
successfully. For example, Altera uses the Direct Message Disposition Notification statuses to identify
successful transmissions of a C-CDA to the next provider of care. CMS has said that the C-CDA can
come from any source, but it is still frequently not possible to get back a confirmation of successful
receipt of a C-CDA a provider sends to an HIE today. Most do not provide MDN statuses, nor do they
provide any type of notification of receipt of a C-CDA. A new entrant would not know those dynamics or
how to best support their clients in that environment, but keeping the requirement for g.1 / g.2 would
alleviate some of the challenges.

EHR users (or of other health IT) who lack these widely adopted, standards-based interoperability
functionalities would possibly be unable to participate in the current flow of patient health information
between providers, and the information exchanged could potentially require significantly more effort to
harmonize the data that is exchanged. This doesn’t just raise concerns about exchange between
providers but also other stakeholders who rely on standardized data availability, such as laboratories.
While we recognize that ASTP/ONC is paving a path to much wider adoption of FHIR APIs for data
sharing and a move away from C-CDA reliance, it is clear that that transition is going to take time and
requires continued attention. New EHRs who don’t include the capacity to exchange a C-CDA would be
significantly impaired in their ability to participate in the data exchange ecosystem. Additionally, we

Information Blocking Proposed Changes

The vast majority of changes that ASTP/ONC proposes specific to Information Blocking terms,
definitions, and requirements are not deregulatory and not only don’t reduce burden but actually add it.
We recognize the criticality of public policy specific to information blocking, but ASTP/ONC still has an
obligation to make full efforts to honestly assess the impact of their proposals and conduct Economic
Impact Analysis everywhere that it should be done. That requires additional work in this case.

III.A.5.a. 170.315(e)(1) View, Download & Transmit to 3rd Party
• Health IT developers and other health programs or activities who receive Federal financial
assistance under the Certification Program or other federal programs likely qualify as covered
entities under Section 1557 and would be required to comply with applicable accessibility
requirements.  
• Under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, recipients of financial assistance from HHS must
conform to certain accessibility requirements, including but not limited to using the WCAG 2.1 AA
success criteria for web content and mobile applications, as detailed in 45 CFR 84.82 through 84.89.  
• Additionally, under Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act (Pub. L. 93-112; Sep. 26, 1973), HHS must
ensure the information and communication technology it develops, procures, maintains, or uses,
allows comparable access and use for people with disabilities, including by conforming to the
success criteria of WCAG 2.0 AA.  
• See 29 U.S.C. 794d; 36 CFR part 1194.  
• As such, we expect health IT developers will continue to meet these requirements and provide the
necessary accessibility within their products certified to the VDT certification criterion.

Altera is concerned that ONC’s proposal to remove the explicit WCAG 2.0 accessibility
standard from 170.315(e)(1) may create ambiguity for developers and providers. While
ONC notes that other federal statutes (e.g., Section 1557, Section 504, and Section 508)
continue to require WCAG-level accessibility, the proposed rule removes the only
certification-based mechanism for verifying conformance.

• We also propose to remove the VDT requirement in § 170.315(e)(1)(ii)(A)(2) to conform with any
Network Time Protocol (NTP) standard for purposes of “date and time each action occurred” under
the activity log.  
• In the HTI-1 Final Rule, we revised the requirement from conformance with the Network Time
Protocol v4 of the RFC 5905 standard to compliance with any Network Time Protocol standard (89
FR 1283; § 170.210(g)).  
• As a result, health IT could be certified by demonstrating that it could use any NTP to synchronize
clocks for time accuracy.
• However, we have received feedback over the years from developers stating that the requirement
should not be part of certification because the functionality was not native to the EHR technology
(rather part of the operating system within which the EHR technology runs) and was unnecessary for

First, we believe implying abuse of this Exception is inappropriately accusatory without

In terms of the suggested changes to the Manner Exception Exhausted option, Altera
cautiously supports – only with suggested amendments – the first proposal regarding the
revision of the “Manner Exception Exhausted” condition. We certainly oppose removing
the exception altogether. However, ASTP/ONC still significantly misunderstands the work
that must go into the process that leads to application of the Manner Exception even
generally, much less the Manner Exception Exhausted – clearly evidenced by the lack of
an Economic Impact Analysis specific to the proposed added burden in the proposal.

First, ASTP/ONC suggests changing certain terms to less definitive ones that will
introduce ambiguity to both negotiations and the ability to investigate any concerns about
information blocking. “Analogous” here seems to be poorly defined and unevenly applied
in the proposed rule, and moving from “substantial number” to “any” is a very significant
proposed change.

Second, the NPRM suggests a highly prescriptive approach (certainly not deregulatory or
aiming to reduce burden) and does not reflect the reality of how the process can most
efficiently work for both parties. In reality, it usually works best for both parties to discuss
all alternative options to meet the goals of the original request simultaneously, not one by
one. The idea of a prescribed order to the conversation with option B not allowed to be
discussed until option A is checked off is highly impractical, and to have to follow what
was suggested here in the NPRM would add measurable length to a process already
constrained by the 10-day Infeasibility Exception clock.

that, while not intended by the 21st Century Cures law, legal actions between private
parties based on or including allegations of information blocking are increasing in
