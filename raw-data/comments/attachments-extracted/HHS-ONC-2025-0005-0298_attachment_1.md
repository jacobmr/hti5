The Center for AI and Digital Policy (CAIDP) Comments to the Health and Human Services Department on ASTP/ONC Deregulatory Actions To Unleash Prosperity February 27, 2026 We write from the Center for AI and Digital Policy (“CAIDP”) in response to the Proposed Rule on Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability: ASTP/ONC Deregulatory Actions To Unleash Prosperity (“Proposed Rule”).1 The Proposed Rule is issued pursuant to Executive Order 141922 to “significantly reduce the private expenditures required to comply with Federal regulations to secure America's economic prosperity and national security and the highest possible quality of life for each citizen.”3 Specifically the HHS is issuing the proposed rule “in an effort to streamline and reduce administrative burdens on health care providers, health information technology (IT) developers, and the health IT community overall.”4 CAIDP’s comments are directed to those aspects of the proposed rule that are deregulatory to enable acceleration of AI deployment in the healthcare system. CAIDP reinforces that deregulation to reduce administrative burdens should not eliminate or roll back guardrails required to preserve equitable access to healthcare, privacy and security protections, and accessibility centered design. Our comments in brief are: 1. HHS should preserve AI transparency requirements including current enforcement of model card protocols 2. HHS should preserve the privacy and security certification criteria provided under § 170.315(d) and § 170.550(h) 3. HHS should preserve accessibility-centered design requirements, maintaining enforcement mechanisms and legal incentives for IT developers to uphold fair practices 4. HHS should ensure requirements for representative datasets for AI training data 5. About CAIDP The Center for AI and Digital Policy (CAIDP) CAIDP is a non-profit, independent research and education organization based in Washington D.C. with a global network of AI experts, policy specialists, and human rights advocates.5 CAIDP provides expert guidance on AI guardrails, law and regulations to national governments and international organizations, including US federal agencies and Congress.6 Overview of Recent Agency Actions regarding AI in Healthcare In January 2024, HHS published a final rule – HTI-17 aiming to increase algorithmic transparency by revising the Certification Program criterion. Aligning with the FAVES AI principles (Fair, Appropriate, Valid, Effective, Safe), HTI-1 requires HIT modules to disclose “particular source attributes, or categories of technical performance and quality information” to improve transparency and explainability of the data being used. Moreover, HTI-1 requires developers to keep source attributes updated and to implement risk management practices. In all, HTI-1’s primary mission is to maintain public trust in new AI technologies in the health care industry so that the benefits of these technologies may expand in safer, more appropriate, and more equitable ways. The HTI-1 final rule was aimed at enabling AI adoption with guardrails by introducing baseline transparency obligations for predictive decision support interventions (DSIs) which enabled clinicians and health systems to assess AI tools’ performance and fairness before use. The implementation of source attribute requirements for DSIs (Decision Support Interventions) directly affects how AI tools are disclosed, evaluated, and trusted in clinical workflows. It was critical in building trust and reliability between system developers, vendors, and practitioners.16 It requires disclosure of – who developed the algorithm (organization or vendor), the data sources used to train and test it, the purpose and intended use, known risks, limitations, and bias considerations. It enables health systems assess whether the model is appropriate for their patient population. The documentation of specific DSI purposes and risks in “plain language,” lowers transaction friction and clarifies expectations for AI vendors. While the proposed rule presumes that innovation might be hampered by these transparency and reliability requirements, a recent survey suggests that this gap could be addressed through the provision of complementary tools, clarification of regulations, and the development of “efficient project plans to meet deadlines.”17 Furthermore, HHS should not expand AI Access to health data without corresponding safeguards (§171.102 Definitions). HHS should preserve the privacy and security certification criteria provided under § 170.315(d) and § 170.550(h) The Privacy and Security Certification Framework was applicable for AI embedded in EHR systems. The technical safeguards required by the framework included access control, audit logging, authentication and authorization, integrity controls, encryption, end-user device security, and accounting of disclosures. These safeguards are crucial for vendors to ensure appropriate risk management practices, address vulnerabilities, and provide timely updates. Crucially elimination of the Privacy and Security Certification Framework is likely to render many health IT systems non-compliant with HIPAA.20 For AI-embedded EHR systems the Privacy and Security Framework in HTI-1 builds upon the HIPAA security requirement for regular risk analyses.21 Most significantly, without the technical safeguards vendors and healthcare systems will be less prepared to monitor, report, and respond to breaches under the HIPAA breach notification requirements.22 3. HHS should ensure requirements for representative datasets for AI training data Ensuring demographically representative samples are provided to AI models is crucial in providing positive and unbiased health care decisions. Currently, these tools often perform best for populations and conditions represented in their training data, which may exclude racial or ethnic minorities or rare medical conditions. CAIDP has previously cautioned the NIH against using non-representative training data, i.e. “datasets that underrepresent certain populations (e.g., racial minorities, women, rural patients), leading to poor model performance for those groups.”26 Models trained on data that is not reflective of the treatment population will have discriminatorily dispersed consequences. This is clearly exhibited through the AI-based melanoma prediction models that were trained largely on light-skinned images which performed disproportionately worse when predicting melanoma on darker skin.27 The Proposed Rule purports the removal or modification of data elements pertaining to sex, sexual orientation, and gender identity. Conversely, CAIDP urges HHS to ensure data sets are constructed with diverse, properly deidentified data for AI model training. The HHS should prioritize safety-enhanced design practices by maintaining §170.315(g)(3) and user-centered design (UCD) practices It is unclear whether the proposed deregulation of user-centered design practices will yield benefits compared to costs. The HHS cost-efficiency reasoning for removing the safety-enhanced design criteria in § 170.315(g)(3) is not user-centric contrary to the departments own AI strategy and is likely to conflict with FDA’s human factors guidance (for AI-enabled medical devices).29 §170.315(g)(3) functions to reduce workflow failures (e.g. confusing risk scores) that minimizes patient safety risks which is essential for scalable, real-world clinical adoption. Moreover, rolling back these requirements would raise risks for healthcare providers considering the growing malpractice and liability concerns around AI-assisted care. A 2024 report from the American Medical Association revealed that 66% of U.S. physicians incorporated AI tools into their practices, nearly doubling the figure from 2023.30 A 2025 study further concluded that 65.8% of respondents lacked trust in their healthcare provider’s responsible use of AI, and 57.7% were uncertain that their health system could adequately protect them from potential AI-related harms.31 Therefore, CAIDP recommends that HHS preserve safety and UCD practices and the continued enforcement of thorough user testing prior to the public deployment of systems. We hope you consider our views and look forward to further engagement in HHS’ processes.
