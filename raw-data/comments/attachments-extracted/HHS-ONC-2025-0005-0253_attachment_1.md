Removing Certification for Health Information Technology  
The Certification Program for Health Information Technology has played a pivotal role in accelerating the nationwide adoption of 
electronic health records (EHRs), benefiting both organizations and clinicians. Since 2010, ASTP has advanced technology expectations 
by building on the framework established through Meaningful Use and the incentives for adopting certified EHR systems. These efforts 
have effectively moved the nation from a “gravel road” to a modern, interoperable “highway,” enabling patients across the country to 
experience the benefits of improved data exchange and coordinated care. 
 
However, the proposal to remove many foundational certification criteria raises significant concerns. The proposed rule states: “We do not 
anticipate that the proposed removals or revisions would alter behavior across product implementations by health IT developers for the capabilities that have thus 
far been included as part of certification criteria in the Certification Program. We have observed over time that certification is likely no longer a primary factor 
driving improvements or compliance in particular areas, such as with respect to privacy and security” (60973) of NPRM. This assumption may not hold 
true across the entire vendor landscape. 
 
Certified EHR Technology (CEHRT) establishes a core set of capabilities that EHR systems must deliver to support CMS program 
requirements and ensure interoperability through consistent standards. The proposed approach presumes that all EHR developers will 
continue to build and maintain these baseline capabilities voluntarily. The concern is that without required certification, vendors may 
diverge in ways that leave the broader market fragmented—where innovation advances for some, but not uniformly across the nation. 
 
If developers are no longer obligated to meet these baseline requirements, critical functionalities may be inconsistently implemented or 
omitted entirely. This would undermine interoperability and jeopardize continuity of care. Essential capabilities—such as family health 
history, implantable device lists, audit logs, automatic access time-out, end-user device encryption, data integrity protections, automated 
numerator and measure calculation, safety-enhanced design, quality management systems, C-CDA support, and Direct Project-based 
exchange—remain vital for patient treatment, privacy, safety,  and standards for each are critical for reliable data sharing. Presuming that 
these capabilities will automatically appear simply because USCDI v3 requires them may be shortsighted. USCDI v3 establishes the 
minimum expected capabilities for standardized data exchange, while still allowing the exchange of additional information beyond that 
baseline. ASTP’s approach assumes that clinicians will understand what USCDI v3 requires and will proactively demand these capabilities 
from their EHR vendors. ASTP is trusting the minimum data classes and elements will be supported, however this does not guarantee 
that vendors will implement the full set of corresponding functionalities without certification reinforcing that expectation.   
 
A few examples from the list:  
 
1. Family History: With genetics moving forward family history becomes more and more important in realizing preventative 
treatment instead of reactive treatment, saving payers and the government money.  
2. Implantable Devices: Capturing this information can be crucial to a patient’s health and safety. Though it is noted in the NPRM 
a standard still needs defined noting this identifier in more than one place to prevent the safety from being overlooked is worth 
the effort. To overlook this means a patient could be ill, die, and additional unnecessary cost reflected by insurance.  
3. Audit Logs: The question should not be whether audit logging ought to be required, but rather how accessible, complete, and 
traceable these logs must be. Assuming that all vendors will implement robust, usable audit logs consistently—and without 
certification ensuring parity—invites variability and potentially significant gaps in accountability and patient safety. 
4. Encryption: Encryption should be required—and certified—across multiple components of the health IT ecosystem. Early in 
the Meaningful Use era, organizations incurred penalties when a laptop was stolen because it lacked basic encryption protections 
that should be in place and verified.  HIPAA mandates strong privacy and security safeguards, and it is ASTP’s responsibility to 
ensure vendors build systems that meet those standards rather than relying on optional or uneven implementation. 
 
If these functions are expected to be present in all EHRs, then vendors are expected to build them. Removing the certification element

does not decrease burden of building and if not mandated the compliance may be questionable providing room for some to not possess 
the basic capabilities present today.  Removing these criteria could enable “innovative cowboy” EHR developers to determine 
individually what is or is not necessary, compromising consistency in patient care and interoperability. While some removals may be 
reasonable, the capabilities listed above are foundational and should remain required to ensure a dependable national health information 
infrastructure. 
 
 
Applaud developer relief  
The ASTP deserves recognition for reducing vendor burden by eliminating the requirements for Real World Testing and Insights. These 
changes meaningfully reduce both time and cost pressures while allowing vendors to focus their resources on near-term FHIR API 
development as well as longer-term innovation.
