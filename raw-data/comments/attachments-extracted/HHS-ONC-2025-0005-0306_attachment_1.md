Dear Assistant Secretary and ONC Leadership:

I respectfully submit this comment on the proposed rule, “Health Data,  
Technology,

and

Interoperability:

ASTP/ONC

Deregulatory

Actions

to

Unleash

Prosperity”

(RIN

0955-AA09).

I write as both a board member of The Light Collective, which advances  
ethical

and

patient-centered

digital

health

policy,

and

as

a

patient

living

with

a

rare

and

complex

health

condition

shaped

by

fragmented

data

systems

and

inequities

in

care.

Through

my

work

advancing

patient-centered

approaches

to

AI

governance

in

health

care,

I

have

focused

on

ensuring

that

innovation

is

paired

with

transparency,

equity,

and

accountability—principles

that

inform

the

perspectives

oﬀered

below.

I support modernization of interoperability infrastructure, including  
FHIR-based

exchange

and

eﬀorts

to

reduce

unnecessary

administrative

burden.

However,

modernization

should

not

weaken

the

transparency,

security,

and

equity

safeguards

that

sustain

patient

trust—especially

as

automation

and

AI

become

embedded

in

certiﬁed

health

IT.

My comments address:  
(1) revisions to deﬁnitions under 45 CFR Part 171; (2) changes to the  
Infeasibility

(§

171.201)

and

Manner

(§

171.301)

Exceptions;

(3)

proposed

revisions

to

the

Decision

Support

Interventions

criterion

(§

170.315(b)(11));

and

(4)

removal

of

certain

privacy,

security,

and

accessibility

certiﬁcation

criteria.

I. Automated “Access,” “Use,” and “Exchange” (45 CFR Part 171)

I support clarifying that “access,” “use,” and “exchange” include automated  
and

AI-enabled

systems.

Regulatory

deﬁnitions

should

reﬂect

API-driven,

system-to-system

interoperability.

However, expanding automation increases the scale and speed of data  
ﬂows

and

reduces

direct

human

oversight.

Without

corresponding

safeguards,

this

may

reduce

patient

visibility

into

secondary

data

uses,

increase

opaque

AI-mediated

access,

and

undermine

trust

in

digital

participation.

I encourage the Department to recognize cybersecurity, system stability,  
and

patient

safety

controls

as

legitimate

compliance

considerations;

preserve

meaningful

audit

mechanisms

accessible

to

both

providers

and

patients;

and

clarify

boundaries

between

appropriate

automated

exchange

and

large-scale

data

harvesting

inconsistent

with

the

intent

of

the

21st

Century

Cures

Act.

Modernization

should

strengthen

accountability,

not

dilute

it.

II. Infeasibility (§ 171.201) and Manner (§ 171.301) Exceptions

Providers require prospective clarity to comply conﬁdently with information  
blocking

rules.

If revising these exceptions, the Department should deﬁne “reasonable  
eﬀorts”

in

objective,

operational

terms;

explicitly

recognize

cybersecurity

threats,

system

degradation,

and

patient

safety

risks

as

valid

infeasibility

bases;

and

distinguish

provider

obligations

from

vendor-driven

contractual

constraints.

Absent clear benchmarks, subjective enforcement risk may incentivize  
defensive

practices

and

slow

appropriate

data

exchange—counter

to

statutory

intent.

Clarity

beneﬁts

both

patients

and

providers.

III. Decision Support Interventions (§ 170.315(b)(11))

The proposed removal or reduction of transparency and risk management  
elements

within

§

170.315(b)(11)

warrants

caution.

AI-enabled decision support tools increasingly inﬂuence triage, diagnosis,  
and

treatment.

Eliminating

transparency

expectations

may

limit

clinician

understanding

of

system

limitations,

reduce

patient

awareness

of

algorithmic

inﬂuence,

increase

undetected

bias

or

performance

degradation,

and

erode

trust

in

certiﬁed

health

IT.

Patients with rare conditions and historically underserved communities face  
disproportionate

risk

from

biased

or

data-sparse

models.

These

impacts

may

be

diﬃcult

to

monetize

but

directly

aﬀect

equity

and

fairness.

Rather than eliminate safeguards, the Department could require  
standardized,

machine-readable

model

documentation;

scale

transparency

requirements

based

on

clinical

risk;

and

maintain

expectations

for

real-world

performance

monitoring.

Streamlining

burden

should

not

mean

removing

foundational

governance

guardrails.

IV. Privacy, Security, and API-Based Exchange

Removing certain privacy and security certiﬁcation criteria increases  
variability

across

vendors

and

may

weaken

uniform

technical

expectations

in

API-enabled

environments.

As FHIR-based exchange expands, patients should retain clear visibility into  
who

accesses

their

data,

meaningful

and

accessible

audit

capabilities,

and

assurance

that

interoperability

does

not

dilute

baseline

security

standards.

Interoperability depends on trust. Trust depends on enforceable safeguards.

V. Accessibility and Distributional Impacts

Removing accessibility-related certiﬁcation requirements risks widening  
inequities

in

digital

participation.

Patients managing chronic illness or complex care rely heavily on digital  
tools.

Distributional

impacts

of

deregulation

will

not

be

evenly

borne.

Patients

with

rare

conditions

and

individuals

from

historically

underserved

communities

may

experience

disproportionate

harm

if

transparency,

security,

or

accessibility

protections

are

weakened.

These qualitative impacts—dignity, equity, and fairness—are core regulatory  
considerations,

even

when

diﬃcult

to

quantify

economically.

Modernization

of

standards

may

be

appropriate;

removal

without

replacement

is

not.

Conclusion

I support reducing duplication and aligning regulations with current  
technological

realities.

Automation,

APIs,

and

AI

are

present-day

infrastructure.

However, deregulation should not diminish foundational patient  
protections.

I respectfully urge the Department to preserve scaled transparency and risk  
management

requirements

within

§

170.315(b)(11);

provide

objective,

prospective

clarity

in

revisions

to

§§

171.201

and

171.301;

embed

strong

privacy

and

security

protections

within

updated

API-focused

certiﬁcation

criteria;

maintain

or

modernize

accessibility

baselines;

and

address

distributional

impacts

on

patients

with

complex

conditions

and

historically

underserved

communities.

Thoughtful modernization can advance interoperability while preserving  
transparency,

equity,

and

trust.

Respectfully,

Christine von Raesfeld  
Board Member, The Light Collective  
Rare Disease Patient and Advocate
