GENERAL COMMENTARY 
We appreciate ASTP’s continued efforts to advance thoughtful deregulatory actions 
that meaningfully reduce administrative and operational burden while preserving 
momentum toward the nation’s shared goals of interoperability, transparency, and 
patient access to electronic health information. These actions demonstrate a 
balanced approach that supports innovation and sustainability across the health IT 
ecosystem. 

  
We particularly appreciate ASTP’s updates to the definition of Base EHR, including 
the removal of criteria that are not broadly applicable to health IT developers 
operating across a diverse range of care settings. This refinement more accurately 
reflects the varied functional roles of health IT solutions and acknowledges the 
complexity of supporting interoperability in behavioral health, post-acute, and other 
non-traditional care settings. By aligning regulatory expectations with real-world 
use cases, ASTP enables developers to better focus resources on advancing 
standards-based data exchange, usability, and patient-centered access, supporting 
improved outcomes and a more connected continuum of care. 
We advise ASTP to consider the impact proposed certification changes will have on 
interoperability and patient safety, specifically as it relates to new entrants in the 
market. While established health IT developers will continue to support a majority 
of the criteria previously required by the ONC certification program,  Netsmart asks 
that ASTP consider whether the removal of criteria will create discrepancies in 
available technology and standards technology developers adhere to  if new market 
entrants are certified for the first time to only the remaining criteria.  
Netsmart appreciates ASTP’s coordination with CMS and SAMHSA and asks ASTP to 
prioritize alignment across federal programs such as Quality Reporting Programs, 
the Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS), and Certified Community 
Behavioral Health Clinics (CCBHCs), which is essential for both EHR developers and 
providers participating in Promoting Interoperability (PI) and MIPS. Consistency 
between ONC certification requirements and CMS payment programs supports 
clearer expectations, and enables more predictable roadmap designation and 
implementation across the health IT. 
Netsmart requests alignment in the code of federal regulations related to payment 
program requirements, and for ASTP to consider how those payment program 
requirements rely on specific certified capabilities. Continued alignment across 
federal regulation is critical as ONC certified functionality is modified or removed.  
We also encourage the timely finalization of the certification components of HTI-5 to 
help address the ongoing uncertainty currently impacting health IT development 
roadmaps, certification planning, and provider adoption decisions. Clear and 
finalized requirements are critical to enabling developers and providers to make 
informed investments and avoid delays that could impede progress toward 
interoperability and access goals. 
Additionally, we support the rapid release of updated certification criteria and 
Conditions and Maintenance of Certification requirements that ASTP/ONC has 
indicated will be included in the forthcoming HTI-6 proposed regulation. Providing 

  
early and detailed visibility into these expectations will help reduce disruption and 
allow for more efficient planning and implementation across the ecosystem. As new 
or updated certification requirements are introduced in the future, we urge 
ASTP/ONC to more closely evaluate implementation timelines to ensure they are 
realistic and achievable, particularly given the cumulative impact of overlapping 
regulatory initiatives. We recommend at a minimum, health IT developers should be 
allocated 24 months from the effective date of a regulation to the compliance date to 
allow sufficient lead time for software development and testing. 
We applaud many of the suggested changes and appreciate ASTP efforts to provide 
clarity on the information blocking regulation. However, the complexity and 
ambiguity ASTP’s proposals add to already complex information blocking policies 
remain a concern. Netsmart appreciates ASTP’s clarification of several definitions, 
however, we seek more clarity and guidance related to the accepted use, specifically 
as it relates to the information blocking exceptions. Actors under the information 
blocking regulation continue to look to ASTP to provide clarity and reduce the 
ambiguity related to the application of the regulation and permissible use of the 
outlined exceptions.

Netsmart supports the proposed removal of the “family health history” certification

health IT developer systems. We do not anticipate this criterion as something that is 
removed or no longer supported if eliminated from the ONC certification program.

§ 170.315(b)(1) Transitions of care  
We propose to reduce the scope of the criterion to focus on enabling the receipt of a 
Consolidated CDA (C-CDA) document as a way to position this criterion for a future 
evolution to receipt of FHIR-formatted Data. We propose to remove the requirements 
in § 170.315(b)(1)(i) “send and receive via edge protocol” and in § 170.315(b)(1)(iii) 
“create” and to simplify the requirements in § 170.315(b)(1)(ii) “validate and display” 
to focus on the ability to receive and validate C-CDA documents. 
Netsmart supports the proposed reduction of the “transitions of care” criterion. The 
testing tool utilized for this criterion is not user friendly, test use cases are not 
always updated to the most recent required standards and the tool itself crashes 
frequently. This creates additional complexity as it relates to health IT developers’ 
ability to test and certify to this criterion, increasing overall burden.  
Furthermore, while we recognize currently certified health IT developers will 
continue to support the ability to send C-CDAs as it remains the industry standard, 
we are concerned about the impact on providers if new entrants into the market are 
not required to develop the C-CDA functionality. While we recognize and appreciate 
the industry trend towards a FHIR-forward future, we acknowledge provider 
organizations take time to transition and utilize new technology. Rather than 

  
completely reduce the scope of (b)(1)(i) we recommend ASTP remove the testing 
requirement for health IT developers to reduce burden but maintain the criteria in 
the interim and phase it out once FHIR-enabled data exchange is more widely 
adopted. 
In (b)(1)(ii), we suggest removing the validation functionality from certification, as 
well as the section viewing and filtering, as clinicians and administrators rarely use 
this functionality.  
§ 170.315(b)(2) Clinical information reconciliation and incorporation (CIRI) 
Our review of industry adoption of the CIRI certification criterion indicates widespread 
adoption. Review also indicates that its capabilities are widely implemented and used 
in health IT at this time and thus not likely to go away as supported capabilities by 
developers of certified health IT solely on the basis of removal of the criterion from the 
Certification Program. 
Netsmart supports the proposal to remove CIRI as a certification criterion. As 
written, the requirement constrains innovation by prescribing a specific 
methodology rather than focusing on intended outcomes. This rigidity can 
discourage the development of more effective, scalable solutions that better support 
providers across diverse care settings. 
§ 170.315(b)(11) Decision support interventions 
We propose to revise the “decision support interventions” (DSI) certification criterion. 
For these and the aforementioned reasons, we propose to remove all requirements 
related to source attributes in § 170.315(b)(11)(iv), their access in § 
170.315(b)(11)(v)(A), and their modification in § 170.315(b)(11)(v)(B), as well as 
requirements to manage risks related to the development and deployment of 
predictive DSIs supplied by health IT developers as part of their product in § 
170.315(b)(11)(vi).  
We support the continued inclusion of source attributes for evidence-based DSI. We 
agree with ASTP that the current approach to triggering Predictive Decision Support 
Interventions (PDSI), especially the requirement to support all USCDI data elements 
or to base selection on a broad set of those elements, does not provide meaningful 
functionality. Additionally, in real-world implementations, the concept of “selection” 
has proven difficult to interpret and operationalize, resulting in inconsistent 
application across clinical settings. For these reasons, we support eliminating the 
selection component of the criterion entirely rather than continuing to refine a 
construct that has limited clinical utility. 

  
With respect to the proposed removal of the requirement to surface source 
attributes directly within health IT systems, we agree with ASTP that clinicians 
rarely access this information during point-of-care decision-making. This is 
particularly true for clinical AI, where decision support must be timely, focused, and 
minimally disruptive to workflow. Embedding detailed source attributes into 
clinical interfaces risks increasing cognitive load and alert fatigue without 
improving patient outcomes, especially when the information is not immediately 
actionable in the moment of care.  
Moreover, in clinical environments, evaluation of data sources, evidence quality, and 
model limitations is most appropriately conducted by governance bodies, such as 
clinical informatics, quality, safety, and compliance committees, during 
procurement, implementation, or ongoing oversight, rather than by individual 
clinicians during encounters. Requiring source attributes to be displayed or editable 
within clinical workflows therefore introduces development and usability burden 
while providing limited incremental safety benefit. Similarly, users rarely modify 
source attributes for PDSI, requiring health IT developers to support functionality 
that is not being used creates unnecessary complexity and risk of inconsistent or 
outdated documentation across deployments. 
Netsmart recommends that source attributes and Information Risk Management 
(IRM) practices for clinical AI instead be provided through publicly available model 
cards (or system cards) that are explicitly designed for clinical decision support use 
cases. In the clinical context, model cards can clearly document elements such as: 
the clinical purpose of the model; the patient populations and settings for which it 
was trained and validated; key input data dependencies; performance 
characteristics and limitations; contraindicated or out-of-scope uses; and known 
risks or failure modes relevant to patient safety. Model cards are already being used 
by developers of clinical predictive models, diagnostic support tools (including 
imaging and risk prediction), and other high-impact AI to communicate these details 
in a structured, accessible way. We believe this approach better aligns transparency 
requirements with how clinical AI is evaluated, governed, and trusted in practice, 
supporting responsible adoption without imposing burdens that could slow 
innovation or detract from safe, effective clinical care.

Other Criteria to Consider Removing 
Netsmart believes (b)(10) EHI Export should be considered for removal in favor of 
further investment in standards-based APIs. (b)(10) does not promote use of 
standards, and as a result, it has led to market frustration with the use of the 
functionality. In contrast, standards‑based APIs, particularly FHIR‑based 
approaches, support scalable, secure, and repeatable access to electronic health 
information, align more closely with modern interoperability expectations, and 
better enable patient access, data exchange, and innovation. Redirecting 
certification focus toward standards‑based APIs would reduce duplicative 
regulatory burden, improve consistency across implementations, and accelerate 
progress toward a more interoperable health IT ecosystem. 
USCDI, g(10) 
Netsmart recommends reviewing the number of criteria and the scope of data 
required by the USCDI progression that certified health IT must support. Different 
health IT, including specialty EHRs, need not support all data within USCDI, as it 
creates an unnecessary burden. We ask ASTP to enable health IT to be certified to 
the data they “manage” within the scope of USCDI. Managing could be defined as 
“supporting the ability for the pertinent data to be stored locally and for the 
system’s end-users to view and interact with the discrete USCDI data while 
documenting and managing their clinical, administrative, and/or financial 
workflows.” This approach would enable specialty EHRs to avoid development of 
support that their user community does not need. 
RWT Reporting/Insights  
We appreciate ASTP/ONC finalizing this deregulatory proposal in line with prior 
enforcement discretion. Developers find RWT's value unclear given its reporting

In terms of expectations of conducting real world testing, while ASTP has been clear 
that only (g)(10), (g)(31), (g)(32), (g)(33) results must be published, it is unclear if

burden is not actually reduced. We recommend real world testing only be required 
for the criterion within the narrowed scope of the condition of certification. 
Information Blocking Proposals  
Definition Updates 
We propose to explicitly codify that “access” means the ability or means necessary to 
make EHI available for exchange or use, including by automation technologies such as 
robotic process automation and autonomous artificial intelligence systems. Similarly, 
we propose to explicitly codify that “use” means the ability for EHI, once accessed or 
exchanged through whatever technological means, to be understood and acted upon, 
including, without limitation, by automation technologies such as autonomous 
artificial intelligence systems and robotic process automation. 
Netsmart recommends that ASTP align and update all three definitions, access, 
exchange, and use, so they are applied uniformly. When evaluating automation 
technologies in relation to the definitions of “access,” “use,” and “exchange,” we 
request that ASTP offer guidance on addressing automation solutions that function 
alongside or in the background, such as those performing data scraping, without 
direct integration into health IT systems. We ask ASTP to provide use case examples 
and guidance related to how Actors should respond in accordance with the 
information blocking regulation and exception utilization, while prioritizing privacy, 
and security.  
We are also considering to similarly revise the “exchange” definition in § 171.102. We 
propose to revise the “exchange” definition in addition to the proposed revisions to the 
“access” and “use” definitions, as an alternative proposal to only revising the “access” 
and “use” definitions. Under this alternative proposal, we propose that the meaning of 
“exchange” includes, without limitation, the ability for EHI to be transmitted, through 
use of automation technologies (e.g., robotic process automation, autonomous 
artificial intelligence systems) or otherwise, between and among different 
technologies, systems, platforms, or networks. We seek comments on these proposals, 

  
including perspectives on whether actors and those who interact with actors would 
find these explicit regulatory updates helpful. 
As noted above, we recommend updating all three definitions.  
Infeasibility – Manner Exception Exhausted 
“Based on comments received in response to the CMS-ASTP/ONC RFI, questions 
received through the ASTP/ONC Health IT Feedback and Inquiry Portal,73 and other 
stakeholder feedback, we are concerned that actors may be abusing the exception in 
various ways, including claiming to requestors that the manner exception exhausted 
condition applies when the actor has not, in fact, explored whether they could reach 
agreeable terms to fulfill a request in the manner requested, or has not attempted to 
fulfill the request in at least two other manners consistent with the § 171.301(b) 
alternative manner, or both. Additionally, we are concerned that certain terms and 
criteria of the condition (i.e., “same,” “substantial number” and “similarly situated”) 
are being misused to deny requestors access to existing APIs and other manners of 
access, exchange, and use that have been granted to other parties. To address these 
concerns, we now propose to modify the condition to eliminate the potential for misuse 
and abuse. Specifically, we propose (in § 171.204(a)(4)(ii)) to increase the minimum 
number of alternative manners required to be considered to have “exhausted” the 
Manner Exception from two to “all,” in line with the original (HTI-1 Proposed Rule) 
proposal for the condition.” (131) 
Regarding the infeasibility exception, the regulatory language states “If an actor 
does not fulfill a request for access, exchange, or use of electronic health information 
for any of the reasons provided in paragraph (a) of this section (uncontrollable 
events, segmentation, third party seeking modification use, manner exception 
exhausted, or infeasible under the circumstances), the actor must, within ten 
business days of receipt of the request, provide to the requestor in writing the 
reason(s) why the request is infeasible.  The 10-day window attached to the 
infeasibility exception is not adequate, as the exception process cannot realistically 
be completed in such a short timeframe. Netsmart requests the 10-day window 
should begin when the negotiations have been completed. While still a quick 
turnaround time, it would make it more practical to be able to meet the 
requirements of the Infeasibility Exception and the Manner Exception Exhausted 
sub-condition.  
Furthermore, ASTP/ONC appears to have overly prescriptive expectations 
regarding the roles of both the actor and the requester in suggesting and accepting 
alternatives throughout the negotiation process. In discussions concerning access, 
exchange, or use, there is typically a combination of the requester defining their use 

  
case and objectives and recommending standards they consider most suitable, while 
the actor describes the integration options available. ASTP/ONC should neither 
assume nor require that actors unilaterally determine specific alternatives, as this 
does not accurately represent how integration decisions are made in practice. With 
respect to the Manner Exception Exhausted sub-condition, ASTP should avoid 
establishing an expectation for actors to specify alternatives in a particular 
sequence when it may be more practical or efficient to provide a range of relevant 
options and allow the requester to contribute input based on their needs.  
“In § 171.204(a)(4)(iii), we propose three revisions. First, we proposed to replace 
“same” with “analogous” as it refers to the access, exchange, or use of EHI.” (133) We 
discussed in the HTI-1 Final Rule how “same” meant the same requested access, 
exchange, or use, including the “same” manners of EHI access, exchange, or use, as 
provided to others. We still intend to include the concept of “the same” access, 
exchange, or use of EHI in consideration of whether the provision is met, but we believe 
the more expansive term “analogous” is more appropriate.” 
Netsmart believes the term “analogous” is ambiguous and difficult to operationalize. 
“Analogous” permits variation and requires subjective judgment about which 
similarities matter and how much similarity is sufficient. “Same” establishes a 
clearer, objective equivalence standard. 
The ambiguity is illustrated in ONC’s example: “For example, this condition would 
not be available to an actor when a requestor requests API access with certain 
features, such as ‘write’ capabilities or filtering functionality, that the actor provides 
to other entities.” This example appears to treat categories such as “write” 
capabilities or “filtering functionality” as inherently comparable. They are not. Write 
APIs can differ materially in scope, permissions, transactional limits, audit controls, 
and security safeguards. Filtering functionality may vary significantly in logic, 
performance impact, and privacy protections. Under the ordinary meaning of 
“analogous”— “comparable in certain respects”—materially different 
implementations could still be deemed compliant if they share a high-level 
functional similarity. 
Because system architecture and design approaches vary widely, what is 
“analogous” in one environment may not be in another. This invites interpretive 
discretion, complicates compliance planning, and makes consistent OIG 
enforcement more difficult. 
To promote clarity, and applicability, we recommend retaining the prior term 
“same,” which provides a more objective and enforceable standard. 

  
“Second and third, we propose: changing the “substantial number” element to “any,” by 
which we mean at least one individual or entity; and removing consideration of 
whether such individual or entity is similarly situated to the requestor. The proposed 
revisions to § 171.204(a)(4)(iii) would explicitly exclude from application of the 
condition any situation where the actor offers at least one individual or entity the 
requested access, exchange, or use. These revisions would alleviate concerns that the 
terms “substantial” and “similarly situated” are being manipulated to deny access, 
exchange, or use.” (134) 
Netsmart argues that replacing the term with "any" and eliminating consideration of 
whether an individual or entity is similarly situated to the requestor introduces the 
same ambiguity previously discussed with the use of "analogous." When the manner 
exception exhausted sub-condition of the infeasibility exception was initially 
proposed, it targeted scenarios such as piloting new technologies or developing 
custom solutions without the guardrails needed for broad implementation.  
However, if completing a pilot or custom development allows others to claim their 
use case is “analogous” or the “same,” and development becomes mandatory 
whenever “any” actor provides similar technology, this aspect of the sub-condition 
loses its purpose. As a result, developers would effectively become a development 
shop, which could stifle or discourage innovation and appears to undermine the 
intent of the proposal.  
“In the alternative to our proposed revisions to the condition above, we propose to 
remove the § 171.204(a)(4) manner exception exhausted condition entirely if public 
comments we receive on this proposed rule indicate that our proposed revisions would 
not be sufficient to eliminate or substantially mitigate the misuse or abuse of the 
condition. We seek comments on this alternative approach.” (135) 
Netsmart recommends keeping the manner exception exhausted sub-condition of 
the infeasibility exception, as written, and focus on providing additional clarifying 
guidance. This condition allows actors to allocate reasonable resources towards 
interoperable, standards-based methods and prevents requestors from trying to 
force the use of non-standard or unscalable solutions simply by refusing to accept 
access, exchange, or use of EHI in any other way. However, we request that ASTP 
provide further guidance on how to apply the manner exception exhausted 
condition. 
As mentioned above, ASTP should clarify what "negotiation" entails, ideally with 
examples. More importantly, the timing for the “responding to requests” condition 
within the infeasibility exception needs adjustment. Specifically, for the manner 
exception exhausted condition, actors should be given at least ten business days 

  
from the conclusion of negotiations, not from the initial receipt of the request. It is 
unrealistic to identify, scope, negotiate the original manner requested, and then 
negotiate three alternative manners all within ten business days. We also ask ASTP 
to provide flexibility regarding the order of alternative manners provided to the 
requestor and allow for the negotiations process to be flexible in accordance with 
the parties involved. Health IT Developers receive numerous daily requests, often 
lacking complete information, and require sufficient time to scope and negotiate 
various ways of provision. Also, the speed of negotiation depends on both the actor's 
and requestor's participation. 
The timing under the responding to requests condition should be revised for several 
aspects of the infeasibility exception, including the manner exception exhausted 
condition. 
Manner Exception 
“Based on comments received in response to the CMS-ASTP/ONC RFI, questions 
received through the ASTP/ONC Health IT Feedback and Inquiry Portal,77 and other 
stakeholder feedback, we are concerned that some actors may be attempting to abuse 
the exception, and that all participants in the health information ecosystem would 
benefit from greater certainty that the manner requested condition does not apply to 
any contracts, agreements, or licenses that are for the purposes of accomplishing 
access, exchange, or use of EHI in the manner requested, but that: (1) are not at 
“market” rate (85 FR 25877); (2) are contracts of adhesion; or (3) contain 
unconscionable terms (as defined below).” (136) 
“First, we propose to revise § 171.301(a)(2) to add a new subparagraph (iii) to 
incorporate in regulation text and explicitly state that any contract or agreement 
related to the access, exchange, or use of EHI: (1) must be at market rate; (2) must not 
be a contract of adhesion; and (3) must not contain unconscionable terms.” (139) 
We agree with ASTP’s observation that the manner exception is complicated, though 
we do not agree that the areas ASTP attempts to clarify as “plain language” were 
clear to industry stakeholders in the past, we believe the vast majority of actors are 
operating in good faith to comply with the information blocking regulation, 
including the application of the exceptions.  We ask ASTP to provide additional 
clarity related to the implementation and adoption of the proposed definitions and 
their applicability, specific use cases would be appreciated. 
“We propose to define, in § 171.102, “market rate” using paragraph (1) of the 
definition of “fair market value” found in the Stark Law regulations at 42 CFR 411.351: 

  
“(1) General. The value in an arm’s-length transaction, consistent with the general 
market value of the subject transaction” (42 CFR 411.351).” (139) 
While we support and recognize the intent behind the proposed inclusion of the 
term “fair market value,” Netsmart does not recommend the adoption of the 
proposed definition of “fair market value.” If the goal of ASTP is to promote 
innovation, encourage interoperability and advance health information technology. 
requiring “fair market value” as defined by Stark, will grind negotiations to a halt as 
both actor and requestor determine what constitutes “fair market value” per the 
Stark Law. When actors subject to Stark law need to identify fair market value, they 
often engage a professional healthcare valuation firm--there is an entire industry 
that performs Stark fair market valuations. Pursuing such valuations for every 
possible product or module would be time-consuming and expensive.  
Additionally, in the manner requested condition, custom work may be contemplated 
for which it is not possible to have a market rate or in an arms-length transaction. 
Rather than the adoption of this terminology, we recommend ASTP retain the 
current language and outline use cases actors can utilize to guide their 
interpretation. 
“We propose to define, in § 171.102, “contract of adhesion” as a contract provided on a 
standardized form, or on a “take it or leave it basis” without a realistic opportunity to 
bargain where the desired product, services, access, use, or exchange cannot be 
provided except by acquiescing to the form contract.” (139) 
Netsmart does not support the definition of “contract of adhesion” as written. ASTP 
is proposing to define “contract of adhesion” as a contract provided on a 
standardized form, or on a “take it or leave it basis” without a realistic opportunity 
to bargain where the desired product, services, access, use, or exchange cannot be 
provided except by acquiescing to the form contract.” Contracts are by nature 
standardized forms or templates in their initial creation. During the negotiations 
process is when contracts are finetuned for the specific needs of the contractual 
parties. It is not feasible to create custom contracts in each instance and would not 
align with the turnaround times expected. Additionally, the precise amount of 
bargaining that is expected is unclear. We ask ASTP to reassess the proposed 
definition to ensure actors are able to follow accepted contracting processes but 
require both parties to reasonably engage in the negotiation and contracting 
process. 
“For example, should we instead, or in addition, include the definition of “general 
market value” at 42 CFR 411.351 – “with respect to the purchase of an asset, the price 
that an asset would bring on the date of acquisition of the asset as the result of bona 

  
fide bargaining between a well-informed buyer and seller that are not otherwise in a 
position to generate business for each other.”? “(140) 
This proposed definition shares the same limitations as previous ones. However, 
adopting a market value framework is preferable to broadly applying the fees and 
licensing exception because it allows parties at EHI to arrange customized 
exchanges under terms they find mutually acceptable.  
“Therefore, we also propose in the alternative to remove paragraph (a)(2) from the 
manner requested condition of the Manner Exception, and explicitly apply the 
conditions of both the Fees Exception (§ 171.302) and the Licensing Exception 
(§171.303) to any agreement an actor makes with a requestor related to fulfilling the 
request for access, exchange, or use of EHI in any manner requested.” (141) 
We understood the “manner requested” condition of the Manner Exception to 
permit flexibility in terms and fees for potentially custom or non-standard work, 
provided the arrangement is mutually agreeable. Where parties cannot reach 
agreement, the Manner Exception appropriately directs them toward standards-
based alternatives, subject to compliance with the other applicable exceptions. 
Based on this understanding, we oppose the proposal to “explicitly apply the 
conditions of both the Fees Exception (§ 171.302) and the Licensing Exception 
(§171.303) to any agreement an actor makes with a requestor related to fulfilling 
the request for access, exchange, or use of EHI in any manner requested” because it 
appears to discourage innovation. Requests for unique or custom exchanges of EHI 
frequently require tailored technical development and corresponding custom 
pricing arrangements. Flexibility in pricing and licensing is essential to allow parties 
to reach mutually agreeable terms that facilitate the requested manner of exchange.  
Requiring actors to default to the Fees and Licensing Exceptions in these 
circumstances effectively replaces negotiated, market-based arrangements with 
rigid regulatory constraints. This could hinder innovative solutions thereby limiting 
the ways in which EHI may be exchanged—an outcome that would be counter to 
ASTP’s and the Administration’s stated policy goals of promoting interoperability, 
competition, and innovation. 
Moreover, the proposal undermines the original incentive structure of the Manner 
Exception. As we understood it, the exception was designed to encourage the use of 
standards-based approaches by making them the practical alternative when custom 
negotiations fail. For example: A healthcare provider requests a custom integration 
from its EHR developer. The developer outlines the cost and timeframe for the 
custom build. The provider believes the cost is too high or the timeframe too long, 

  
and the parties cannot reach agreement on the requested custom manner. At that 
point, the Manner Exception contemplates pivoting to a standards-based 
alternative. Because those standards-based alternatives must comply with the Fees 
Exception, they are typically offered at more predictable and constrained pricing, 
making them more attractive to the provider. The developer, in turn, benefits from 
implementing a reusable, standards-based solution rather than a one-off custom 
integration. 
This framework creates a natural incentive: parties may negotiate freely for custom 
solutions, but when agreement cannot be reached, standards-based approaches 
become the economically and operationally efficient path forward. The proposed 
changes disrupt that balance by discouraging flexible negotiation at the outset and 
weakening the intended incentive to adopt standards-based interoperability 
solutions. 
For these reasons, we urge ASTP to preserve the flexibility embedded in the current 
Manner Exception. 
TEFCA Exception 
“We now propose to remove the TEFCA Manner Exception (§ 171.403) from 45 CFR 
part 171. Based on TEFCA’s continued implementation and maturation and public 
comments received, including those received in response to the CMS-ASTP/ONC 
Request for Information; Health Technology Ecosystem (90 FR 21034) (CMS-
ASTP/ONC RFI), the removal of the TEFCA Manner Exception (§ 171.403) is 
appropriate for multiple reasons.” (143) 
As a Qualified Health Information Network (QHIN), we support TEFCA and the 
participation in TEFCA/QHIN to advance interoperable data exchange. We are 
supportive of the TEFCA manner exception but have not found the TEFCA manner 
exception to be an incentive to join TEFCA to date. If ASTP is concerned about the 
inclusivity or misuse of the TEFCA manner exception, we ask ASTP to provide 
additional guidance for those participating in TEFCA in good faith.  
 CONCLUSION 
We appreciate ASTP’s continued efforts to advance thoughtful deregulatory actions 
that reduce administrative and operational burden while maintaining momentum 
toward national goals of interoperability, transparency, and patient access to 
electronic health information. We particularly support updates to the definition of 
Base EHR, including the removal of certification criteria that are not broadly 
applicable across diverse care settings. This refinement better reflects the varied 

  
functional roles of health IT solutions, including those supporting behavioral health, 
post‑acute, and other non‑traditional care settings, and enables developers to focus 
resources on advancing standards‑based exchange, usability, and patient‑centered 
access.  
We also appreciate ASTP’s coordination with CMS and SAMHSA and urge continued 
alignment across federal programs, including MIPS, QRPs, and CCBHCs, as 
consistency between ONC certification requirements and payment program 
dependencies is essential for predictable planning and implementation. In addition, 
we encourage the timely finalization of HTI‑5 certification provisions and early 
visibility into forthcoming HTI‑6 certification and Conditions and Maintenance of 
Certification requirements, with realistic implementation timelines.  
Finally, while we support many of the proposed information blocking updates and 
appreciate ASTP’s efforts to provide greater clarity, we ask for further guidance to 
support consistent, predictable compliance across actors.
