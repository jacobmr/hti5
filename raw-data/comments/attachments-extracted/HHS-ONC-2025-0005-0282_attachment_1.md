Invest for Progress is a collaborative of health tech investors with behavioral health portfolio
companies and other key innovation stakeholders seeking delivery and payment reform for
mental health and substance use treatment. As an investor collaborative, Invest for Progress
aligns the strategic deployment of capital, addresses barriers to scaling innovative models, and
supports the acceleration of outcomes-based financing to drive improved mental health and
measurable impact. Partnering with Action for Progress, this investor collaborative supports
development of regional testbeds with integrated data, tracking longitudinal outcomes that
inform financing approaches to ensure progress is both sustainable and accountable. Our goal is
to accelerate change by aligning capital and best-in-class solutions with payment model reform
to amplify what works for lasting community benefit.

Out of the 60 certification criteria for health IT currently in effect, ASTP is proposing removal of
34, and revisions for 7, including predictive decision support interventions and clinical quality
measure capabilities. ASTP also proposes changes to information blocking requirements. Our
comments highlight the following recommendations:
• Finalize Information Blocking updates, specifically:
o Finalize “Access” and “Use” Definitions as proposed to include automated
access and use, and
o Finalize the infeasibility updates to remove the third-party seeking modification
use condition.

Information blocking
Information blocking provisions are intended to promote health data interoperability and improve
access to electronic health information (EHI). Prior legislation has included measures designed to
encourage the use of health IT to facilitate convenient, secure access to and exchange of EHI
among patients, providers, and other stakeholders. Through subsequent rulemaking, ASTP has
refined and clarified the information blocking framework, including updates to the scope of
applicable exceptions, clarification of which entities are considered to be offering health IT, and
revisions intended to improve compliance and reduce regulatory ambiguity.

In addition, ASTP has observed—through its automation technology FAQs—that questions
surrounding automated tools underscore the need for clearer guidance regarding how providers
and health IT developers access and exchange data using technologies such as robotic process
automation and artificial intelligence. Together, these efforts reflect an ongoing commitment to
ensuring that the information blocking framework evolves alongside technological advancement
while maintaining its core goal of enabling secure, seamless data exchange.

“Access ” and “Use” Definitions  
ASTP proposes to finalize definitions of “access” and “use” to clarify that EHI data cannot be
used by providers until the data can be exchanged between entities. Given stakeholder
comments, ASTP has proposed definition updates to explicitly state that “access” includes
making EHI data available through automation technologies (such as robotic process automation
and artificial intelligence systems), and “use” to include the ability for EHI to be interpreted and
acted upon by technologies once the data has been exchanged.

Invest for Progress strongly supports ASTP’s proposed updates to clarify that “access”
includes making EHI available through automation technologies, such as robotic process
automation (RPA) and artificial intelligence (AI) systems, and that “use” includes the ability
for EHI to be interpreted and acted upon by technologies once exchanged. As a collective of

functions. By recognizing this functionality within the definition of “use,” ASTP reinforces the
principle that interoperability should facilitate real-world application—not just passive data
transfer.

These updates will foster innovation by reducing ambiguity and minimizing the risk that
developers face inconsistent interpretations of permissible data activities. Clear definitions
support responsible design, encourage investment in advanced analytics and automation
capabilities, and promote a competitive marketplace where innovative solutions can interoperate
securely and effectively. At the same time, these clarifications remain consistent with existing
privacy and security safeguards, ensuring that expanded technological use of EHI occurs within
established compliance frameworks. For these reasons, we strongly encourage ASTP to
finalize the proposed definitional updates as proposed.
Infeasibility Exception  
ASTP proposes to remove the third-party seeking modification use condition of the Infeasibility
exception, as originally finalized in the HTI-1 Final Rule. In this condition, organizations were
permitted to refuse a request to use EHI for modification purposes, and the refusal would not be
considered information blocking. Stakeholders have expressed concern for potential misuse of
the condition and would allow third parties to refuse EHI data requests.

Invest for Progress strongly supports and applauds ASTP’s proposed rule to remove the
third-party seeking modification use condition. We believe closing this loophole is an
important step toward ensuring that innovative third-party applications can support true
bidirectional exchange within the EHR environment. The ability not only to retrieve data, but also
to contribute relevant, structured information back into the record, is essential for care
coordination, referral management, quality measurement, and social care integration. By
preventing arbitrary or restrictive limitations on write-back capabilities, this policy promotes a
more open and interoperable ecosystem in which developers can design tools that integrate
seamlessly into clinical workflows.

This, in turn, enables providers to incorporate specialized applications—such as care
management, behavioral health, remote monitoring, or social needs platforms—without
fragmenting documentation or creating duplicative work. It also ensures that patients’ records
more fully reflect the services and interventions delivered across the care continuum.
Importantly, eliminating this use condition provides greater regulatory clarity and reduces
uncertainty for developers seeking to innovate responsibly within established interoperability
frameworks. This is particularly important for the serious mental illness population, particularly
those who rely on multiple public benefit programs such as Medicaid, housing assistance, and
social services. Public behavioral health providers and state or local agencies often operate
within complex, resource-constrained environments that depend on timely, automated exchange
of data across health and human services systems.

This change, as proposed, reinforces the principle that certified health IT should support secure,
standards-based exchange in both directions, consistent with broader HHS goals around data
liquidity and coordinated, whole-person care. For these reasons, we encourage ASTP to finalize
this provision as proposed.

Predictive Decision Support Interventions (§ 170.315(b)(11)(iv) and (v) )
ASTP is proposing to revise the decision support interventions (DSI) certification criterion by
removing the requirements regarding transparency and risk management, specifically source
attribute support, access, modification, and intervention risk management, for predictive DSIs.
ASTP rationale for this revision includes lack of ongoing evidence that clinicians are engaging with
source attribute information and that previous estimates of source attribute transparency
requirements would result in a significant cost savings. ASTP further notes that certified health IT
developers are subject to these requirements, but other health technology companies are not,
and ASTP lacks the authority to implement an HHS-wide policy.

Invest for Progress strongly urges ASTP to not finalize this provision as proposed and retain
existing transparency and risk management requirements. Under HTI-1, ASTP finalized
requirements that applied to a wide range of predictive tools, including AI-based tools and certain
types of software as a medical device (SaMD) also subject to FDA oversight. These requirements
went into effect January 1, 2025 and required predictive DSIs interfacing with health IT modules
to provide information on what data was used to train the DSI, how the predictive DSI should be
used, updated, and maintained, and how the predictive DSI performs using validity and fairness
metrics in testing and local data. ASTP notes that no additional evidence was published to
support previous cost-saving estimates, not that evidence has contracted original cost-savings
estimates. One year of implementation, especially given the timeline required to reach fully
functional implementation, is insufficient for appropriate data collection and analysis of the
impact and use of these regulatory requirements. To address ASTP concerns about lack of
evidence, Invest for Progress recommends ASTP maintain existing transparency and risk
management standards but begin meaningful, standardized data collection before removing
or implementing significant revisions to the predictive DSI requirements.

Additionally, while we are aware of the limitations of ASTP authority, Invest for Progress
remains highly concerned about the potential impact the proposed changes will have on
regulatory alignment across agencies. Removal of the transparency and risk management
requirements under ASTP’s purview could fragment federal oversight by shifting responsibility
across agencies without a clearly defined coordinating mechanism. Invest for Progress strongly
supports the alignment of ASTP requirements with FDA oversight to remove duplicative and
potentially conflicting regulatory obligations for developers. Greater harmonization across federal
agencies can promote efficiency, clarity, and innovation in the health IT ecosystem. However, we
remain deeply concerned that the proposed revisions to the predictive DSI framework—
particularly those that reduce or eliminate transparency and risk management
expectations—could unintentionally weaken important safeguards and hinder HHS-wide
coordination. Without clear expectations for documentation and disclosure, healthcare
providers and other end users may lack sufficient information to assess the reliability, bias risks,
and clinical appropriateness of AI-enabled tools. This could undermine trust, impair informed
decision-making, and create variability in how risk is evaluated and mitigated across care
settings.

For developers, this lack of standardized expectations can also create significant operational and
compliance challenges as discussed above. In the absence of uniform documentation and
transparency requirements, developers may be required to respond to inconsistent and evolving

Privacy and Security Certification Criteria (§ 170.315(d) )
Invest for Progress is highly concerned about the removal of Privacy and Security
certification criteria and urges ASTP to align certification criteria with HIPAA and other
existing criteria rather than remove completely. This proposal removes the requirement for
vendors to demonstrate Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA), Audit Logs, and Encryption during
certification. As discussed above, while we appreciate ASTP’s focus on removing burden on
developers, the removal of the privacy and security certification criteria would shift responsibility
to healthcare providers and other health IT acquirers to perform due diligence to confirm
continued alignment with HIPAA privacy and security safeguard requirements, embed specific
protections within contractual arrangements, and integrate business associate security
assessments into their HIPAA compliance oversight, as appropriate.

In practice, absent a consistent and recognized certification framework, developers may
face a patchwork of individualized security questionnaires, contractual requirements, and
audit demands from customers seeking assurance that products meet HIPAA standards.

Care Plan Certification Criterion (§ 170.315(b)(9) )
ASTP proposes to remove the care plan certification criterion, which supports documentation of
patient name, health concerns, goals, interventions, and health status evaluations and
outcomes. This criterion facilitates the creation and management of a patient-centered care plan
for both ambulatory and in-patient settings.
Invest for Progress appreciates ASTP’s continued efforts to streamline certification
requirements and reduce unnecessary burden on health IT developers. However, we
encourage ASTP to not finalize this provision as proposed. The care plan criterion ensures that
certified health IT can capture and organize core elements such as patient name, health
concerns, goals, planned interventions, and ongoing health status evaluations and outcomes.
These elements are foundational to meaningful patient engagement. They help ensure that care is
not solely problem-oriented, but goal-oriented—reflecting the patient’s priorities, preferences,
and evolving health needs. By structuring this information in a consistent and interoperable
manner, the criterion promotes shared understanding among patients, caregivers, and
multidisciplinary care teams across ambulatory and inpatient settings.
Removing this requirement could unintentionally diminish the visibility and consistency of
patient-centered planning within certified health IT. Without a defined capability to document and
manage care plans, providers may rely on fragmented or unstructured documentation practices,
which can make it more difficult for patients to access, understand, and participate in their own
care planning. This may be particularly impactful for individuals with chronic conditions or
complex care needs, for whom longitudinal goal tracking and coordinated interventions are
essential.

Additionally, the behavioral health field is increasingly moving toward integration with primary
care settings. Collaborative, team-based models that involve primary care providers,
behavioral health specialists, care managers, and community-based supports are
increasingly considered the gold standard of whole-person care. A structured, shared care
plan helps ensure alignment around treatment goals, therapeutic interventions, medication
management, and social service referrals. Again, this is particularly important for alignment
between payers and those who may be serving dually eligible populations. Without standardized
capabilities to document and update these elements in a consistent and interoperable format,
coordination across settings may become more fragmented, increasing the risk of duplicative
services, gaps in follow-up, or misaligned treatment approaches.
Moreover, the care plan criterion supports continuity of care during transitions between settings
by standardizing how goals, interventions, and outcomes are recorded and shared. In its absence,
variability in documentation practices may increase, potentially undermining coordination and
limiting the ability of patients and caregivers to remain active partners in care. Invest for Progress
finds this antithetical to the growing call for better interoperability and information sharing, which
is particularly important for advancing whole-person care that includes behavioral health
services. For these reasons, while we support efforts to modernize and rationalize
certification requirements, we encourage ASTP to retain the care plan certification
criterion—or, at minimum, preserve core functional expectations that ensure health IT
continues to meaningfully support patient engagement, shared decision-making, and
longitudinal care coordination. Additionally, ASTP should consider the adaptability of care plans
for elements that should be embedded into apps and non-desktop devices in order to ensure
ease of data collection for field-based care managers.
Clinical Quality Measures Certification Criteria (§ 170.315(c)(3) and § (4))  
ASTP is proposing to remove the Clinical Quality Measures-Report and –Filter criterion which
enables electronic transmission of CQM data for reporting purposes and to record, change, and
filter CQM data by specific patient characteristics (e.g., race, sex, provider, or location),
respectively.
Most urgently, Invest for Progress is concerned about the impact removal of the Filter
criterion will have on clinicians’ ability to efficiently and accurately meet reporting
requirements that are stratified by specific data elements, such as demographic
characteristics, diagnoses, interventions, or outcomes. The Filter capability plays an essential
role in enabling providers to sort, segment, and analyze clinical data in a structured manner that
supports both internal quality improvement and external reporting obligations. A standardized

criteria filtering logic also maintains comparability across systems and supports quality data

In the behavioral health field in particular, there is a growing emphasis on measurement-informed
care—an approach that relies on the systematic collection and analysis of standardized
assessment data to guide clinical decision-making and evaluate outcomes over time. As federal
and state policymakers increasingly prioritize data-driven accountability, standardized and
stratified quality reporting has become central to demonstrating program effectiveness,
identifying disparities, and advancing value-based care models. The ability to filter data by
relevant variables—such as age, diagnosis, treatment modality, payer type, or social risk
factors—is critical to producing accurate and actionable reports.
Eliminating the Filter criterion could create operational challenges for providers who depend on
certified health IT to generate stratified reports efficiently. Without a standardized filtering
capability, clinicians and administrators may be forced to rely on manual data extraction
processes, custom reporting workarounds, or costly third-party tools. At the same time, the
absence of a uniform criterion may fragment market expectations. Instead of aligning to a
consistent certification standard, developers may be required to respond to highly individualized
customer demands for stratified reporting capabilities. As discussed above, this can increase
sales friction, prolong procurement cycles, and shift product strategy toward bespoke reporting
features tailored to specific contracts rather than scalable, standardized solutions. Over time,
this dynamic may reduce predictability in product development and complicate long-term
investment planning. Smaller behavioral health providers, in particular, may lack the technical
resources to build bespoke reporting solutions, placing them at a disadvantage in meeting
evolving regulatory and payer requirements.
Moreover, stratified reporting is foundational to identifying and addressing disparities in access,
treatment engagement, and outcomes. As the healthcare system works to improve transparency
and outcomes, the ability to analyze and report on data by key demographic and clinical
characteristics is not merely a technical convenience—it is a policy imperative. Removing the
Filter criterion may inadvertently weaken the infrastructure that supports these broader HHS
goals. For these reasons, Invest for Progress strongly encourages ASTP to retain the Filter
certification criterion or, at minimum, ensure that certified health IT continues to include
robust and standardized data filtering capabilities that support stratified reporting,
measurement-informed care, and equitable quality improvement efforts across behavioral
health settings.
