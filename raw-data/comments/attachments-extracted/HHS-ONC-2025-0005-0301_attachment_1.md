Centauri Health Solutions is the largest independent provider of Direct Secure Messaging (DSM) services in the United 
States, supporting a nationwide network of more than 600,000 providers and health system entities. Through our 
DirectTrust‑accredited and ONC‑certified HISP, RA, and CA platforms  (Secure Exchange Solutions and MedAllies) , we 
provide the secure transport infrastructure that underpins transitions of care, care coordination, and clinical data exchange  
across critical access and rural hospitals, major health systems, ambulatory practices, post‑acute facilities, behavioral health, 
health plans, state agencies, and over 100 health information technology vendors, including dozens of ONC -HIT certified 
electronic health record technology (CEHRT)  vendors which rely on Centauri as the preferred HIPAA complian t method 
for secure data exchange.  DSM is the backbone of interoperable communication for a large portion  of the healthcare 
ecosystem, and its continued inclusion in the certification program is essential to maintaining a trusted, secure, and equitable 
national exchange environment. 
 
We are particularly concerned about the proposed removal of certification criteria related to the Direct Protocol, Edge 
Protocol, and XDR/XDM at 45 CFR §§170.315(h)(1) and (h)(2). Centauri supports secure electronic health information 
exchange across the entire healthcare continuum —including primary care, specialty providers, rural health and critical 
access hospitals, health systems, plans, laboratories, and other ecosystem participants.  
 
The HTI‑5 proposal to remove certification criteria at 45 CFR §§170.315(h)(1) and (h)(2) would have significant unintended 
consequences for interoperability, care coordination, and patient safety. While we support ONC’s goals of reducing burden 
and modernizing certification, eliminating the DSM conditions of certification (§§170.315(h)(1) and (h)(2)) from CEHRT 
requirements undermines those goals and weaken s the very infrastructure that enables nationwide secure, scalable. 
affordable, exchange today. 
 
Our nationwide infrastructure enables real‑time clinical event alerting and care coordination messages to flow directly into 
provider workflows within CEHRT systems which comply with the conditions of certification, including §§170.315(h)(1) 
and (h)(2). This reduces administrative friction, ensures that essential treatment, safety, and care‑gap notifications reach 
providers when needed, and strengthens coordination of care and care management efforts. These capabilities help reduce 
avoidable readmissions and improve overall patient outcomes. 
 
The HTI‑5 proposal seeks to remove redundant requirements, increase efficiency, reduce burden, and promote improved 
access, exchange, and use of electronic health information. However, eliminating the conditions of certification at 
§§170.315(h)(1) and (h)(2), the criteria that DSM solutions using the Applicability Statement for Secure Health Transport 
adopted by ONC at 45 CFR § 170.202 (the “Direct Protocol”) enable providers to meet , undermines these objectives. For 
the reasons outlined below, we believe that removing these certification requirements do not advance the goals articulated 
in the HTI‑5 proposed rule. 


 
1. ONC explicitly acknowledges Direct is widely adopted and widely relied on for transitions of care and clinical 
data exchange supporting care management . Direct revolutionized data exchange between unaffiliated parties 
and allowed healthcare to rely on a secure, inexpensive, reliable and effective means of point -to-point messaging.  
Removal from the certification program will re duce vendor  support and investment  and negatively impact the 
broader healthcare community. As ONC stated in July 2025, “Direct has come ‘standard’ in certified health IT for 
years and is widely available and frequently used.” 1 Recent data reinforces this: hospital adoption of Direct 
approaches 80%, 75% of hospitals regularly use DSM to deliver summaries of care or other care coordination 
messages, and 55% of hospitals receive clinical data for care summaries via DSM.2   
 
2. Direct Secure Messaging is not legacy plumbing , DSM is a minimum viable interoperability capability that 
supports ONC/ASTP’s access, exchange, and use objectives for care coordination, transitions of care, equitable 
exchange for under resourced providers, and safety net redundancy when the APIs are unavailable or inappropriate. 
 
3. No equivalent required functionality for secure transport and exchange of health data exists . Certified 
capabilities should not be removed from the program unless there is a functionally equivalent, universally adopted 
and secure replacement which does not exist today for the DSM use case.  Eliminating DSM, the backbone of data 
transmissions for much of healthcare, undermines trust and public confidence in ensuring that medical information 
is securely transmitted.  
 
4. A significant portion of the provider ecosystem still relies on Direct as their primary interoperable transport 
method. Direct is critical to downstream participants including smaller rural hospitals, critical access care, primary 
care, specialty, long term/skilled nursing, and behavioral health which rely on Direct and lack resources  to adopt 
other functionality.  ONC has an equity mandate, removing DSM would disproportionately harm providers least 
able to implement custom API workflows.  CMS mandates which include conditions of participation requiring 
electronic discharge notifications (42 CFR § 482.24) to be delivered by discharging hospitals to community 
physicians for affected patients, are met with the auditable assurance that discharge notifications delivered via DSM 
will be received by provider CEHRT systems which include the certification standards of §§170.315(h)(1) and 
(h)(2).  Moreover, use of DSM-delivered health event notifications improve care management by reducing 30-day 
readmission rates by allowing providers to connect with patients immediately post -discharge, cutting  readmission 
rates by nearly half (e.g., to 8.3% compared to a 17% national average).3  Similar outcomes have been long-evident 
among Medicaid populations where state agencies use DSM-enabled event notifications to reduce readmission rates 
and improve care management among vulnerable populations. 4  The integration of DSM -enabled delivery into 
provider workflows is a key driver of improved outcomes and care management, made possible by the requirement 
that CEHRT systems support integration with DSM solutions under §§170.315(h)(1) and (h)(2).  Absent that key 
integration, notification delivery becomes fragment ed, blocked by proprietary system moats created by CEHRT 
vendors seeking to expand their footprint rather than using standards-based methods of exchange to assure the best 
outcomes for patients.  
 
5. DSM uniquely supports multiple types of clinical data formats. DSM health data transport is file type agnostic, 
supporting exchange of unstructured and semi -structured clinical documents, large attachments (PDFs, images, 
scanned records), asynchronous clinical communications, cross organizational workflows without pre -established 
trust framework.  It enables legal, administrative, and clinical handoffs that are not transaction based and functions 
effectively across all care settings. 
 
6. FHIR is not enough. FHIR-based APIs  play an important has a role in supporting data access and interoperability 
in healthcare data exchange, they cannot independently meet all transport and workflow needs across the healthcare 
ecosystem. DSM remains the only certified (required), scalable, low cost, least burdensome method to exchange 
clinical information securely and ubiquitously across clinical summaries, referrals, attachments, and unstructured 
data essential for the coordination of care.  Mandatory certification criteria and guidelines are necessary in order to

attestations, and utilizing enforcement discretion to allow for lighter weight and more easily managed methods of 
demonstrating compliance with the specified conditions of certification, such as relying on alternative certifying authorities 
beyond the ONC-ACB program and including existing industry self-regulatory organizations such as DirectTrust, meet the 
HTI-5 goals of reducing regulatory burden while retaining the important requirements of assuring that an efficient, 
demonstrated, secure, successful method of clinical data exchange remains an integral part of healthcare policy  and 
interoperability requirements.
